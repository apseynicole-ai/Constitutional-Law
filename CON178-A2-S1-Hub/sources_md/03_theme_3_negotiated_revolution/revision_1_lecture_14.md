Parliamentary Sovereignty & Judicial Review

Core Idea: Parliamentary Sovereignty

• Parliament was the SUPREME law maker

• Could make or unmake any law

• No Higher law

• Courts could not strike down acts of parliament based on substance

Judicial Role (Very Limited)

• Judicial review is when a court reviews a decision by parliament, focusing on whether the

correct Procedure was followed

• During Apartheid, Courts could  declare Acts of Parliament invalid (limited to procedure)

• Cannot review law based on law

Parliamentary Sovereignty & Judicial
review (Apartheid Era)

Why this matters

• Enabled Apartheid legislation

• No effective legal check and balances on Parliament

Constitutional Crisis & Parliamentary
Supremacy (Sovereignty)

● The 1910 Constitution had two entrenched provisions

○ 1) Language
○ 2) Limited voting rights of for Black  South Africans
○ To amend an entrenched provision – must have 2/3rds majority in parliament

● 1951 – Parliament passed the Separate Representation of Voters Act

○ Purpose – Parliament attempts to remove Black and Coloured voters from the

common roll

○ Houses sitting separately, adopted by a simple majority (thinking they did not

have to follow the procedural rules)

Harris v Minister of the Interior [Harris I]

● Legal Question

○ Could parliament validly remove Black and Coloured voters from the common

role WITHOUT complying with the entrenched amendment procedure?

● Decision

○ Appellant Division said NO
○ Even under parliamentary sovereignty, Parliament must follow the manner

and form requirements laid down in the Constitution (Judicial Review)

● Key Principle

○ Parliament is sovereign, but when altering entrenched provisions, it must

comply with the prescribed procedure

○ The Act was declared invalid

Harris v Minister of the Interior [Harris 1]

● Why Harris I matters

○ Recognised that procedural limits on Parliament are

enforceable by courts

○ Confirmed the idea of “manner and form” limitations

○ Strengthened the role of courts in protecting the constitutional

structure

Minister of the Interior v Harris (Harris II]

● The government refused to accept defeat in Harris I

● It attempted to bypass the Decision of the Appellate Division by:

○ Passed the High Court of Parliament Act 35 of 1952

○ This new body

■ Composed of Members of Parliament

■ Had the power to “review and overturn decisions” of the Appellate

Division

■ Could declare court judgments invalid

○ Parliament tried to make itself the final court of appeal over

constitutional matters

Minister of the Interior v Harris (Harris II]

Why is this problematic?

● Under the 1910 Constitution, there was

○ Parliamentary Sovereignty

○ But also a separation between legislative and judicial functions

(SOP)

● Even under PS – parliament could not simply

○ Convert itself into a court

○ Set aside judicial decisions retroactively

● Violated core structural principles of the constitutional order

Minister of the Interior v Harris (Harris II]

● The Appellate Division declared the High Court of Parliament Act

invalid

Reasoning

1. Parliament cannot confer judicial power on itself in a way

inconsistent with the constitutional structure

2. Judicial power is vested in the courts

3. Parliament cannot overturn a court judgment by simply declaring

it wrong

● This was a major assertion of judicial independence

Collins v Minister of the Interior

● Another attempt to ensure racial disenfranchisement

● Parliament

○ Expanded the senate to obtain the required majority

○ Passed the amendment to the Act through a joint sitting

● This case challenged the validity of the change to the Senate. The

Argument was such that parliament manipulated its own composition to

achieve the constitutional majority

Collins v Minister of the Interior

● The Appellate Division upheld the validity of the Senate Act and

constitutional amendments

Reasoning:

1. Parliament had complied with the formal “manner and form”

requirements

2. The Court would not inquire into Parliament's political motives

3. Once the proper procedure was followed, the amendment to the Senate

was valid

● If the formal constitutional procedure is satisfied, the courts will not

question how Parliament secured that majority

Collins v Minister of the Interior

● What this tells us

○ “courts were subservient and subordinate to Parliament. The

Court could only test the manner and form in which legislation

was promulgated. This means that only formal requirements

could be tested. The content or Substance of legislation could

not be tested. The Court could not use the fact that certain

guaranteed rights were violated as. a ground for its decision

(Kleyn et al )

Summary

● Harris  I = Parliament must follow the rules of the

Constitution

● Harris II – High Court of Parliament – Parliament cannot

turn itself into a court …  (SOP considerations)

● Collins = If Parliament follows the rules, courts cannot

question what it does

○ The Constitution is the final say!

■ Substantive review ( Judiciable BOR)

Two stage Transition Solution

The negotiators  (CODESA) devised a two-stage transition

solution:

• Stage 1: Unelected negotiating parties draft and adopt the IC; an

interim government and the IC would govern the country after

the first election

• Stage 2: A democratically elected Constitutional Assembly

drafts the Final Constitution after the election.
