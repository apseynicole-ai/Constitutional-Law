Election results = Representation in the
Constitutional Assembly
Election Results:
○ ANC 63.66% of the vote / 252 seats (not quite 2/3rds)
○ NP: 20.39% / 82 seats
○ FP: 10.54% / 43 seats
○ Freedom Front: 2.17% / 9 seats
○ Democratic Party: 1.73% / 7 seats
○ PAC: 1.25% / 5 seats
○ ACDP: 0.45% / 2 seats

Election results = representation in the
Constitutional Assembly
● The election results directly affected the dynamics of the
constitutional assembly and thus the drafting process
● No one party had enough votes to draft the constitution on their own
● IE the ANC did not get the 2/3rds majority needed to pass the final
constitution on its own
● The ANC had to seek consensus with the other parties in the GNU,
particularly the NP which had significant implications for what ended up
in the 1996 Constitution

Constitutional Assembly and First Draft of
FC
● An enormous Public Participation Process was launched – this was to
aid in popular acceptance of the final document
○ If the people felt they had a hand in shaping the FC, they would feel
a greater connection to it
● First draft of the FC submitted to the Constitutional Court - 10 May 1996
○ This draft was not certified by the CC.
○ The CC felt several provisions did not align with the constitutional
Principles

The First Certification Judgment 1996 4 SA
744 (CC)
● Once the final draft of the FC was submitted to the CC for certification, all
political parties other than the ANC and PAC lodged objections to various
provisions within the New Text (NT)
● This was the result of a document which emerged via negotiations = not
everyone was happy with the final outcomes
● The court determined that the Constitutional Principles had to be interpreted
holistically and in a manner that was not technically ridge to ensure all citizens
could exercise their fundamental rights and freedoms

Summary of the First Certification Judgement
● The IC stated that the CC would be BOUND by its own judgment.
○ It was final, nothing more could be said on the matter
● The various submissions and objections were heard over a two-day hearing
● The Court's judgment was divided into eight chapters, each dealing with a
particular main topic under various subheadings.
● Having sketched the background and context of the certification exercise
(para 1– 46), the judgment explains the Court's approach to its task and
then deals with each identified issue bearing on the question of certification.

The First Certification Judgment
● The CC evaluated the text in two distinct ways:
1) Does the basic structure and premises of the text accord with
that as envisioned by the Constitutional Principles
2) Does the contents of the provisions align with the
Constitutional Principles
● If it was determined that the basic structure did not align, the CC would
not even bother with the substantive enquiry
● The structure did align, as such the judgment deals mostly with
substantive objections

The First Certification Judgment
Some objections
1) Objection in relation to inclusion of socio-economic rights within the Justiciable
BOR
○ Para 76-78
2) Objection in relation to no complete separation of powers between executive
and legislature
○ Para 106-113
3) General None Compliance with Various Principles: Reasons why the court found
certain provisions of the Constitutional text not complying with the Constitutional
principles
○ Para 149-165

Objections: Inclusion of socio-economic rights
● Socio Economic Rights are inter alia access to:
○ Housing (s 26); Health care, food, water, social security (s 27); Basic education (s
29)
● Additional protections for children (s 28)
Objection 1: Not “universally accepted” rights
● Argument made: These rights should not be included in a Justiciable Bill of Rights
○ Court rejected this argument
○ CP II allows inclusion of additional rights beyond universally accepted ones
● The Constitution is not limited to “traditional” civil and political rights
● It embraces a broader, transformative vision of rights protection

Objections: Inclusion of socio-economic rights
Objection 2: Violation of separation of powers
● Concern: Courts will interfere with budgeting and policy that is
in the purview of executive
● Court’s response:
○ Courts already make orders with budgetary implications
(e.g. legal aid, equality remedies)
○ Socio-economic rights are not fundamentally different

Objection: Inclusion of socio-economic rights
Objection 3: Such rights are not justiciable
● Argument: Courts cannot enforce rights tied to resources
○ Court’s rejected this
○ These rights are at least partially justiciable
○ At minimum:
■ Can be negatively enforced (state may not
unjustifiably interfere)

Objection: Inclusion of socio-economic rights
Key takeaway:
● Socio-economic rights are constitutionally valid and
enforceable
● Their inclusion does not breach separation of powers
● Courts play a legitimate role in their protection
● In line with the Constitutional Principles

Objection: SOP- Legislature and Executive
● Implicated Constitutional Principle CI (CP VI):
○ Requires: Separation of legislature, executive, judiciary
■ With checks and balances ensuring accountability
Objection 1 :
● NT allows members of the executive to also sit in the legislature
● Concern:
○ Weakens separation of powers
○ Enhances executive dominance
○ Undermines democratic accountability

Objection: SOP- Legislature and Executive
Comparative argument raised:
● Systems like:
→
○ USA, France, Netherlands stricter separation / less flexible
● Claim: SA model is deficient
● Court’s starting point:
○ No issue with separation involving the judiciary
○ Dispute focuses ONLY on executive–legislature overlap
● Key takeaway:
● The challenge is not about whether separation exists, but how strict it must be

Objection: Other Constitutional Principles
Court’s Response: Flexible Separation + Accountability
1. No single model of separation of powers
● No universal standard
● Systems differ (e.g. Germany allows overlap)
● Separation is always partial, not absolute
2. Core idea: balance between
● Functional independence AND checks and balances
● “Branches are partly interacting, not wholly disjointed”

Objection: SOP- Legislature and Executive
3. South African model: deliberate overlap
● Executive drawn from legislature
● Enhances accountability, not undermines it
Key accountability mechanisms:
● Cabinet accountable to Parliament (s 92(2))
● Duty to report to Parliament (s 92(3))
● Parliament can remove President (s 89)

Objection: SOP- Legislature and Executive
4. Constitutional design
● Powers formally separated:
○ Legislature (s 43); Executive (ss 85, 125); Judiciary (s 165)
Final finding:
● CP VI does NOT require strict separation
● NT model is constitutionally compliant
Key takeaway:
● South Africa adopts a hybrid model:
○ Partial separation
○ Strong accountability through overlap

Other Objections: Immunising Legislation from
Constitutional Scrutiny
Key provisions in the NT
● NT 241 (1) LRA remains valid despite the Constitution
Core problem
● This provision (among others) attempt to shield ordinary legislation from
constitutional review
● All laws must be consistent with the Constitution and subject to judicial review
○ CP IV – The Constitution is the Supreme Law
● Such protections would It undermine constitutional supremacy
● It allows Parliament to insulate laws from rights-based review
● It could lead to widespread abuse (any law could be “protected”)

Other Objections: Amending the Consitution
CP XV Requires
● Special majority's and special procedures
● Purpose: to protect constitution from inter alia rapid or opportunistic amendment
NT 74 provided inter alia
● Supermajorities
○ 2/3rd NA (for general amendments)
○ 75% (for foundational provision amendments)
NT fall short of CP requirement
○ Did not provide for “distinct special procedures”
○ Amendments could follow ordinary legislative processes

Other Objections: Amending the Constitution
Courts reasoning:
● “Special procedures” must mean more then just higher voting thresholds
● Additional deliberative and procedural safe guards
Why
● The Constitution is the foundation of legal order
● It should be harder to amend than ordinary legislation
Key takeaway
● Constitutional amendments require numerical and procedural entrenchment

Other Objections: Entrenchment of BOR
CP II:
● Rights must be entrenched AND justiciable
Objection
● Bill of Rights not more protected in the NT than other constitutional provisions
Court Stated:
• Entrenchment requires a higher level of protection than ordinary constitutional
provisions
● NT only requires a 2/3 majority in NA
● BOR not sufficiently entrenched

Early Responses to Apartheid

Defiance Campaign
Volunteer pledge:
•
I, the undersigned, Volunteer of the National Volunteer Corps, do hereby solemnly
pledge and bind myself to serve my country and my people in accordance with the
directives of the National Volunteer Corps and to participate fully and without
reservations to the best of my ability in the Campaign for the Defiance of Unjust
Laws. I shall obey the orders of my leader under whom I shall be placed and strictly
abide by the rules and regulations of the National Volunteer Corps framed from time
to time. It shall be my duty to keep myself physically, mentally and morally fit."

Defiance Campaign
• The first group of volunteers included Nelson Mandela, Yusuf
Dadoo, Moses Kontane, and Walter Sisusulu among others
• Their goal was to defy apartheid laws in JHB and other major cities
• The goal was to be arrested, to show the unjustness of the laws,
and to ultimately change/ abolish apartheid laws
• The campaign was unsuccessful in changing the laws but was
successful in awareness building

Defiance Campaign
• Resulted in a United Nations Commission to investigate the
unjust racial policies (international recognition)
• The period marked the beginning of non-racial cooperation in
the resistance to apartheid which was cemented by the
Congress Alliance in 1954, in the run-up to the Congress of
the people

Congress Alliance
• Coalition of anti-apartheid organisations formed in the early 1950s to
coordinate resistance against apartheid laws
• Included:
• African National Congress
• South African Coloured Peoples Organization
• Congress of Democrats
• South African Congress of Trade Unions
• Among others

Congress Alliance
• It was explicitly NON-racial at a time when apartheid
enforced racial division
• It developed a shared constitutional vision of equality
and citizenship.
• Organised the Congress of the people 1955

Congress of the People
• Held in Kliptown (Near JHB) on 26 June
1955
• 3000 delegates from across South Africa
Gathered
• Mass democratic process
• Started with a campaign asking
ordinary people what kind of South
Africa do you want
• Adopted the Freedom Charter

Freedom Chater
• Declared “South Africa belongs to
all who live in it”
• Envisioned equality, shared
citizenship, and socio-economic
rights
• Became the normative precursor
to the 1996 Final Constitution

Freedom Chater
• Key Principles
• Universal suffrage and democratic government
• Equality before the law
• Human rights and freedoms, including speech,
assembly and movement
• Redistribution of land and economic opportunity
• Access to housing, education and work
• Although not legally binding, Freedom Charter
became a foundational ideological document for the
liberation movement

Road to Sharpeville (1959 – March 1960)
• Anti-Pass campaign
• 6 Dec 1959 – ANC conference – Chief Albert
Luthuli announces 1960 as the “ Year of the Pass”
• 16 March 1960 – Robert Sobukwe and the PAC
announce that on 21 March 1960 there will be a
mass pass law protest
• PAC had recently split from the ANC

Road to Sharpeville (1959 – March 1960)
• 18 March 1960: PAC activists distribute circulars
calling on black South Africans to:
• Leave pass books at home
• Voluntarily present themselves at police
stations for arrest
• The aim was to overwhelm the apartheid system and
challenge pass laws directly

Sharpeville Massacre
• 21 March 1960: Thousands march peacefully to the
Sharpeville police station without their passbooks
• Police responded by opening fire on the crowed
• 69 people where killed
• Hundreds injured
• Witnesses later testified that police fired into an unarmed
crowd, and later when and placed weapons in the hands of
the dead

Sharpeville: Government Response
• 23 March 1960: Prime Minister Verwoed tells Parliament that
the protests were not about pass laws and praisesd thr
police response
• 23 March: Robert Sobukwe and PAC leaders are arrested
• Protests spread across the country in response
• 24 March: gatherings of 12 or more people are banned and
police surveillance increases

Sharpeville: Government Response
• 30 March 1960: Government declares a state of emergency
• Used to suppress protests
• Resulted in the mass detention of thousands of people
• Resulted in the ANC and PAC being banned (Unlawful
Organisations Act )
• Lasted several months and marked a shift to the more
violent, repressive phase of apartheid
• Historical turning point and led to international criticism

1961
Constitution

1961 Constitution
• 5 October 1960: Referendum in which white-only voters
approve the move to become a Republic, separate from
Britain.
• The British Monarch would be removed as head of state
• South Africa wanted to be part of the Commonwealth but
due to the recent political violence was not accepted by
international community

1961 Constitution
• 32 May 1961 SA became a Republic and left the
commonwealth
• Retained the parliamentary system from the 1910
constitution
• This constitution reinforced white minority dominance, and
continued to exclude black South Africans from the political
process

1961 Constitution
• Most important change was that the Monarch would no
longer be the head of state, this would not vest with the
President, as elected by the people.
• First president was C R Swart

Rivonia Trial
• Suppression of political dissent
• Suppression of Communism Act (1950),
• Sabotage Act (1962),
• Carried the death penalty
• 90-Day Detention Law (1963)
• Allowed indefinite solitary confinement without
charge, access to lawyers, or contact with family.

Rivonia Trial
• Held from 1963-1964
• Landmark legal proceeding that resulted n life
imprisonment of Nelson Mandela and other anti-
apartheid leaders
• Another historical turning point, which resulted in
international scrutiny on apartheid regime

Rivonia Trial
• Trialists charged under laws specifically designed to
criminalize resistance to racial oppression
• Demonstrates how a legal system can be weaponised by those
in power against the very people it should protect
• Makes a case for why constitutions must entrench rights
that constrain legislative power, not just procedural rules

Rivonia Trial
• Rather than mount a purely criminal defence, the accused chose to put
apartheid itself on trial.
• Mandela's famous "I am prepared to die" statement from the dock reframed
the proceedings as a question of political legitimacy:
• can a government that excludes the majority from political
participation claim the right to prosecute them for defying its laws?
• This connects directly to social contract theory and the question of what
gives law its moral authority.

Rivonia Trial
• In short, the Rivonia Trial is a foundational case study in the difference
between law and justice, the role of courts in authoritarian systems, why
entrenched constitutional rights matter, and how the legacy of historical
abuse shapes constitutional design.
• It was a political win for the fight against apartheid, but with many of the
leaders now in jail, the resistance saw setbacks and stagnation for several
years following, in which time the oppression of the government continued
and escalated .

1983 Constitution

1983 Constitution
● Republic of South Africa Constitution Act No 110 of 1983
● Key legislative reform under Apartheid Regime – to entrench apartheid
rule and response to internal protest and international pressure
● Aimed to incorporate Coloured and Indian groups, to create a broader
base of support for the white minority and fracture the non-racial dissent
● Direct response to ever increasing urban protest and unrest, needed to
ensure white control was maintained
● Referendum November 2, 1983, with a 66.3% (whites only) yes vote for
change

1983 Constitution
● Created the Tricameral Parliament: Parliament was restructured into
three separate houses
○ House of Assembly: For the white population group
○ House of Representatives: For the Coloured population group
○ House of Delegates: For the Indian population group
● Total exclusion of Majority of Black African population – complete
exclusion from any representation in the central government
● Created a hierarchy among the non-white race groups (similar to the
caste system in India?)

1983 Constitution
● Competencies were categorised as “own” or “general” affairs
○ Own Affairs – handled by each separate house
○ General affairs – handled by full parliament
● The Office of the Prime Minister was abolished
○ Symbolic State President replaced with executive State president,
created consolidating power into an office not directly elected by
the public but by the electoral college dominated by white ruling
party
● Executive authority rested with the cabinet
