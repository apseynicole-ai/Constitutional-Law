# Theme 4 Overview: The Theory of the South African Constitution[[1]](#footnote-1)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Theme 4**  Theme 4 studies the basic principles, concepts and doctrines of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996. It asks how the Constitution structures power, limits power, transforms society, protects rights, embeds democracy and makes public power accountable.  Authority: Theme 4 lecture roadmap; Constitution ss 1, 2, 7, 8, 36, 39, 42, 55, 59, 72, 118, 165, 167, 172, 181-182. |

The central idea of this theme is that the 1996 Constitution is not merely a technical legal document. It is a supreme, value-based, democratic and transformative constitutional framework. It was created against the background of colonialism, apartheid, parliamentary sovereignty and the abuse of law as an instrument of oppression. Its doctrines are designed to ensure that public power must be exercised lawfully, rationally, accountably and in a manner consistent with human dignity, equality and freedom.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17 identifies the roadmap: constitutionalism, constitutional supremacy, transformative constitutionalism, foundational values, interpretation and limitation of rights, horizontal application, rule of law, democracy and separation of powers. Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 1, 2, 7, 8, 36, 39. |

* Explain different conceptions of democracy in the Constitution.
* Explain separation of powers and its South African model, including checks and balances and institutional overlap.

## The Constitution, 1996: basic facts

* Formally enacted as the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 108 of 1996.
* Now referred to as the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996.
* Drafted by the Constitutional Assembly.
* Finally certified by the Constitutional Court in the Second Certification Judgment.
* Took effect on 4 February 1997, replacing the 1993 Interim Constitution.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slides on "The Constitution, 1996". Constitutional authority: Certification process under the Interim Constitution and the Constitutional Court certification judgments. |

## Important aspects of the 1996 Constitution

* A justiciable Bill of Rights containing basic rights for everyone.
* Universal adult suffrage.
* Constitutional supremacy and the rule of law.
* Non-racialism and non-sexism.
* Co-operative government across national, provincial and local spheres.
* Separation of legislative, executive and judicial authority.
* Chapter 9 institutions supporting constitutional democracy, including the Public Protector and Auditor-General.
* Recognition of traditional leaders.
* Recognition of languages.

| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slide "Important aspects of the 1996 Constitution". Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 1, 2, 6, 7-39, 40, 43, 85, 165, 181-194, ch 12. |

## How to understand the 1996 Constitution

The lecture gives a two-part method: first, understand the historical context in which the Constitution originates and operates; second, understand the fundamental doctrines, concepts and principles contained in the Constitution. This is important because South African constitutional law is not abstract. It is explicitly shaped by historical injustice and aimed at building a different future.

# Constitutionalism[[2]](#footnote-2)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Constitutionalism**  Constitutionalism is the philosophy and doctrine that governmental authority is derived from, and limited by, a body of fundamental law, usually a written constitution. It ensures that rulers act according to binding boundaries and not according to their own desires.  Authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17; De Vos 2.2; Currie and De Waal pp 10-21. |

The simplest exam definition is: constitutionalism means that government is created by law, empowered by law and limited by law. In South Africa this law is the Constitution. It does not merely describe the state. It sets out the moral, legal and institutional terms on which public power may be exercised.

The lecture emphasises that constitutionalism requires a constitution to do two things at the same time: it must STRUCTURE power and CONSTRAIN power. A state needs enough power to govern effectively, but that power must be limited and dispersed to prevent abuse.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 1(c) supremacy of the Constitution and rule of law; s 2 supremacy clause; ch 4 legislature; ch 5 executive; ch 8 judiciary; ch 9 institutions; ss 7-39 Bill of Rights. |

## Structure and constrain: the two halves of constitutionalism

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Function** | **Meaning** | **Example in the Constitution** |
| Structure power | The Constitution creates institutions and allocates authority so the state can govern. | Parliament under ch 4; executive under ch 5; courts under ch 8; Chapter 9 institutions under ch 9. |
| Constrain power | The Constitution limits public power so that it is not abused. | Supremacy clause s 2; Bill of Rights; rule of law; judicial review; separation of powers; legality. |

## Characteristics of constitutionalism

1. It identifies and establishes institutions and structures of government.
2. It formally and legally distributes power to these institutions and structures through a written Constitution.
3. It describes the nature and basis of the relationship between institutions and structures.
4. It provides for the creation of binding rules and laws for institutions and the people they govern.
5. It places limits on the exercise of public or state power.
6. It structures the state while also awarding and constraining power.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slides "Characteristics of Constitutionalism". Textbook authority: Currie and De Waal describe constitutionalism as requiring both sufficient governmental power and limits to prevent oppression. |

## Descriptive constitutionalism

A descriptive understanding of constitutionalism is a factual account of governmental structures and institutions. It explains how institutions work, interact, share power and distribute power. It does not evaluate the system against constitutional values. It focuses on form rather than substance.

|  |
| --- |
| **KEY CONCEPT**  Colonial and apartheid South Africa could have constitutional structures in a descriptive sense, but that did not mean the system was normatively constitutionalist in the democratic sense. A state can have a constitution and still fail constitutionalism if the constitution does not limit power and protect rights. |

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slide "Descriptive understanding of Constitutionalism". |

## Prescriptive constitutionalism

A prescriptive understanding of constitutionalism evaluates the constitutional system according to norms and values. It asks whether the system adheres to basic doctrines and principles such as constitutional supremacy, limited government, the rule of law, democracy, human rights, separation of powers, judicial independence and transformation.

|  |
| --- |
| **KEY ASPECTS OF PRESCRIPTIVE CONSTITUTIONALISM**  Constitutional supremacy; limited government; rule of law; separation of powers; democratic self-government; protection of human rights; independent judiciary; transformative constitutionalism. |

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slide "Key aspects of Prescriptive Constitutionalism". Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 1, 2, 7, 8, 34, 38, 39, 165, 172. |

## Why South Africa adopts prescriptive constitutionalism

Democratic South Africa adopts a prescriptive, value-based and transformative form of constitutionalism because the old system showed that law can be used oppressively when it lacks entrenched values, rights, institutional accountability and judicial review. The 1996 Constitution therefore does not merely organise institutions. It creates a value-based constitutional democracy.

|  |
| --- |
| **CRITICAL EMPHASIS**  Exam point: Do not say "South Africa has constitutionalism because it has a Constitution". The stronger answer is that South Africa has constitutionalism because the Constitution is supreme, rights-based, justiciable, value-based, transformative, democratic and enforced through judicial review and institutional accountability. |

## Constitutionalism and democracy

Constitutionalism does not reject democracy. It shapes democracy. The Constitution creates a democratic state but also prevents democratic majorities from destroying rights, equality, dignity and freedom. This is why constitutional democracy has a counter-majoritarian element: courts may invalidate even popular laws if they violate the Constitution.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 1(d) democracy, universal adult suffrage, common voters roll, regular elections and multi-party democratic government to ensure accountability, responsiveness and openness; s 2 constitutional supremacy. |

# Constitutional Supremacy[[3]](#footnote-3)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Constitutional supremacy**  Constitutional supremacy means the Constitution is the highest law of the Republic. Any law or conduct inconsistent with it is invalid, and the obligations imposed by it must be fulfilled.  Authority: Constitution s 2; s 1(c); Theme 4 lecture 15-17. |

The lecture identifies constitutional supremacy as the most definitive feature of South Africa's post-apartheid constitutional system. It is the foundation of the democratic constitutional dispensation. It is both a founding value and a binding enforceable rule: section 1(c) identifies the supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law as a founding value, while section 2 gives the supremacy clause legal force.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 1(c) and s 2. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, slides "Constitutional Supremacy". |

## Section 1(c) and section 2

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Provision** | **What it does** | **Exam use** |
| Section 1(c) | Lists supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law as founding values of the Republic. | Use to show supremacy is foundational and value-based. |
| Section 2 | States the Constitution is supreme law; inconsistent law or conduct is invalid; constitutional obligations must be fulfilled. | Use to show supremacy is binding and enforceable. |

## Supremacy makes the Constitution justiciable

Constitutional supremacy renders the Constitution justiciable. This means legal rules and exercises of power can be tested against the Constitution in court. If law or conduct fails to comply with constitutional provisions, it must be declared unconstitutional and invalid. Judicial review is therefore an important practical consequence of constitutional supremacy.[[4]](#footnote-4)

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 2, 38, 167, 172. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17 slide "Constitutional supremacy renders the constitution justiciable". |

## Judicial review

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Judicial review**  Judicial review is the power of courts to test law or conduct against the Constitution and to declare law or conduct invalid to the extent of its constitutional inconsistency.  Authority: Constitution ss 167 and 172; Theme 4 lecture 15-17; Currie and De Waal pp 10-21. |

Judicial review is one of the core mechanisms through which constitutional supremacy becomes real. Without review, the Constitution could be treated as aspirational. With review, unconstitutional law and conduct can be challenged and set aside.

## Parliamentary sovereignty vs constitutional supremacy

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Issue** | **Parliamentary sovereignty** | **Constitutional supremacy** |
| Highest authority | Parliament is supreme. | The Constitution is supreme. |
| Power of courts | Courts generally cannot invalidate Acts of Parliament merely because they are unjust. | Courts can declare law or conduct unconstitutional and invalid. |
| Rights protection | Rights depend on ordinary legislation and political will. | Rights are entrenched and justiciable. |
| Historical association | Apartheid system and Westminster-style parliamentary sovereignty. | Post-apartheid democratic constitutional order. |
| Democratic risk | Majority or ruling party can dominate legally. | Majority rule is limited by constitutional values and rights. |
| Core legal section | No equivalent supremacy clause. | Constitution ss 1(c), 2 and 172. |

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Textbook authority: Currie and De Waal discussion of Westminster constitutionalism, parliamentary supremacy and the move to constitutional supremacy. Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 1(c), 2, 172. |

## Why supremacy matters in South Africa

Supremacy matters because apartheid showed that parliamentary power can be legally valid but morally unjust when Parliament is unconstrained. Under constitutional supremacy, no public institution may claim unlimited authority. Parliament, the executive, the judiciary and Chapter 9 institutions must all operate within the Constitution.

|  |
| --- |
| **CRITICAL EMPHASIS**  The point of supremacy is not that courts replace democracy. The point is that democracy itself operates under the Constitution. This is why South Africa is a constitutional democracy, not a system of unlimited majority rule. |

|  |

# Transformative Constitutionalism[[5]](#footnote-5)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Transformative constitutionalism**  Transformative constitutionalism is the long-term project of using constitutional enactment, interpretation and enforcement to transform South Africa's political and social institutions and power relationships in a democratic, participatory and egalitarian direction.  Authority: Klare definition as used in Theme 4 lecture 15-17; Kleyn et al p 52; CJ Pius Langa lecture. |

The 1996 Constitution is seen as inherently transformative because its central aim is to move South Africa from an unjust past to a just future. The founding values in section 1 confirm the intention to break from the past. Transformation is therefore not a side issue. It is foundational to constitutional interpretation and constitutional identity.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Preamble; Constitution s 1(a)-(d); s 7(1). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17 on transformative constitutionalism. |

## Klare definition unpacked

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Part of definition** | **Meaning for exam answers** |
| Long-term project | Transformation will not happen overnight. The Constitution initiates a continuing project. |
| Constitutional enactment | The text of the Constitution itself is part of the project. |
| Interpretation | Courts, lawyers and legal actors must interpret law in a way that advances constitutional values. |
| Enforcement | Rights and obligations must be given practical effect, not left symbolic. |
| Historical context | Transformation must be read against colonialism, apartheid and structural inequality. |
| Political and social institutions | The project reaches beyond courts into Parliament, executive government, legal education and society. |
| Power relationships | The Constitution seeks to change unequal relations of power. |
| Democratic, participatory and egalitarian direction | Transformation must deepen democracy, participation and equality. |

## Features of transformative constitutionalism

1. It is long-term: transformation is not immediate, and the question remains whether 30 years is enough to address more than 250 years of colonial and apartheid history.
2. It is interpretive and enforcement-based: transformation depends on how legal actors interpret and enforce the Constitution in context.
3. It is concerned with substantive equality: it goes beyond formal legal equality to address economic, social and structural inequality.
4. It sees law as proactive: unlike a purely liberal shield model, it sees the Constitution as a tool or sword for social change and justice.
5. It promotes rights and dignity in daily social and legal culture.
6. It is a non-violent revolution: large-scale social change is pursued through political processes grounded in law.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17 slides "Defining Transformative Constitutionalism". Constitutional authority: Preamble; Constitution ss 1, 7(1), 9, 10, 26, 27, 29. |

## Klare's broader characteristics

The lecture notes that Klare describes transformative constitutionalism as social, redistributive, caring, positive, at least partly horizontal, participatory, egalitarian, multicultural and self-conscious about its historical setting and transformative role and mission. Each of these words is examinable because it shows that transformation is not merely formal or institutional.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Characteristic** | **Meaning** |
| Social | The Constitution is concerned with social relations, not only state institutions. |
| Redistributive | It is concerned with material inequality and the distribution of resources. |
| Caring | It values vulnerability, dignity and social responsibility. |
| Positive | It requires active change rather than mere restraint. |
| Partly horizontal | Rights and values may shape private relationships, not only state-citizen relationships. |
| Participatory | People must have meaningful roles in constitutional democracy. |
| Egalitarian | It aims toward equality in substance, not only equality in form. |
| Multicultural | It recognises diversity and difference. |
| Historically self-conscious | It openly recognises past injustice and uses constitutional law to respond to it. |

## CJ Pius Langa: bridge metaphor

Chief Justice Pius Langa described the Constitution as a bridge between an unjust, divided past and a future based on rights, equality and dignity. The objective of the Constitution is to establish a truly equal society, including through the provision of basic socio-economic rights to all. Transformation therefore means a commitment to change, not simply a change in legal documents.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Authority: CJ Pius Langa, Transformative Constitutionalism lecture; Theme 4 lecture 15-17. Constitutional link: Preamble; s 7(1); ss 26, 27 and 29. |

## Culture of justification

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Culture of justification**  A constitutional culture in which every exercise of public power and every legal decision must be capable of substantive justification in terms of constitutional rights and values.  Authority: CJ Pius Langa; Mureinik, A bridge to where; Constitution ss 1, 2, 7, 36, 39. |

Under apartheid, public power often operated through a culture of authority: the fact that an authorised institution had acted was treated as enough. Under the transformative Constitution, authority is not enough. Decisions must be defended by reference to reasons, rights and values.

Langa emphasises that judges cannot rely only on the say-so of Parliament or technical readings of legislation. Under a transformative Constitution, judges must justify decisions by reference to authority and to constitutional ideas and values.

|  |

| **CRITICAL EMPHASIS**  Exam point: Culture of justification is one of the best bridge concepts in Theme 4. It links constitutional supremacy, rule of law, value-based interpretation, transformative constitutionalism, limitation analysis and judicial review. |

## Criticisms of transformative constitutionalism

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Criticism** | **Meaning** | **How to evaluate it** |
| Persistent inequality and slow pace of change | Large-scale social and economic transformation remains unfulfilled despite the constitutional framework. | Acknowledge poverty, inequality and land reform challenges, but distinguish failure of implementation from failure of constitutional principle. |
| Too court-centric | Critics argue transformation relies too heavily on judges and lawyers. | Courts matter, but Parliament, executive, civil society, universities and private actors also carry transformative responsibilities. |
| Counter-majoritarian concern | Unelected judges may invalidate laws created by elected representatives. | In constitutional democracy, courts protect the Constitution, but must respect institutional roles and democratic competence. |
| Liberal compromise | Some argue the constitutional settlement preserved apartheid-era socio-economic structures. | Use as critical analysis: constitutional rights may need stronger political and material implementation. |
| Decolonial critique | Some argue transformative constitutionalism remains rooted in Western legal forms and colonial pedagogies. | Show awareness that legal transformation must also transform legal education, institutions and knowledge systems. |
| Fetishism of law | The Constitution can be treated like an exclusive answer to all social problems. | Law is essential but cannot alone solve structural inequality. Political and social action remain necessary. |

# Value-Based Constitutionalism[[6]](#footnote-6)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Value-based constitutionalism**  Value-based constitutionalism is the legal concept that constitutions are not merely legal or procedural documents but are rooted in core ethical, moral and social values. These values guide the interpretation of rights and laws.  Authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17 and 18-20; Constitution ss 1, 7(1), 36, 39. |

South African constitutional interpretation is not strict legal positivism. Courts do not ask only: "What does the law say?" They also ask: "What outcome best reflects constitutional values?" This is a break from the injustices of the past because apartheid law often had formal legality without substantive justice.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 15-17, value-based constitutionalism slides. Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 1(a)-(d), 7(1), 36, 39. |

## Section 1 values

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Value in s 1** | **Meaning** | **Exam use** |
| Human dignity | Recognition of inherent worth and personhood. | Core value in rights interpretation, Makwanyane, equality and freedom. |
| Achievement of equality | Substantive equality and redress of structural disadvantage. | Use in transformative constitutionalism and s 9. |
| Advancement of human rights and freedoms | Rights are foundational to the constitutional order. | Use with s 7(1) and Bill of Rights. |
| Non-racialism and non-sexism | Break from apartheid and patriarchy. | Use in equality, transformation and historical redress. |
| Supremacy of the Constitution | Constitution above all law and conduct. | Use in s 2 and judicial review. |
| Rule of law | Power must be lawful, rational and non-arbitrary. | Use in legality and EFF v Speaker. |
| Universal adult suffrage | Every adult citizen has the vote. | Use in democracy and representative government. |
| Common voters roll and regular elections | Institutional democracy. | Use in representative democracy. |
| Multi-party democratic government | Plural democratic politics. | Use in political parties, representation and GNU discussion. |
| Accountability, responsiveness and openness | Government must answer to the people and act transparently. | Use in democracy, public participation and oversight. |

## Five characteristics of value-based constitutionalism

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Characteristic** | **Meaning** |
| Normative | The Constitution is based on moral values, not just legal rules. |
| Interpretive | Values guide how courts understand and apply rights. |
| Transformative | The Constitution aims to change society and address injustice. |
| Contextual | It must respond to and reflect South Africa's social and historical context. |
| Counter-majoritarian | Courts may uphold constitutional values even against public opinion. |

# Limitation of Rights: Section 36[[7]](#footnote-7)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Limitation clause**  A limitation clause allows constitutionally valid limitation of rights in certain circumstances. Under s 36, rights in the Bill of Rights may be limited only by law of general application to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom.  Authority: Constitution s 36; Theme 4 lecture 15-17; De Vos and Freedman pp 453-455. |

Rights are not absolute. The Constitution permits rights to be limited, but only under strict constitutional conditions. Section 36 ensures that rights are not balanced away casually. A court must ask whether the limitation is legally authorised, rational, proportionate and consistent with an open and democratic society based on dignity, equality and freedom.

## Two-stage enquiry

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stage** | **Question** | **Authority** |
| Stage 1: infringement | Has a right in the Bill of Rights been infringed or limited? | S v Walters; Theme 4 lecture; s 36. |
| Stage 2: justification | If there is a limitation, is it reasonable and justifiable under s 36? | Constitution s 36; De Vos and Freedman pp 453-455. |

## Section 36 factors

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Factor** | **Question to ask** | **Exam use** |
| Nature of the right | How important is the right and what interests does it protect? | Life, dignity and equality usually carry very high weight. |
| Importance of purpose | What goal is the state or limiting law trying to achieve? | The purpose must be legitimate in an open and democratic society. |
| Nature and extent of limitation | How severely does the limitation interfere with the right? | A severe infringement needs stronger justification. |
| Relation between limitation and purpose | Is there a rational connection between the means and the purpose? | A measure that does not achieve its aim cannot be justified. |
| Less restrictive means | Could the purpose be achieved in a way that limits the right less? | This is often decisive in proportionality. |

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: s 36(1)(a)-(e). Case authority: S v Walters on two-stage limitation enquiry; S v Makwanyane on application of values and proportionality. Reading authority: De Vos and Freedman pp 453-455 on threshold questions, rational connection, less restrictive means and proportionality. |

## De Vos/Freedman five-stage proportionality guide

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Stage** | **Question** |
| Stage 1 | What is the purpose of the limitation and is it legitimate in an open and democratic society? |
| Stage 2 | What is the relationship between the limitation and its legitimate purpose? Are they rationally connected? |
| Stage 3 | Are there alternate, less restrictive means? |
| Stage 4 | Is the limitation proportionate, taking into account the extent of the infringement, nature of the right, breadth of the limitation and social good achieved? |
| Stage 5 | If proportionate, the infringement is constitutionally valid; if not, it is constitutionally invalid. |

# Application of the Bill of Rights[[8]](#footnote-8)

The Bill of Rights is constructed to give effect to the transformative vision of the 1996 Constitution. It sets out a unique human rights vision for South Africa. The broad range of rights is aimed at facilitating political and socio-economic transformation while protecting human dignity. Importantly, it has broad application: it applies to the state and, in certain circumstances, to private parties.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution ss 7, 8, 36, 39. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20, Bill of Rights application slides. |

## The application decision tree

|  |
| --- |
| **APPLICATION FLOW**  Step 1: Identify the right in Chapter 2. Step 2: Ask whether the right is being applied directly or indirectly. Step 3: Ask whether the relationship is vertical or horizontal. Step 4: Identify the relevant constitutional section: s 8(1), s 8(2) or s 39(2). Step 5: If rights compete, balance them using constitutional values and s 36 where relevant. |

## Direct vs indirect application

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Type** | **Meaning** | **Legal basis** | **Example** |
| Direct application | The Bill of Rights itself applies to conduct or law. A person relies directly on a constitutional right. | s 8; s 2 where law/conduct inconsistent with Constitution is invalid. | Death penalty invalidated in Makwanyane; private employer discrimination may trigger equality directly. |
| Indirect application | The Bill of Rights influences interpretation or development of ordinary law rather than directly invalidating it. | s 39(2). | Carmichele: common law of delict developed in light of constitutional values. |

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Direct application**  Direct application occurs when a person, company or organ of state violates a constitutional right in Chapter 2, or where ordinary law such as legislation, common law or customary law is inconsistent with the Bill of Rights. If applied directly, the Bill of Rights overrides inconsistent law or conduct.  Authority: Constitution ss 2, 8; Theme 4 lecture 18-20. |

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Indirect application**  Indirect application asks whether ordinary rules of law promote the values of the Bill of Rights. If they do not, courts interpret legislation or develop the common law or customary law to conform with constitutional values.  Authority: Constitution s 39(2); Carmichele; Theme 4 lecture 18-20. |

## Vertical vs horizontal application

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Type** | **Relationship** | **Constitutional section** | **Purpose** |
| Vertical application | State versus individual/private person. | s 8(1). | Protects people against abuse of public power and binds the legislature, executive, judiciary and all organs of state. |
| Horizontal application | Private person versus private person, including individuals and companies. | s 8(2), read with the nature of the right and duty. | Extends constitutional values into private relationships where appropriate. |

## Vertical application: section 8(1)

Vertical application looks at the relationship between the state and private individuals. It imposes obligations on the state to respect, protect, promote and fulfil rights in the Bill of Rights. It operates as an additional check on state power and protects citizens from government abuse.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 8(1); s 7(2). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20, vertical application slides. |

### Examples of vertical application

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Right** | **Example from lecture** | **Authority** |
| Equality - s 9 | Individual sues a government department for refusing to hire or promote them based on race, gender or sexual orientation. | Constitution s 9; s 8(1). |
| Freedom of expression - s 16 | Journalist or citizen challenges police arrest for lawful public protest. | Constitution s 16; s 17; s 8(1). |
| Access to information - s 32 | Person uses PAIA to require a public body such as DHA to disclose state records. | Constitution s 32; PAIA; s 8(1). |
| Socio-economic rights - ss 26 and 27 | Community challenges government for failing to provide adequate basic water or housing; Grootboom-type claim. | Constitution ss 26, 27; Grootboom. |
| Fair trial - s 35 | Accused argues police or prosecutor violated rights during arrest or detention. | Constitution s 35. |
| Property - s 25 | Landowner challenges state expropriation not for public purpose or without fair compensation. | Constitution s 25. |

## Horizontal application: section 8(2)

Horizontal application is governed by section 8(2). It requires consideration of the nature of the right and the nature of the duty imposed. When applied horizontally, the Bill of Rights imposes obligations between private persons in certain circumstances. This is important because rights violations do not only come from the state. Private power can also undermine dignity, equality, housing, education, expression and other rights.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 8(2). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20, horizontal application slides. |

|  |
| --- |
| **CRITICAL EMPHASIS**  Horizontal application is a transformative feature. It shows that the Constitution does not only restrain the state. It can reshape private law and private relationships where power imbalance, human needs or constitutional values require it. |

## Direct horizontal application

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Direct horizontal application**  Direct horizontal application occurs when the Bill of Rights applies directly to private conduct, allowing a private person to rely directly on a constitutional right in a dispute with another private person.  Authority: Constitution s 8(2); Theme 4 lecture 18-20. |

## Indirect horizontal application

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Indirect horizontal application**  Indirect horizontal application occurs when the Bill of Rights influences private law through section 39(2), requiring courts to interpret legislation or develop common law in a way that promotes the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.  Authority: Constitution s 39(2); Carmichele. |

## Examples of horizontal application

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Right** | **Scenario** | **Court's likely question** |
| Equality - s 9 | Private employer refuses to hire someone based on race, gender or disability. | Does equality apply directly in this private relationship? Discrimination is not only a state problem. |
| Expression - s 16 | Private media company refuses to publish an opinion article. | Balance writer's freedom of expression against company's freedom to choose what it publishes. |
| Housing - s 26 | Private landowner evicts people without following legal processes. | Is eviction just and equitable? What process was followed? What is the impact on vulnerable people? How does s 25 property balance with s 26 housing? |
| Education - s 29 | Private school refuses admission without fair and lawful reason. | Does exclusion undermine basic education? Are contractual or policy justifications reasonable? How does basic education balance with school autonomy? |

## Key takeaways from the examples

* Rights can shape private relationships, especially where there is a power imbalance.
* Rights can shape private relationships where basic human needs are affected.
* Direct horizontal application means a person may use the Constitution itself in disputes with private parties.
* Courts will always ask whether direct horizontal application is appropriate and how competing rights should be balanced.
* The answer depends on the nature of the right, relationship between the parties and need to balance competing rights.

| **COMMON MISTAKE / WARNING**  Common mistake: saying horizontal application always applies between private parties. Section 8(2) requires analysis of the nature of the right and duty imposed. It is not automatic for every right in every private dispute. |

# Rule of Law[[9]](#footnote-9)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Rule of law**  The rule of law means that public power must be exercised according to law, not arbitrary will. It requires legality, rationality, accountability and respect for constitutional limits.  Authority: Constitution s 1(c); s 2; Fedsure; Pharmaceutical Manufacturers; EFF v Speaker. |

The rule of law is a founding value in section 1(c), together with constitutional supremacy. It is therefore not just a technical doctrine. It is one of the foundations of the Republic. It ensures that public power cannot be exercised outside lawful authority or in an arbitrary manner.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Constitution s 1(c), s 2, s 172. Case authority: Fedsure Life Assurance; Pharmaceutical Manufacturers; EFF v Speaker. |

## Principle of legality

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Principle of legality**  The principle of legality is the constitutional rule that all exercises of public power must have legal authority and must be rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose.  Authority: Constitution s 1(c); Pharmaceutical Manufacturers; Fedsure. |

Legality is often treated as a minimum requirement of the rule of law. Even where no specific right is directly invoked, state action must still comply with legality. This means there must be legal authority for the action, the decision-maker must act within power and the action must be rational.

# Democracy in the 1996 Constitution[[11]](#footnote-11)

Democracy is one of the dominant and enduring political ideas of modern times. In the 1996 Constitution, democracy is a core concept and value. It permeates virtually all aspects of the Constitution. South Africa is not merely a democracy in a voting sense. It is a constitutional democracy based on democratic values, accountability, responsiveness, openness and human rights.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: Preamble; Constitution s 1(d); s 7(1); s 19; ss 42(3), 59, 72, 118. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20 democracy slides. |

## Preamble and democratic values

The Preamble links democracy to healing the divisions of the past, establishing a society based on democratic values, social justice and fundamental human rights, laying the foundations for a democratic and open society in which government is based on the will of the people and every citizen is equally protected by law, improving quality of life and building a united and democratic South Africa.

|  |
| --- |
| **PREAMBLE EXAM USE**  Use the Preamble to show that democracy in South Africa is not only elections. It is linked to social justice, healing past divisions, equality before law, openness, dignity and transformation. |

## Section 1(d)

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Section 1(d) democratic state**  South Africa is founded on universal adult suffrage, a national common voters roll, regular elections and a multi-party system of democratic government to ensure accountability, responsiveness and openness.  Authority: Constitution s 1(d). |

## Theuys Roux definition

The lectures use Theuys Roux's idea that the core of democracy is that decisions affecting members of a political community should be taken by the members themselves, or at least by elected representatives whose power to make those decisions ultimately derives from the members. This captures both direct and representative dimensions of democracy.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20, democracy definition slides citing Theuys Roux. |

## Forms of democracy

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Form** | **Meaning** | **Constitutional authority** | **Exam point** |
| Direct democracy | Members themselves make decisions affecting the political community. | s 17; s 84(2)(g); s 127(2)(f). | South Africa has elements of direct democracy, but not a strong substantive direct democracy system. |
| Representative democracy | Elected representatives make most decisions. Their power ultimately derives from the people. | s 42(3); s 19(3)(a); s 1(d). | This is the main workable form of democracy in modern states. |
| Participatory democracy | Citizens have opportunities to be heard in decisions that affect them. | s 1(d); ss 59(1)(a), 72(1)(a), 118. | Participation supports and gives vitality to representative democracy. |
| Constitutional democracy | Democratic decisions are made in terms of a supreme Constitution and limited by rights and values. | s 1, s 2, s 7, s 36, s 39, judicial review. | Democracy is not unlimited majority rule. |

## Direct democracy

Direct democracy means that decisions affecting members of the political community are taken by the members themselves. It is historically considered the purest form of democracy. In South Africa, examples include the right to assemble, demonstrate, picket and present petitions peacefully and unarmed, the President's power to call a national referendum and a provincial premier's power to call a provincial referendum.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: s 17; s 84(2)(g); s 127(2)(f). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20. Suggested further authority in slides: Merafong discussion in De Vos s 2.5.3. |

|  |
| --- |
| **COMMON MISTAKE / WARNING**  Common mistake: saying South Africa is a direct democracy. Better: South Africa has elements of direct democracy, but its main democratic system is representative, participatory and constitutional. |

## Representative democracy

Representative democracy exists because most decisions are not made directly by the citizenry. Citizens elect representatives to govern for a limited time. The National Assembly is elected to represent the people and ensure government by the people. Every adult citizen has the right to vote in elections. Political parties historically formed the main link between citizens and government in South Africa's proportional representation system.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: s 42(3), s 19(3)(a), s 1(d). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20. |

## Multi-party democracy and electoral reform

The Constitution founds South Africa on a multi-party system of democratic government. Multi-party government involves three or more political parties having the capacity to gain control of government separately or through coalition. It is characterised by competitive elections, proportional representation and coalition or party collaboration to form majorities.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: s 1(d) read with s 19. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 18-20, representative democracy slides. |

## Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023 and 2024 elections

The Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023 allows independent candidates not affiliated to political parties to contest elections for the National Assembly and provincial legislatures for the first time. It came into effect for the 2024 elections. The lecture notes that independent candidates must prove support to qualify, with candidates needing 1000 signatures in support.

The 2024 elections resulted in no party obtaining a majority, leading to a Government of National Unity. Democratic implications include power-sharing and more inclusive governance through multi-party collaboration, stronger checks and balances because no single majority controls policy-making, and challenges arising from ideological tensions over issues such as VAT, NHI and foreign policy.

## Limitations of representative democracy

* Failure to regulate political parties can produce elite-driven democracy.
* Citizens may be unable to directly influence elected members between elections.
* Those not actively involved in political parties may have limited influence.
* One-party dominance can affect democratic accountability.
* Dominance may erode the separation between political parties and the state.
* Electoral reform may help, but does not automatically solve all accountability problems.

## Participatory democracy

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Participatory democracy**  Participatory democracy affords citizens an opportunity to have their voices heard when decisions are made that affect them.  Authority: Constitution ss 1(d), 59(1)(a), 72(1)(a), 118; Doctors for Life. |

# Separation of Powers

|  |
| --- |
| **DEFINITION - Separation of powers**  Separation of powers is a procedural doctrine that divides government power between the legislature, executive and judiciary, separates their functions and personnel to a degree, and creates checks and balances to avoid concentration of power.  Authority: Theme 4 lecture 21; Constitutional Principle VI; Constitution chapters 4, 5 and 8. |

The lecture frames separation of powers as a procedural limit on public power. Rights are substantive limits because they protect individuals and groups. Separation of powers is procedural because it controls the ways or manners in which government exercises public power.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21. Constitutional authority: Constitution ch 4, ch 5, ch 8; ss 43, 85, 165; s 1(d) accountability; s 2 supremacy. |

## Four principles

1. Division of government power.
2. Separation of functions.
3. Separation of personnel.
4. System of checks and balances.

## Classical model vs South African model

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Classical model** | **South African model** |
| Strict conceptual division between legislature, executive and judiciary. | The branches are separated but there is overlap, especially between legislature and executive. |
| No overlap is the ideal. | Overlap is accepted in South Africa, particularly due to Westminster influence and parliamentary executive arrangements. |
| Judiciary separate from politics. | Judicial independence remains strongly protected. There should be no improper overlap with the judiciary. |
| Three branches only. | Chapter 9 institutions introduce an additional layer of accountability outside the classic three-branch model. |

|  |
| --- |
| **CRITICAL EMPHASIS**  The branches are not a hierarchy. Separation of powers is about divided authority, functional competence and checks and balances, not one branch being generally superior to another. |

## Why separation of powers matters

* It avoids concentration of power, for example preventing the executive from taking over law-making power entirely.
* It promotes specialisation, for example trained judges adjudicating legal disputes.
* It supports accountability and checks and balances.
* It protects judicial independence.
* It is closely linked to human rights because procedural limits help protect substantive rights.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21, concepts and principles slides. Constitutional authority: s 1(d), s 2, ch 4, ch 5, ch 8. |

## Historical background

The doctrine has roots in 17th century Enlightenment political philosophy, including the development from Hobbes to Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau and the United States constitutional tradition. It is linked to the historical experience of monarchical and tyrannical governments, the rise of liberalism, negative conceptions of freedom and constitutionalism. The lecture warns that this history is an oversimplification, but it helps explain why divided power became a constitutional ideal.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21, historical background slides. Textbook authority: Currie and De Waal pp 10-21 on Westminster and American constitutionalism. |

## Application in South Africa

The final Constitution does not expressly mention "separation of powers" as a single phrase. However, Constitutional Principle VI in the Interim Constitution required separation of powers, and the Constitutional Court has treated the doctrine as implicit in the Final Constitution. The South African model has no universal template; it must be understood historically and contextually.

|  |
| --- |
| **AUTHORITY**  Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21; Constitutional Principle VI; De Lange v Smuts; Certification judgments. |

## Legislature

The legislature is governed mainly by Chapter 4 of the Constitution. Parliament consists of the National Assembly and National Council of Provinces. It makes legislation and holds the executive accountable. There is overlap with the executive because members of the executive are generally drawn from the National Assembly, reflecting Westminster influence.

| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: ch 4; ss 42(3), 43, 44, 55. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21. |

## Executive

The executive is governed by sections 84 and 85 of the Constitution. The President has powers and functions under section 84, while executive authority is addressed in section 85. Cabinet is composed of Ministers, and there is an obligation to select Cabinet from the National Assembly, which creates overlap between legislature and executive. The National Assembly exercises oversight, may recall the executive, initiate votes of no confidence and hold the executive accountable under section 55(2).

| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: ss 84, 85, 89, 102, 55(2). Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21. |

## Judiciary

The judiciary is governed by Chapter 8 of the Constitution. It includes the hierarchy of courts and is protected by judicial independence and impartiality. Appointment of judges through the Judicial Service Commission involves all three branches in different ways, but judicial independence must be protected. Judges may be removed only on limited grounds such as incapacity, gross incompetence or gross misconduct, through constitutionally regulated procedures.

|  |

| **AUTHORITY**  Constitutional authority: ch 8; ss 165, 174, 177. Lecture authority: Theme 4 lecture 21; SA Association of Personal Injury Lawyers v Heath mentioned in slides. |

## Judicial review and remedies

Section 172 empowers courts to declare law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution invalid to the extent of inconsistency. Section 167(5) gives the Constitutional Court final authority on whether an Act of Parliament, provincial Act or conduct of the President is constitutional. These provisions show how judicial review gives practical effect to constitutional supremacy.

## Chapter 9 institutions

Chapter 9 institutions are a unique set of institutions supporting constitutional democracy. They do not fall neatly within the classic legislature-executive-judiciary model. Examples include the Public Protector and South African Human Rights Commission. They exercise oversight, occupy a unique position and introduce another layer of checks and balances. Their independence is essential.

South African courts must enforce constitutional supremacy without taking over the functions of the political branches. This produces a difficult balance. Courts must not abdicate their role, but they must also avoid prescribing policy details where the Constitution leaves discretion to another branch.

# Separation of Powers - Expanded Primary Notes[[13]](#footnote-13)

|  |
| --- |
| **WHY THIS EXTRA SECTION MATTERS**  Separation of powers is often tested because it links almost every part of Theme 4: constitutionalism, rule of law, democracy, judicial review, accountability, Chapter 9 institutions and transformative constitutionalism. The key exam move is to explain that South Africa uses a flexible, contextual model rather than a rigid textbook model. |

## 1. Separation of powers as a procedural limit on public power[[14]](#footnote-14)

The lecture distinguishes substantive and procedural limits on public power. Rights are substantive limits because they set content-based boundaries on what the state may do to people. Separation of powers is a procedural limit because it controls the manner, institutional route and legal process through which public power is exercised. In other words, it asks not only whether government may act, but which institution may act, how that institution may act, and which institution may review or check that action.[[15]](#footnote-15)

## 2. The four principles: what each one really means[[16]](#footnote-16)

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Principle** | **Meaning** | **Exam explanation** |
| Division of government power | Power is allocated among different branches/institutions. | Use this to show why no single institution may monopolise public power. |
| Separation of functions | Different branches perform different core tasks. | Legislature makes law; executive implements policy; judiciary interprets and applies law. |
| Separation of personnel | The same people should not completely control all branches. | South Africa has some overlap between legislature and executive, but judicial personnel must remain independent. |
| Checks and balances | Branches may control, review, scrutinise or hold each other accountable. | The doctrine is not only about separation. It also requires mechanisms of accountability. |

## 3. The classical model and the South African model[[17]](#footnote-17)

The classical model imagines three distinct branches: legislature, executive and judiciary. The legislature makes law, the executive puts law and policy into operation, and the judiciary decides disputes and interprets law. The South African model accepts this basic division, but does not apply it mechanically. There is overlap between the legislature and executive because the President appoints Cabinet largely from the National Assembly and because Parliament oversees executive action. The judiciary, however, must remain institutionally independent because its function is to decide legal disputes and enforce constitutional supremacy.[[18]](#footnote-18)[[19]](#footnote-19)[[20]](#footnote-20)

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Classical idea** | **South African correction** |
| Three separate branches with minimal overlap. | South Africa has a flexible model with accepted legislature-executive overlap. |
| Judiciary as one of three ordinary branches. | Judiciary has a special role because it enforces constitutional supremacy and must remain independent. |
| Checks are sometimes treated as secondary. | Checks and balances are essential because the Constitution requires accountability, responsiveness and openness. |
| Focus on the three branches only. | Chapter 9 institutions add an extra accountability layer that supports constitutional democracy. |

## 4. Why there is overlap between legislature and executive[[21]](#footnote-21)

South Africa inherits features from the Westminster tradition, where the executive is closely connected to Parliament. The National Assembly represents the people and ensures government by the people, while also scrutinising and overseeing executive action. Cabinet members are usually drawn from the National Assembly, so complete separation of personnel does not exist. This is constitutionally acceptable, provided the overlap does not destroy accountability, democratic control or the independence of the judiciary.[[22]](#footnote-22)[[23]](#footnote-23)

## 5. Legislature: law-making and oversight[[24]](#footnote-24)

The legislature is mainly regulated by Chapter 4 of the Constitution. Parliament consists of the National Assembly and the National Council of Provinces. Its law-making role must be understood together with its accountability role: the National Assembly is elected to represent the people and to ensure government by the people. It does this by choosing the President, scrutinising and overseeing executive action, and maintaining mechanisms to hold the executive accountable.[[25]](#footnote-25)[[26]](#footnote-26)[[27]](#footnote-27)

## 6. Executive: policy, implementation and accountability[[28]](#footnote-28)

The executive develops policy, administers the state and implements legislation. Sections 84 and 85 are central. Section 84 contains presidential powers and functions. Section 85 concerns executive authority. The executive is powerful because it controls administration and policy implementation, but it is not legally free-floating: it is bound by the Constitution, accountable to Parliament and subject to legality and judicial review.[[29]](#footnote-29)[[30]](#footnote-30)

## 7. Judiciary: independence, review and the limits of judicial power[[31]](#footnote-31)

The judiciary must apply and interpret the law independently and impartially. Section 165 provides that judicial authority is vested in the courts, that courts are independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law, and that no person or organ of state may interfere with the functioning of the courts. This is why the lecture asks why there should be no improper overlap with the judiciary: judicial independence is the condition that makes constitutional supremacy enforceable.[[32]](#footnote-32)

At the same time, courts must respect the institutional roles of other branches. Judicial review allows courts to declare invalid law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution, but courts should not ordinarily prescribe detailed policy choices to Parliament or the executive where the Constitution leaves those choices to the political branches. The task is to enforce constitutional boundaries without becoming the legislature or executive.[[33]](#footnote-33)[[34]](#footnote-34)

## 8. Delegation and flexibility: Executive Council of the Western Cape[[35]](#footnote-35)

Delegation of law-making power is an important exam example because it shows that separation of powers is not absolute. The Constitutional Court accepted that some delegation of law-making authority may be permissible, but its constitutional validity depends on the nature and extent of the delegation. The question is whether delegation remains compatible with democracy, specialisation and the need to avoid concentration of power.[[36]](#footnote-36)

## 9. Chapter 9 institutions as a fourth accountability layer[[37]](#footnote-37)

Chapter 9 institutions complicate the classical three-branch model. They are state institutions supporting constitutional democracy. They are independent and must be assisted and protected by other organs of state to ensure their independence, impartiality, dignity and effectiveness. The Public Protector is the most important Theme 4 example because EFF v Speaker confirms that remedial action can be binding and may not simply be ignored or second-guessed without approaching a court.[[38]](#footnote-38)[[39]](#footnote-39)

## 10. EFF v Speaker: separation of powers, accountability and the Public Protector[[40]](#footnote-40)

EFF v Speaker is vital because it shows separation of powers operating in real constitutional conflict. The Public Protector took remedial action against the President. The President did not fully comply. The National Assembly adopted a resolution absolving the President from liability. The Constitutional Court held that the Public Protector’s remedial action was binding and that both the President and the National Assembly failed to fulfil constitutional obligations. The case links rule of law, Chapter 9 institutions, parliamentary oversight, executive accountability and judicial enforcement.[[41]](#footnote-41)

The separation of powers point is nuanced. The Court could declare that the National Assembly failed to fulfil constitutional duties, but it also emphasised that it was not for the judiciary to prescribe exactly how the National Assembly must conduct oversight or design its internal accountability mechanisms. That is the deference principle: courts enforce constitutional obligations while respecting institutional competence.[[42]](#footnote-42)

## 11. De Lange v Smuts: no universal model[[43]](#footnote-43)

De Lange is useful because it supports the idea that separation of powers has no single universal model. Each constitutional system gives content to the doctrine in its own text, history and institutional design. In South Africa, apartheid history, Westminster inheritance, constitutional supremacy, judicial independence and Chapter 9 institutions all shape the local model.[[44]](#footnote-44)[[45]](#footnote-45)

## 12. Counter-majoritarian dilemma and deference

The counter-majoritarian dilemma arises because unelected judges may invalidate law or conduct of elected representatives. In South Africa this is not a defect in the constitutional system; it is part of constitutional supremacy. However, courts must exercise review carefully. Deference means that courts recognise the expertise, democratic mandate or constitutional function of another branch, but deference does not mean surrender. Courts must still enforce rights, legality and constitutional obligations.[[46]](#footnote-46)[[47]](#footnote-47)

## 13. Participatory democracy as a separation of powers check[[48]](#footnote-48)

Doctors for Life shows that separation of powers is not only about relations between institutions. It also concerns the constitutional relationship between institutions and the people. When Parliament makes law, it must facilitate public involvement. Public participation gives vitality to representative democracy and prevents law-making from becoming purely elite-driven.[[49]](#footnote-49)

## 15. Model paragraph: separation of powers

Separation of powers is a procedural constitutional doctrine that divides public power between the legislature, executive and judiciary, while also creating checks and balances between them. In South Africa, the doctrine is not applied as a rigid universal model. It must be understood contextually, because the Constitution permits some overlap between the legislature and executive, especially through parliamentary government, but strongly protects judicial independence. The purpose of the doctrine is to avoid concentration of power, promote institutional specialisation and ensure accountability. Cases such as Executive Council of the Western Cape show that some delegation is constitutionally permissible, while EFF v Speaker shows that courts may enforce constitutional obligations without prescribing the exact internal mechanisms Parliament must use. Separation of powers therefore operates together with constitutional supremacy, rule of law and democracy.[[50]](#footnote-50)[[51]](#footnote-51)[[52]](#footnote-52)

## Model 5-mark answer: horizontal and vertical application

|  |
| --- |
| **5-MARK MODEL**  Vertical application refers to the application of the Bill of Rights in relationships between the state and private persons. It is governed by s 8(1), which binds the legislature, executive, judiciary and all organs of state. For example, a person may challenge unlawful arrest by the police under the Bill of Rights. Horizontal application refers to application between private persons and is governed by s 8(2), depending on the nature of the right and duty. For example, a private employer refusing to hire someone because of race may implicate equality under s 9. The distinction matters because the Constitution can control both public power and, where appropriate, private power. |

## Model 10-mark answer: transformative constitutionalism

|  |
| --- |
| **10-MARK MODEL**  Transformative constitutionalism describes the long-term project of using constitutional enactment, interpretation and enforcement to transform South Africa's political and social institutions and power relationships in a democratic, participatory and egalitarian direction. Its historical purpose is to move South Africa from an unjust apartheid past to a future based on dignity, equality and freedom. This is reflected in the Preamble, s 1 values and s 7(1), which describes the Bill of Rights as a cornerstone of democracy. Klare emphasises long-term transformation, while Langa explains that the Constitution creates a culture of justification where decisions must be defended by reference to rights and values. Soobramoney shows that the Court recognised poverty, inequality and lack of access to basic services as central to the constitutional project. Critically, transformative constitutionalism has been criticised as too slow, too court-centric and unable on its own to solve structural inequality. Therefore, it is best understood as a foundational but incomplete project requiring courts, legislature, executive and society to participate in constitutional change. |
