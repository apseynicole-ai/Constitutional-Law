# **4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer**

## **A. Introduction: why this topic matters**

The constitutional crisis of the 1950s is important because it shows the limits of judicial review under South Africa’s pre-1994 constitutional order. Under parliamentary sovereignty, Parliament was treated as the supreme law-maker. Courts could interpret legislation and, in limited cases, enforce procedural requirements, but they could not invalidate legislation simply because it was unjust, oppressive or inconsistent with fundamental rights.

This matters because apartheid was built through law. If Parliament had the final say and there was no entrenched, justiciable Bill of Rights, then racially oppressive laws could be legally valid even if they were morally illegitimate. The revision lecture says parliamentary sovereignty enabled apartheid legislation because there were no effective legal checks and balances on Parliament.

The key through-line is therefore:

**Harris I shows that courts could enforce procedure; Harris II shows that Parliament could not convert itself into a court; Collins shows that once Parliament complied with procedure, courts could not review the substance or motive of the legislation.**

That is the line you must keep clear in the exam.

## **B. Definition of parliamentary sovereignty**

Precision point: South African parliamentary sovereignty was not pure Diceyan parliamentary sovereignty. Parliament was substantively supreme, but the South Africa Act 1909 created limited entrenched provisions and manner-and-form procedures. Courts could enforce those procedures, but they could not invalidate legislation because it was morally unjust, racially oppressive or inconsistent with rights.

**Parliamentary sovereignty** means that Parliament is the highest law-making authority. In the classical Westminster sense, Parliament can make, amend or repeal any law, and no ordinary court can invalidate an Act of Parliament merely because it is unjust or rights-violating.

In the South African pre-1994 context, parliamentary sovereignty meant that:

Parliament was the supreme law-maker;

there was no supreme, fully justiciable Constitution controlling the substance of legislation, although the South Africa Act 1909 imposed limited manner-and-form requirements for entrenched provisions;

courts could not strike down Acts of Parliament based on substantive injustice;

rights protection depended mainly on political will, not entrenched constitutional law;

judicial review was limited mainly to whether correct procedures had been followed.

The revision lecture states this very clearly: Parliament was the supreme law-maker, could make or unmake law, there was no supreme, fully justiciable Constitution controlling the substance of legislation, and courts could not strike down Acts of Parliament based on substance.

### **Exam sentence**

Parliamentary sovereignty meant that courts could not invalidate Acts of Parliament for substantive injustice, although they could enforce procedural or “manner and form” requirements where those requirements existed.

That sentence is important because it avoids the inaccurate claim that courts had **no review power at all**.

## **C. The old judicial role: limited review, not substantive review**

Under the old system, judicial review was narrow. Courts could review whether Parliament had followed required procedures, but they could not ask whether the content of the legislation was consistent with equality, dignity, freedom or rights.

So the old judicial role was:

| **Type of review** | **Was it allowed?** | **Meaning** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Procedural / manner and form review** | Yes, in limited cases | Courts could check whether Parliament followed entrenched procedural requirements. |
| **Substantive rights-based review** | No | Courts could not strike down legislation because it violated rights or was unjust. |
| **Review of political motive** | Generally no | Courts would not ask why Parliament secured a majority if the formal procedure was followed. |

The revision lecture explains that during apartheid, courts could declare Acts invalid only in a limited procedural sense, and that they could not review legislation based on substance.

## **D. Historical background: entrenched provisions under the 1910 Constitution**

Statutory precision: section 152 of the South Africa Act 1909 supplied the amendment mechanism for altering the Act, subject to entrenched procedures. The protected subject matter included provisions such as the Cape franchise protection in section 35 and language equality protection in section 137. In an exam, the safest phrasing is: the South Africa Act contained entrenched provisions that required special manner-and-form procedures.

The 1910 constitutional framework contained entrenched provisions. The lecture identifies two important entrenched areas:

**language provisions**; and

**limited voting rights / Cape franchise protections**, especially affecting Coloured voters and other non-white voters on the common roll.

To amend an entrenched provision, Parliament had to comply with a special procedure, including a **two-thirds majority**. The constitutional crisis began when Parliament attempted to remove voters from the common roll without following the prescribed procedure.

In 1951, Parliament passed the **Separate Representation of Voters Act**. Its purpose was to remove Coloured voters from the common roll and place them on a separate roll. Parliament passed the Act with the Houses sitting separately and by simple majority, rather than complying with the special entrenched procedure.

### **Exam sentence**

The crisis was not simply about voting; it was about whether Parliament could evade entrenched constitutional procedures in order to advance racial disenfranchisement.

## **E. Case 1: Harris I**

## **Facts**

In **Harris v Minister of the Interior** — usually called **Harris I** in the lectures — Parliament had passed the **Separate Representation of Voters Act 1951** to remove voters, especially Coloured voters in the Cape, from the common roll. Parliament did not comply with the special entrenched amendment procedure required by the 1910 constitutional framework.

## **Issue**

Could Parliament validly remove voters from the common roll **without complying with the entrenched “manner and form” amendment procedure**?

## **Court reasoning**

The Appellate Division said no. Even under parliamentary sovereignty, Parliament had to follow the procedural requirements laid down in the Constitution when amending entrenched provisions. The court was not saying that the Act was invalid because racial disenfranchisement was substantively unjust. Rather, it was saying that Parliament had failed to comply with the required procedure.

The revision lecture states that the Appellate Division held Parliament had to follow the “manner and form” requirements, and that the Act was declared invalid because the prescribed procedure had not been followed.

## **Judgment**

The Act was declared invalid.

## **Constitutional significance**

Harris I is important because it shows that courts were not completely powerless. They could enforce procedural limits on Parliament where the Constitution required a specific procedure. The lecture says Harris I recognised that procedural limits on Parliament were enforceable by courts and confirmed the idea of “manner and form” limitations.

## **Exam use**

Use Harris I for:

1. limited judicial review;
2. manner and form requirements;
3. entrenched provisions;
4. procedural invalidity;
5. the beginning of the constitutional crisis.

### **One-sentence case memory**

**Harris I = Parliament must follow entrenched constitutional procedure; courts could enforce manner and form limits, but not substantive rights.**

## **F. Case 2: Harris II**

## **Facts**

After losing in Harris I, the government refused to accept defeat. Parliament passed the **High Court of Parliament Act 35 of 1952**. This Act created a body made up of Members of Parliament and gave it the power to review and overturn decisions of the Appellate Division. In effect, Parliament tried to make itself the final court of appeal in constitutional matters.

## **Issue**

Could Parliament create a parliamentary body that functioned like a court and could overturn judgments of the Appellate Division?

## **Court reasoning**

The Appellate Division held that Parliament could not do this. The issue was not that Parliament could never regulate courts, but that it could not create an artificial parliamentary body outside the constitutional framework to function as a court of appeal over Appellate Division judgments or to override judicial decisions. This protected a limited boundary between legislative and judicial authority, even under the old order.

## **Judgment**

The **High Court of Parliament Act** was declared invalid.

## **Constitutional significance**

Harris II is important because it asserted a limited form of judicial independence and separation of powers under the old order. It did not create modern constitutional supremacy, but it did show that Parliament could not openly collapse the distinction between Parliament and the courts.

## **Exam use**

Use Harris II for:

High Court of Parliament Act;

separation of powers;

judicial independence;

Parliament cannot turn itself into a court;

distinction from Collins.

### **One-sentence case memory**

Harris II = Parliament cannot create an artificial parliamentary court to overturn judicial decisions.

## **G. Case 3: Collins v Minister of the Interior**

## **Facts**

Collins involved a different strategy from Harris II. Instead of trying to turn Parliament into a court, the government expanded the Senate to obtain the required majority and then passed the amendment through a joint sitting. The case challenged the validity of this change, arguing that Parliament had manipulated its own composition to secure the constitutional majority.

## **Issue**

Could the court invalidate the Senate changes and constitutional amendments because Parliament had manipulated the Senate to achieve the required majority?

## **Court reasoning**

The Appellate Division upheld the validity of the Senate Act and related constitutional amendments. Its reasoning was strictly formal: Parliament had technically complied with the required manner-and-form procedure by using a joint sitting and securing the necessary two-thirds majority, even though that majority had been achieved through Senate enlargement. Once those formal steps were satisfied, the Court refused to examine political motive or substantive injustice.

## **Judgment**

The court upheld the legislation and amendments.

## **Constitutional significance**

Collins is crucial because it shows the ultimate weakness of manner-and-form review under parliamentary sovereignty. Procedural review could delay constitutional abuse, but it could not prevent it once Parliament mastered the formal procedural steps. If Parliament followed the required procedure, courts could not examine the substance of the law, the political motive behind it or its racially oppressive impact.

## **Exam use**

Use Collins for:

limits of judicial review;

procedural compliance;

no inquiry into political motive;

no substantive review;

court subordination under parliamentary sovereignty.

### **One-sentence case memory**

**Collins = once Parliament followed formal procedure, courts could not question the substance, motive or injustice of the law.**

## **H. Harris I vs Harris II vs Collins: do not confuse them**

This is the exam danger zone.

| **Case** | **What happened** | **Court’s answer** | **Core principle** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Harris I** | Parliament removed voters from the common roll without following entrenched procedure. | Invalid. | Parliament must comply with manner and form requirements. |
| **Harris II** | Parliament created the High Court of Parliament to overturn the Appellate Division. | Invalid. | Parliament cannot turn itself into a court or retroactively set aside court decisions. |
| **Collins** | Parliament expanded the Senate, secured the majority, and followed formal procedure. | Valid. | Once formal procedure is followed, courts will not review motive or substance. |

The A1 feedback specifically warns that students lost marks by confusing Harris II and Collins. Harris II is about the **High Court of Parliament Act** and judicial independence; Collins is about the **enlargement of the Senate** and Parliament complying with formal procedure despite improper political motives.

## **I. What the three cases collectively show**

Together, Harris I, Harris II and Collins show the limited nature of judicial review in the old order.

They show that courts could:

enforce entrenched procedural requirements;

prevent Parliament from turning itself into a court;

protect some basic institutional boundaries.

But they could not:

invalidate legislation because it violated equality or dignity;

review the substance of apartheid legislation;

invalidate laws merely because Parliament’s motive was racially oppressive;

protect rights through a justiciable Bill of Rights;

treat the Constitution as supreme over Parliament.

The lecturer’s feedback says the key thematic elements are parliamentary sovereignty, subordination of the courts, and the inability of courts to review the substance of legislation, focusing instead on limited procedural aspects.

### **Exam sentence**

The 1950s cases show that the courts had enough power to insist on procedure, but not enough power to prevent Parliament from using law as an instrument of racial exclusion.

## **J. Why this created a constitutional crisis**

The crisis was not only legal. It was also political and moral.

The apartheid government wanted to remove vulnerable voters from the common roll. The courts briefly blocked this in Harris I by enforcing procedure. Parliament then attempted to bypass the courts through the High Court of Parliament Act, which failed in Harris II. Finally, Parliament succeeded through the Collins route by manipulating the Senate and then complying with formal procedure.

This revealed the weakness of parliamentary sovereignty:

Procedure could slow Parliament down, but not stop racially oppressive law.

Courts could not review substantive injustice.

Parliament could eventually achieve racial disenfranchisement if it followed the formal steps.

Rights had no strong entrenched protection.

Judicial review lacked the force of constitutional supremacy.

The revision lecture’s summary captures this: **Harris I = Parliament must follow the rules; Harris II = Parliament cannot turn itself into a court; Collins = if Parliament follows the rules, courts cannot question what it does**.

## **K. Link to constitutional supremacy**

The later move to constitutional supremacy directly responds to this weakness.

Under constitutional supremacy:

1. the Constitution is the highest law;
2. legislation must comply with the Constitution;
3. rights are entrenched and justiciable;
4. courts can review law and conduct for constitutional validity;
5. Parliament is no longer legally unlimited.

The Theme 3 slides show that the Interim Constitution brought fundamental changes, including the shift from parliamentary sovereignty to constitutional supremacy, the creation of a new independent Constitutional Court, a justiciable Bill of Rights, a certification mechanism, multi-party democracy and separation of powers.

The safest precise wording is:

Parliamentary sovereignty was abolished when the **Interim Constitution of 1993** took effect. The **1996 Final Constitution** confirmed and deepened constitutional supremacy.

This avoids the imprecise “1993 or 1996” wording that caused problems in the Theme 4 audit.

## **L. Conclusion**

High-value theoretical link: This old-order system reflects what Mureinik later described as a culture of authority: law demanded obedience because it came from Parliament, not because public power had to justify itself through rights and reasons.

Parliamentary sovereignty in pre-1994 South Africa meant that Parliament had the final say over legislation and courts could not invalidate laws merely because they were unjust or rights-violating. The Harris and Collins cases reveal both the limited power and ultimate weakness of judicial review under that system. Harris I shows that courts could enforce manner and form requirements. Harris II shows that Parliament could not turn itself into a court. Collins shows that once Parliament complied with formal procedure, courts would not question the substance or motive of legislation. This constitutional crisis exposed why South Africa needed constitutional supremacy, a justiciable Bill of Rights, an independent Constitutional Court and stronger judicial review.

# **4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer**

## **A. Introduction: resistance as constitutional imagination**

The liberation struggle must not be treated only as background history. It is part of South Africa’s constitutional development because resistance movements did not merely oppose apartheid; they imagined a different constitutional order. They challenged racial exclusion, parliamentary supremacy, authoritarian law, unequal citizenship and the use of law as an instrument of repression.

This matters because the 1996 Constitution did not appear from nowhere. Its commitments to dignity, equality, freedom, universal suffrage, non-racialism, non-sexism, rights and socio-economic transformation were shaped by decades of resistance, constitutional imagination and struggle.

The lecturer’s slides describe this historical movement as part of South Africa’s “constitutional revolution”: before 1994 there was parliamentary sovereignty, racial exclusion from citizenship, no entrenched Bill of Rights and an authoritarian security state; after 1994 there was constitutional supremacy, universal suffrage and a justiciable Bill of Rights.

## **B. Defiance Campaign: resisting legality without justice**

The **Defiance Campaign** showed that a law can be legally valid but morally unjust. The campaign involved volunteers deliberately defying apartheid laws in order to expose their injustice. The lecture notes that the first group of volunteers included Nelson Mandela, Yusuf Dadoo, Moses Kotane and Walter Sisulu, and that their goal was to defy apartheid laws in Johannesburg and other major cities, be arrested, show the unjustness of those laws, and ultimately change or abolish them.

The campaign was not immediately successful in changing the laws, but it was successful in awareness-building. It also led to international recognition, including a United Nations Commission investigating unjust racial policies. Importantly, it marked the beginning of non-racial cooperation in resistance, later cemented by the Congress Alliance.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Defiance Campaign shows the difference between **legality** and **legitimacy**. Apartheid laws were formally valid under parliamentary sovereignty, but they lacked moral and democratic legitimacy because they were imposed by a racially exclusive state.

### **Exam sentence**

The Defiance Campaign is constitutionally significant because it exposed the inadequacy of formal legality: law cannot claim legitimate authority merely because Parliament enacted it if the people governed by it are excluded from political power and treated unequally.

## **C. Congress Alliance: non-racial constitutional cooperation**

The **Congress Alliance** was a coalition of anti-apartheid organisations formed in the early 1950s to coordinate resistance against apartheid laws. It included the African National Congress, South African Coloured Peoples Organization, Congress of Democrats, South African Congress of Trade Unions and others.

Its significance is that it was explicitly **non-racial** at a time when apartheid enforced racial division. It developed a shared constitutional vision of equality and citizenship and organised the **Congress of the People** in 1955.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Congress Alliance matters because it anticipated core values later entrenched in the Constitution, especially:

* non-racialism;
* equality;
* shared citizenship;
* democratic participation;
* social justice.

### **Exam sentence**

The Congress Alliance challenged apartheid’s racial constitutional order by developing a non-racial vision of citizenship before formal constitutional democracy existed.

## **D. Congress of the People: participatory constitutional imagination**

The **Congress of the People** was held in Kliptown near Johannesburg on **26 June 1955**. About **3000 delegates** from across South Africa gathered. The process began with a mass campaign asking ordinary people what kind of South Africa they wanted, and it culminated in the adoption of the **Freedom Charter**.

This is very important for constitutional law because it shows that constitutional ideas were being generated through popular participation long before the formal constitutional negotiations of the 1990s.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Congress of the People matters because it was a form of **popular constitutional imagination**. Ordinary people were asked to participate in articulating the values of a future South Africa.

### **Exam sentence**

The Congress of the People is important because it shows that South Africa’s later constitutional democracy was preceded by a participatory liberation vision, not only by elite negotiation.

## **E. Freedom Charter: normative precursor to the Final Constitution**

The **Freedom Charter** is one of the most important historical documents for Theme 3. It declared that **South Africa belongs to all who live in it** and envisioned equality, shared citizenship and socio-economic rights. The lecture describes it as the normative precursor to the 1996 Final Constitution.

Its key principles included:

universal suffrage and democratic government;

equality before the law;

human rights and freedoms, including speech, assembly and movement;

redistribution of land and economic opportunity;

access to housing, education and work.

The lecture also stresses that although the Freedom Charter was **not legally binding**, it became a foundational ideological document for the liberation movement.

Woolman and Swanepoel make the same point more academically: South Africa’s Final Constitution has a history, and an important part of that history is the adoption of the Freedom Charter. They specifically say that the Final Constitution owes a debt to the Freedom Charter as a founding document of South Africa’s liberation movements.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Freedom Charter is not a statute and not a court judgment. Its importance is **normative and historical**. It gave content to the liberation movement’s constitutional vision and anticipated many themes later found in the Constitution:

| **Freedom Charter idea** | **Later constitutional connection** |
| --- | --- |
| “South Africa belongs to all who live in it” | Preamble: South Africa belongs to all who live in it, united in diversity |
| Universal suffrage | Constitutional democracy and political rights |
| Equality before the law | Equality, non-racialism and non-sexism |
| Speech, assembly and movement | Civil and political rights |
| Housing, education and work | Socio-economic rights and transformation |
| Land and economic opportunity | Redress and substantive equality |

### **Exam sentence**

The Freedom Charter was not legally binding, but it was constitutionally significant because it articulated a liberation vision of equal citizenship, democracy, human rights and socio-economic justice that later influenced the values and structure of the Final Constitution.

## **F. Sharpeville: state violence and the need for rights-limited power**

The **Sharpeville Massacre** is a constitutional turning point because it exposed the violent nature of apartheid legality. The road to Sharpeville began with the anti-pass campaign. In March 1960, Robert Sobukwe and the PAC announced a mass pass-law protest, and PAC activists called on Black South Africans to leave their pass books at home and voluntarily present themselves at police stations for arrest. The aim was to overwhelm the apartheid system and challenge pass laws directly.

On **21 March 1960**, thousands marched peacefully to the Sharpeville police station without passbooks. Police opened fire on the crowd. The lecture records that **69 people were killed** and hundreds were injured.

The government response intensified repression. A state of emergency was declared on 30 March 1960, used to suppress protest and detain thousands of people. The ANC and PAC were banned under the Unlawful Organisations Act. The lecture states that this marked a shift to a more violent and repressive phase of apartheid and led to international criticism.

### **Constitutional significance**

Sharpeville shows why the later Constitution needed:

1. entrenched rights;
2. freedom of assembly;
3. freedom of expression;
4. protection against arbitrary detention;
5. limits on emergency powers;
6. accountability for state violence;
7. constitutional supremacy over security-state power.

### **Exam sentence**

Sharpeville is constitutionally significant because it showed that without entrenched rights and limits on public power, the state could respond to peaceful protest with violence, emergency rule and political banning while remaining formally within apartheid legality.

## **G. 1961 Constitution: republic without democratic transformation**

After Sharpeville, South Africa’s constitutional order did not become democratic. The **1961 Constitution** removed the British monarch as head of state and made South Africa a republic. The lecture notes that a white-only referendum approved the move to become a republic and that the 1961 Constitution retained the parliamentary system from the 1910 Constitution. It reinforced white minority dominance and continued to exclude Black South Africans from the political process.

**Accuracy note:** the slide appears to contain a typo saying “32 May 1961”. The historically accepted date for South Africa becoming a republic is **31 May 1961**. The legal point for this essay is not the exact date but the constitutional continuity: the republic retained parliamentary structures and racial exclusion.

### **Constitutional significance**

The 1961 Constitution shows that formal constitutional change does not necessarily produce democratic transformation. South Africa became a republic, but not a constitutional democracy.

### **Exam sentence**

The 1961 Constitution changed the symbolic head of state, but it did not cure the deeper constitutional problem of racial exclusion and parliamentary supremacy.

## **H. Rivonia Trial: law, justice and political legitimacy**

Authority precision: when mentioning Rivonia, name the repressive statutes if space allows: the General Law Amendment Act 76 of 1962 (the Sabotage Act) and the Suppression of Communism Act 44 of 1950. This strengthens the point that apartheid used formally valid law to criminalise resistance.

The **Rivonia Trial** is one of the strongest examples of the difference between law and justice under apartheid.

The lecture explains that the trial was held from **1963–1964** and resulted in life imprisonment for Nelson Mandela and other anti-apartheid leaders. It became a historical turning point and drew international scrutiny to apartheid.

The trialists were charged under laws designed to criminalise resistance to racial oppression. The lecture says the case demonstrates how a legal system can be weaponised by those in power against the very people it should protect, and why constitutions must entrench rights that constrain legislative power, not merely procedural rules.

The People’s Law Journal reading gives more detail: by the time the Rivonia Trial began on **26 November 1963**, the apartheid state had created an intimidating legal framework to stamp out political dissent. The trialists were charged under laws including the **General Law Amendment Act 1962**, commonly known as the Sabotage Act, the Suppression of Communism Act and the Criminal Law Amendment Act.

Rather than mount only a technical criminal defence, the accused put apartheid itself on trial. The lecture explains that Mandela’s “I am prepared to die” statement reframed the proceedings as a question of political legitimacy: can a government that excludes the majority from political participation claim the moral authority to prosecute them for defying its laws?

### **Constitutional significance**

Rivonia matters because it shows:

law can be used to repress rather than protect;

formal legality is not the same as justice;

political legitimacy depends on democratic inclusion;

rights must be entrenched and enforceable;

courts in authoritarian systems may be constrained by unjust legal frameworks;

constitutional design after apartheid had to respond to the abuse of criminal law and emergency powers.

The lecture summarises Rivonia as a foundational case study in the difference between law and justice, the role of courts in authoritarian systems, why entrenched constitutional rights matter, and how historical abuse shapes constitutional design.

### **Exam sentence**

Rivonia shows that apartheid’s legal system could prosecute resistance through valid-looking statutes, but the trial exposed the deeper constitutional problem: a state that excludes the majority from political participation lacks democratic legitimacy when it criminalises their struggle for freedom.

## **I. 1983 Constitution: limited reform without equal citizenship**

The **1983 Constitution** is important because it shows that apartheid attempted reform without giving up racial domination. The lecture describes it as a key reform under the apartheid regime intended to entrench apartheid rule while responding to internal protest and international pressure. It created the **Tricameral Parliament**, with separate houses for white, Coloured and Indian population groups, while completely excluding the Black African majority from central government representation.

The 1983 system also created “own affairs” and “general affairs” categories, abolished the office of Prime Minister, and created an executive State President.

### **Constitutional significance**

The 1983 Constitution shows that partial inclusion is not constitutional democracy. It widened participation for some groups while preserving white control and excluding the majority.

### **Exam sentence**

The 1983 Constitution illustrates the failure of limited racial reform: it created institutional change without universal citizenship, equal political participation or genuine constitutional democracy.

## **J. ANC Constitutional Guidelines: from resistance to constitutional design**

By the late 1980s, the liberation movement’s constitutional vision became more formal. The **ANC Constitutional Guidelines for a Democratic South Africa** set out the ANC’s plan for a future South Africa and invited people and organisations to discuss it. The document explained that people in South Africa could be part of shaping the future.

The Guidelines were based on democracy, an end to racism and an end to sexism. They spoke about creating a society where people could live as free citizens in a free country, have a say at all levels of government, and address issues such as land, workers, women and family.

Woolman and Swanepoel explain that in 1988 the ANC published the **Constitutional Guidelines for a Democratic South Africa** as its first public expression of a desire to work towards a negotiated settlement, and that the ANC committed itself to a justiciable Bill of Rights and constitutionalism generally.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Guidelines matter because they show the move from resistance to constitutional design. They connect the Freedom Charter’s broad ideals to a more concrete constitutional programme based on:

democracy;

non-racism;

non-sexism;

participation;

rights;

land reform;

workers’ rights;

women’s equality;

social justice;

constitutionalism.

### **Exam sentence**

The ANC Constitutional Guidelines are significant because they translated liberation ideals into a more concrete constitutional vision, including democracy, non-racism, non-sexism, participation, a justiciable Bill of Rights and constitutionalism.

# **4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer**

## **A. Introduction: negotiation as constitutional design**

South Africa’s transition was not a simple revolution where one side defeated the other and imposed a new constitutional order. It was a **negotiated constitutional revolution**. This means the transition was radical in substance because it moved South Africa from parliamentary sovereignty, racial exclusion and authoritarian government to constitutional supremacy, universal suffrage and a justiciable Bill of Rights. But it was negotiated in form because the new order emerged through bargaining, compromise, interim arrangements and certification.

The central problem was legitimacy. The apartheid government still controlled the state, but lacked moral and democratic legitimacy. The liberation movements had democratic and moral legitimacy among oppressed communities, but had not yet won a formal national election. This meant the transition had to create a process that was stable enough to govern immediately, but democratic enough to produce a legitimate final Constitution.

## **B. CODESA I and the legitimacy problem**

One of the major issues facing **CODESA I** was legitimacy. The negotiators had not been elected through free and verifiable elections, so they did not have the democratic mandate to write the final Constitution. The slides make this point directly: because the negotiators were unelected, a properly elected body was required for the drafting of any final Constitution.

This created a constitutional dilemma:

| **Problem** | **Why it mattered** |
| --- | --- |
| **Unelected negotiators** | They could negotiate a transition, but lacked democratic authority to impose a permanent Constitution. |
| **Need for immediate stability** | South Africa needed a legal framework to govern the first democratic election and transition period. |
| **Need for democratic legitimacy** | The final Constitution had to be drafted by representatives elected by the people. |
| **Need for legal continuity** | The transition still had to use existing legal procedures to avoid state collapse. |

### **Exam sentence**

The legitimacy problem at CODESA was that unelected negotiators could design a pathway to democracy, but could not claim full democratic authority to write South Africa’s permanent constitutional settlement.

## **C. CODESA I: the central disagreement**

CODESA I also exposed a major disagreement about how decisions would be made in the new constitutional order.

The apartheid government wanted:

power-sharing agreements;

strong minority protections;

supermajority requirements for constitutional decisions;

mechanisms that would give minority parties something close to veto power.

The ANC argued for:

* majority rule based on democratic election;
* a Constitution drafted by an elected body;
* equal political participation for all citizens.

This was not merely a political disagreement. It was a constitutional disagreement about **the meaning of democracy after apartheid**.

### **NP position**

The National Party feared that simple majority rule would allow the ANC to dominate the future constitutional order. It therefore wanted strong minority protections and high voting thresholds to protect white and minority interests.

### **ANC position**

The ANC argued that a democratic South Africa had to be based on majority rule, universal suffrage and equal citizenship. From this perspective, giving the minority veto power would reproduce apartheid’s logic of minority control.

### **Exam sentence**

The CODESA dispute was a clash between minority-security constitutionalism and majority-rule constitutionalism.

## **D. Why CODESA stalled**

The disagreement over legitimacy and decision-making meant that CODESA could not easily produce a final settlement. The parties could not agree on the voting thresholds for constitutional decisions or the structure of transitional government.

The slides state that CODESA II, the second round of negotiations in 1992, failed because parties could not agree on voting thresholds for constitutional decisions and the structure of the transitional government, leading to a temporary collapse of negotiations in mid-1992.

### **Exam sentence**

CODESA failed because it could not reconcile the NP’s demand for minority-protective power-sharing with the ANC’s demand for democratically legitimate majority rule.

## **E. Political violence and crisis during negotiations**

The early 1990s were not a peaceful transition. Negotiations occurred alongside significant political violence. The slides mention township violence, political assassinations such as Chris Hani, conflict between political organisations such as the Inkatha Freedom Party, and security force involvement in destabilisation, including Boipatong.

This violence mattered constitutionally because it created mistrust between negotiating parties and made the transition more urgent. Violence threatened to destroy the possibility of a peaceful constitutional settlement.

### **Constitutional significance**

Political violence showed that:

negotiations were fragile;

compromise was necessary to avoid civil conflict;

the new Constitution would need strong rights and accountability protections;

the transition needed a binding legal framework;

democratic legitimacy had to be achieved without allowing the process to collapse.

### **Exam sentence**

Political violence did not merely form the background to negotiations; it shaped the urgency and content of constitutional compromise.

## **F. Boipatong Massacre: crisis and turning point**

The **Boipatong Massacre** occurred on **17 June 1992**. The slides state that more than 40 residents of Boipatong township were killed, that the attack was linked to hostel dwellers aligned with the Inkatha Freedom Party, and that allegations emerged that apartheid security forces had been involved. The massacre shocked the country and led the ANC to withdraw from negotiations.

The lecture gives an important interpretive point:

Boipatong did not end the possibility of negotiation — it clarified for both sides that the cost of failure was higher than the cost of compromise.

This is a beautiful exam sentence because it explains why violence both disrupted and intensified the need for negotiation.

### **Constitutional significance**

Boipatong matters because it:

deepened mistrust;

weakened the NP’s legitimacy;

exposed the danger of uncontrolled state/security force power;

contributed to the collapse of negotiations;

made compromise more urgent;

showed why a rights-based, accountable constitutional order was necessary.

### **Exam sentence**

Boipatong transformed the negotiations by showing that failure to compromise could lead to greater violence and instability than the compromises required to reach a constitutional settlement.

## **G. The two-stage transition solution**

Formal title precision: the “Interim Constitution” is the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 200 of 1993. It was finalised through the MPNF and passed through the old legal order to preserve legal continuity while enabling a radical constitutional break.

The two-stage transition was the key compromise.

The negotiators devised a model with two stages:

| **Stage** | **What happened** | **Why it mattered** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stage 1** | Unelected negotiating parties drafted and adopted the Interim Constitution. It governed the country after the first democratic election. | Created immediate legal stability and enabled the first democratic election. |
| **Stage 2** | A democratically elected Constitutional Assembly drafted the Final Constitution after the election. | Solved the legitimacy problem by ensuring the final Constitution came from elected representatives. |

The slides describe the two-stage transition as follows: Stage 1 involved unelected negotiating parties drafting and adopting the Interim Constitution (Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 200 of 1993), which governed the country after the first democratic election. Stage 2 required the National Assembly and Senate sitting jointly as the Constitutional Assembly to draft the Final Constitution after the election, subject to the 34 Constitutional Principles and Constitutional Court certification.

### **Why the two-stage model was brilliant**

It balanced competing needs:

| **Need** | **How the two-stage model responded** |
| --- | --- |
| **Stability** | The Interim Constitution governed during the transition. |
| **Democratic legitimacy** | The Final Constitution was drafted by an elected Constitutional Assembly. |
| **Minority protection** | The 34 Constitutional Principles bound the Final Constitution. |
| **Majority rule** | The first democratic election determined representation in the Constitutional Assembly. |
| **Legal certainty** | The Constitutional Court certified the Final Constitution against the Principles. |

### **Exam sentence**

The two-stage transition was the constitutional compromise that allowed unelected negotiators to create a temporary bridge to democracy while reserving final constitution-making authority for a democratically elected body.

## **H. MPNF: successor to CODESA**

After CODESA stalled, the **Multiparty Negotiating Forum** became the successor process. The slides describe the MPNF as the successor to CODESA between 1993 and 1994. It included **26 political parties and groups**, firmly established the two-stage transitional government, set the date for the first democratic election as **27 April 1994**, and finalised the Interim Constitution, which was adopted on **18 November 1993**.

The slides also highlight the negotiations of **Roelf Meyer** for the NP and **Cyril Ramaphosa** for the ANC as part of the successful finalisation of the Interim Constitution.

### **Constitutional significance**

The MPNF matters because it moved the transition from failed talks to workable constitutional design. It did not produce the permanent Constitution, but it produced the legal structure that made the first democratic election and later Final Constitution possible.

### **Exam sentence**

The MPNF transformed the failed CODESA process into a workable constitutional pathway by finalising the Interim Constitution and setting the election date that would legitimate the final constitution-making process.

## **I. Interim Constitution as negotiated bridge**

The **Interim Constitution** was finalised by the unelected MPNF before the first democratic election. It was adopted late in 1993 and became binding after **27 April 1994**, the date of the first democratic election. It was adopted using the procedures of the 1983 Constitution.

This is constitutionally important because it shows both continuity and rupture:

| **Continuity** | **Rupture** |
| --- | --- |
| It was adopted through procedures of the existing legal order. | It replaced parliamentary sovereignty with constitutional supremacy. |
| It was negotiated by unelected parties. | It enabled the first universal democratic election. |
| It was interim, not final. | It created binding Constitutional Principles for the Final Constitution. |

The Interim Constitution was therefore a bridge between apartheid legality and democratic constitutionalism.

## **J. Constitutional Principles as negotiated safeguards**

The 34 Constitutional Principles were crucial because they solved the fear that the elected majority might later impose a Constitution without respecting negotiated safeguards.

The slides define the Constitutional Principles as **benchmarks** that the Final Constitution had to meet. They would be used by the Constitutional Assembly when drafting the Final Constitution, and the newly established Constitutional Court would evaluate and certify the Final Constitution using those Principles. If the Final Constitution did not comply, the Court could not certify it and it would have to be redrafted.

Key principles included:

* constitutional democracy based on constitutional supremacy and an independent judiciary;
* universal adult suffrage and regular elections;
* separation of powers and checks and balances;
* a justiciable Bill of Rights;
* one sovereign state structured at national, provincial and local levels;
* proportional representation and common voters roll;
* recognition of traditional leadership and customary law;
* protection of the Constitution from amendment except through special processes.

### **Constitutional significance**

The Principles were not ordinary political promises. They were legally binding standards for the Final Constitution.

### **Exam sentence**

The Constitutional Principles were the legal mechanism that allowed the negotiated settlement to bind the later democratic constitution-making process without allowing unelected negotiators to write the permanent Constitution themselves.

## **K. Critical evaluation: was the negotiated settlement democratic?**

A high-mark answer should evaluate the democratic tension.

### **Argument that it was not fully democratic**

The early negotiation process was not fully democratic because CODESA and the MPNF were not elected through universal suffrage. The negotiators lacked the democratic mandate to write a permanent Constitution.

### **Argument that it became democratically legitimate**

However, the two-stage transition responded to this problem. The Interim Constitution was temporary. The Final Constitution had to be drafted by a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly after the first democratic election. The Constitutional Principles ensured that the final text complied with the negotiated framework, while the certification process gave legal certainty.

### **Balanced conclusion**

The negotiated settlement was therefore not pure direct democracy, but it was a **democratically controlled transition**. It used negotiation to avoid collapse, election to create legitimacy, and certification to ensure constitutional compliance.

### **Exam sentence**

The two-stage transition converted an initially elite-negotiated process into a democratically authorised and legally controlled constitution-making process.

## **L. Conclusion**

CODESA and the MPNF show why South Africa’s constitutional transition was both negotiated and revolutionary. CODESA failed because of disputes over legitimacy, minority protections, majority rule, voting thresholds and transitional government. Political violence, especially Boipatong, deepened mistrust but also made compromise urgent. The two-stage transition solved the core problem by allowing unelected negotiators to adopt an Interim Constitution while reserving final constitution-making for a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly. The 34 Constitutional Principles and certification mechanism ensured that the Final Constitution would remain faithful to the negotiated settlement. This is why South Africa’s transition can be described as a negotiated constitutional revolution.

7. recognition of traditional leadership and customary law;
8. protection of the Constitution from amendment except through special processes.

### **Exam sentence**

The Constitutional Principles were the negotiated constitutional DNA of the Final Constitution: they did not write the final text, but they determined the minimum features it had to contain.

## **C. Why were the Principles needed?**

The Principles were needed because the transition had to solve two problems at the same time.

## **1. The legitimacy problem**

The Interim Constitution was negotiated before the first democratic election by unelected parties. Those negotiators could create a temporary framework, but they could not claim full democratic authority to write South Africa’s permanent Constitution.

## **2. The fear of majoritarian domination**

After the first election, the ANC was expected to be the dominant political force. Minority parties feared that an elected majority could draft a Constitution that ignored negotiated safeguards, minority interests, provincial powers, language/cultural concerns, independent institutions or rights protections.

The Constitutional Principles solved this by allowing:

democratic drafting by the elected Constitutional Assembly; but

legal control through agreed principles and court certification.

### **Exam sentence**

The Principles solved the transition’s legitimacy dilemma by combining elected constitution-making with negotiated constitutional safeguards.

## **D. The Constitutional Assembly and democratic drafting**

The Final Constitution was not drafted by CODESA or the MPNF. It was drafted by the **Constitutional Assembly**, formed after the first democratic election.

Under **s 68(1)** of the Interim Constitution, the **National Assembly and Senate sitting jointly** formed the Constitutional Assembly. The Assembly had to draft and adopt the new constitutional text according to the Interim Constitution’s rules.

This detail matters because some students incorrectly say the NCOP formed part of the Constitutional Assembly. That is inaccurate. The NCOP belongs to the 1996 Constitution. Under the Interim Constitution process, the correct term is **Senate**.

### **Exam sentence**

The Constitutional Assembly gave the Final Constitution democratic legitimacy because it was made up of representatives chosen in South Africa’s first democratic election.

## **E. Section 71: certification as a legal gatekeeper**

Statutory map: s 68(1) created the Constitutional Assembly as the National Assembly and Senate sitting jointly; s 71(1) required the new text to comply with the 34 Constitutional Principles; s 71(2) stated that the text had no force or effect unless certified; and s 71(3) made certification final and binding. Section 73 then regulated adoption, including the two-year deadline, two-thirds majority and fallback process.

Section **71** of the Interim Constitution is the core provision.

Section **71(1)** required the new constitutional text to comply with the Constitutional Principles in Schedule 4 and to be passed in accordance with the Interim Constitution.

Section **71(2)** provided that the new constitutional text, or any provision of it, would have **no force or effect** unless the Constitutional Court certified that all provisions complied with the Constitutional Principles.

Section **71(3)** made the Court’s certification decision **final and binding**, and no court could later enquire into or pronounce upon the validity of the text or any provision once certified.

### **Exam sentence**

Certification was the legal gatekeeper: without Constitutional Court certification, the Final Constitution had no force and effect.

## **F. Section 73: adoption rules and fallback mechanisms**

Section **73** of the Interim Constitution regulated adoption of the new constitutional text.

Important points:

| **Rule** | **Explanation** |
| --- | --- |
| **Two-year deadline** | The Constitutional Assembly had to pass the new text within two years of the first sitting of the National Assembly. |
| **Two-thirds majority** | Adoption required support of at least two-thirds of all members of the Constitutional Assembly. |
| **Provincial provisions** | Text relating to provincial boundaries, powers and functions also required two-thirds approval of all Senate members. |
| **Expert panel fallback** | If the text failed to get two-thirds but had majority support, it could be referred to a panel of constitutional experts for advice. |
| **Referendum fallback** | A text supported by majority but not two-thirds could, after certification, be put to a national referendum and become the Constitution if approved by at least 60% of votes cast. |
| **This option was not used** | The Final Constitution was ultimately adopted through the Constitutional Assembly route. |

The Interim Constitution’s text confirms the two-year deadline, two-thirds requirement, and referendum route requiring at least **60% of votes cast** if the referendum process was triggered.

### **Exam sentence**

Section 73 shows that the Final Constitution had to be democratically adopted, but within a carefully structured and legally controlled process.

## **G. Certification and the “solemn pact”**

Woolman and Swanepoel explain that the Constitutional Court treated certification as a legal duty arising from the Interim Constitution. The Court emphasised that the Interim Constitution created a **“solemn pact”** between the negotiating parties to create a Final Constitution that adhered to the specified Constitutional Principles.

This phrase is extremely useful in an exam.

It means the Principles were not optional. They were the binding terms of the negotiated transition. Even if the Constitutional Assembly had strong democratic support, the Court had to send the text back if it did not comply.

### **Exam sentence**

The Constitutional Principles were a solemn pact: they bound the democratic constitution-making process because they were the legal price of the negotiated transition.

## **H. The Constitutional Court’s role: legal, not political**

A major 90% point is that the Constitutional Court’s certification role was **legal**, not political.

Woolman and Swanepoel explain that the Court said it had a **judicial, not political mandate**. The wisdom or correctness of the New Text was left to the Constitutional Assembly. The Court had no power and no right to express a view on the political choices made by the Constitutional Assembly. Its role was only to decide whether the text complied with the Constitutional Principles.

This prevents a dangerous mistake: do **not** say the Court rejected the first text because it disliked the political choices. It rejected the text only where it found non-compliance with the Principles.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court’s role was not to choose the best Constitution, but to decide whether the Constitution chosen by the elected Assembly complied with the agreed Principles.

## **I. Holistic interpretation, not technical rigidity**

The Theme 3 lecture says that in the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Court determined that the Constitutional Principles had to be interpreted **holistically** and not in a technically rigid way, to ensure citizens could exercise their fundamental rights and freedoms.

This matters because the Principles were broad and sometimes open-textured. The Court had to interpret them carefully, without turning certification into a mechanical technical exercise.

### **Exam sentence**

Certification required careful constitutional interpretation: the Principles had to be applied legally, but holistically and without technical rigidity.

## **J. First Certification Judgment: the first text was not certified**

The Constitutional Assembly adopted a first draft of the Final Constitution and submitted it to the Constitutional Court. The Theme 3 slides record that the first draft was submitted on **10 May 1996** and was not certified because the Court found several provisions did not align with the Constitutional Principles.

The First Certification Judgment, formally **Ex parte Chairperson of the Constitutional Assembly: In re Certification of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996** 1996 (4) SA 744 (CC), is the key case.

The lecture identifies three major objection categories:

inclusion of socio-economic rights in a justiciable Bill of Rights;

lack of complete separation between executive and legislature;

other instances of non-compliance with Constitutional Principles.

The Court rejected some objections but upheld others. This is important: the first draft did not fail completely. The Court was careful to say that most of the text complied, but certain provisions did not.

Woolman and Swanepoel emphasise how extraordinary this was: a judicial body declared a constitution “unconstitutional” even though the text had been adopted by 86% of the democratically elected Constitutional Assembly.

### **Exam sentence**

The First Certification Judgment was extraordinary because the Constitutional Court refused to certify a constitutional text adopted by an overwhelming democratic majority, not for political reasons, but because parts of it failed to comply with the solemn pact contained in the Constitutional Principles.

## **K. Why this did not undermine democracy**

At first, certification may look counter-majoritarian: unelected judges could prevent a Constitution adopted by elected representatives from coming into force.

But the better answer is that certification **protected** the negotiated democratic process because the elected Constitutional Assembly was never free to ignore the Principles. Those Principles were part of the Interim Constitution and were accepted as the legal framework for drafting the Final Constitution.

Certification therefore:

gave legal certainty;

protected negotiated safeguards;

prevented opportunistic constitution-making;

ensured compliance with supremacy, rights, democracy and separation of powers;

strengthened public confidence in the Final Constitution.

### **Exam sentence**

Certification did not replace democracy; it enforced the legal conditions under which democratic constitution-making had been authorised.

## **L. Second Certification Judgment: final legal hurdle**

After the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Assembly amended the text. The Second Certification Judgment was delivered on **4 December 1996**. The Constitutional Court unanimously certified that the Amended Text complied with the 34 Constitutional Principles. The lecture describes this as the final legal hurdle for South Africa’s transition to full democracy.

Following certification, President Nelson Mandela signed the Constitution into law on **10 December 1996** in Sharpeville, and the Constitution took effect on **4 February 1997**, replacing the Interim Constitution.

### **Exam sentence**

The Second Certification Judgment completed the legal transition from negotiated interim constitutionalism to the Final Constitution.

The Final Constitution’s major doctrines were not accidental; they were the legal product of the Constitutional Principles and the certification process.

### **Strengths**

The certification mechanism:

# **4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer**

## **A. Introduction: what certification meant**

The certification process was the legal mechanism that made the negotiated transition enforceable. The Constitutional Assembly had democratic authority to draft the Final Constitution, but the text could not come into force unless the Constitutional Court certified that it complied with the **34 Constitutional Principles**.

The First Certification Judgment is therefore important because it shows that even the making of the Final Constitution was subject to law. The Court did not simply approve the text because it had political support. It tested the text against the Principles and refused certification where the New Text failed to comply.

The Theme 3 lecture records that the first draft of the Final Constitution was submitted to the Constitutional Court on **10 May 1996**, but was not certified because several provisions did not align with the Constitutional Principles.

## **B. The Court’s certification approach**

The Constitutional Court evaluated the text in two ways:

* **Structural enquiry:** did the basic structure and premises of the New Text accord with the Constitutional Principles?
* **Substantive enquiry:** did the contents of the provisions comply with the Constitutional Principles?

The lecture explains that if the basic structure had not aligned, the Court would not have needed to continue with the substantive enquiry. However, the structure did align, so the judgment dealt mostly with substantive objections.

The Court’s task was legal, not political. It did not ask whether the New Text was the best possible Constitution. It asked whether the New Text complied with the agreed Constitutional Principles. This distinction is important because it protects you from saying the Court “rewrote” or “politically rejected” the Constitution.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court’s certification role was not to choose the ideal Constitution, but to decide whether the Constitution chosen by the elected Constitutional Assembly complied with the binding Constitutional Principles.

## **C. Holistic interpretation of the Constitutional Principles**

The Court interpreted the Constitutional Principles **holistically** and not with technical rigidity. This meant the Court did not apply the Principles like narrow checklist items detached from the purpose of the transition. Instead, it considered whether the text as a whole allowed citizens to exercise fundamental rights and freedoms.

### **Exam sentence**

Certification required legal interpretation, but not mechanical literalism: the Principles had to be read holistically in light of the purpose of constitutional democracy.

## **D. Objection 1: socio-economic rights in a justiciable Bill of Rights**

Exam warning: do not say socio-economic rights caused non-certification. The Court rejected that objection. It accepted that socio-economic rights could form part of a justiciable Bill of Rights and that their budgetary implications did not automatically breach separation of powers.

One of the most important objections concerned the inclusion of **socio-economic rights** in the justiciable Bill of Rights.

The rights included, among others:

housing under **s 26**;

healthcare, food, water and social security under **s 27**;

basic education under **s 29**;

children’s rights under **s 28**.

There were three main objections.

## **E. Socio-economic rights objection 1: not “universally accepted” rights**

The first argument was that socio-economic rights should not be included in a justiciable Bill of Rights because they were not “universally accepted” fundamental rights in the same way as traditional civil and political rights.

The Court rejected this. The lecture explains that **CP II** allowed the inclusion of additional rights beyond universally accepted rights. The Constitution was not limited to traditional civil and political rights; it embraced a broader transformative vision of rights protection.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court rejected the idea that the Bill of Rights had to be limited to traditional civil and political rights; socio-economic rights were compatible with the broader transformative vision permitted by the Constitutional Principles.

## **F. Socio-economic rights objection 2: separation of powers and budgeting**

The second argument was that socio-economic rights would violate separation of powers because courts would interfere with budgeting and policy, which are usually matters for the executive and legislature.

The Court rejected this too. The lecture notes that courts already make orders with budgetary implications, such as legal aid and equality remedies, and socio-economic rights were not fundamentally different.

This is an important precision point: the Court did **not** say courts may freely run budgets or take over government policy. It said the mere fact that a right has budgetary implications does not make it non-justiciable or unconstitutional.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court accepted that socio-economic rights may have budgetary consequences, but held that budgetary consequences alone do not make a right non-justiciable or incompatible with separation of powers.

## **G. Socio-economic rights objection 3: non-justiciability**

The third argument was that courts could not enforce rights tied to resources.

The Court rejected the objection. The lecture says socio-economic rights are at least **partially justiciable**, and at minimum can be **negatively enforced**, meaning the state may not unjustifiably interfere with existing access to those rights.

The key takeaway from the lecture is:

socio-economic rights are constitutionally valid and enforceable;

their inclusion does not breach separation of powers;

courts play a legitimate role in their protection;

their inclusion was consistent with the Constitutional Principles.

### **Exam sentence**

The First Certification Judgment confirmed that socio-economic rights are justiciable, although their enforcement requires sensitivity to institutional competence and resources.

## **H. Objection 2: separation of powers between legislature and executive**

The second major objection concerned separation of powers. The challenge was based on **Constitutional Principle VI**, which required separation between the legislature, executive and judiciary, with checks and balances to ensure accountability.

The objectors argued that the New Text allowed members of the executive also to sit in the legislature. They said this weakened separation of powers, enhanced executive dominance and undermined democratic accountability.

The comparative argument was that countries such as the United States, France and the Netherlands used stricter models of separation, so South Africa’s model was defective.

## **I. The Court’s response: no single universal model**

The Court rejected the argument that CP VI required strict or complete separation between the executive and legislature.

The Court’s starting point was that there was no issue with separation involving the judiciary; the dispute concerned only executive-legislature overlap. The lecture explains the Court’s response:

there is no single model of separation of powers;

systems differ between countries;

separation is always partial, not absolute;

the core idea is balance between functional independence and checks and balances;

branches are partly interacting, not wholly disjointed.

South Africa deliberately adopted overlap between the legislature and executive. The executive being drawn from the legislature could enhance accountability, because Cabinet is accountable to Parliament under **s 92(2)**, Cabinet members must report to Parliament under **s 92(3)**, and Parliament can remove the President under **s 89**.

The Court also recognised that powers were formally separated in the constitutional design: legislative authority under **s 43**, executive authority under **ss 85 and 125**, and judicial authority under **s 165**. The final finding was that **CP VI did not require strict separation**, and the New Text’s hybrid model was constitutionally compliant.

### **Exam sentence**

The First Certification Judgment confirms that South Africa’s separation of powers is flexible and contextual: CP VI requires separation with checks and balances, not a rigid American-style separation.

## **J. Objection 3: immunising legislation from constitutional scrutiny**

The Court did, however, find real non-compliance in other parts of the New Text.

One problem was that some provisions attempted to shield ordinary legislation from constitutional review. For example, **NT 241(1)** provided that the Labour Relations Act would remain valid despite the Constitution. The problem was that this attempted to insulate ordinary legislation from constitutional scrutiny.

This violated the logic of constitutional supremacy. Under **CP IV**, the Constitution had to be the supreme law. If Parliament could protect ordinary legislation from constitutional review, then constitutional supremacy and justiciability would be undermined.

Woolman and Swanepoel also note that section 241(1) attempted to place the Labour Relations Act beyond constitutional scrutiny, which was impermissible because **CP IV** required constitutional supremacy and **CP II and CP VII** required a justiciable and enforceable Bill of Rights. They also note that a similar schedule provision attempted to place the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act beyond constitutional scrutiny.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court refused to certify provisions that attempted to place ordinary legislation beyond constitutional review because that would undermine constitutional supremacy and the justiciability of the Bill of Rights.

## **K. Objection 4: constitutional amendment procedures and Bill of Rights entrenchment**

Entrenchment precision: the problem was that the Bill of Rights did not receive sufficiently distinct protection from amendment when compared with ordinary constitutional provisions. CP II required entrenched and justiciable fundamental rights, and CP XV required special procedures and special majorities for constitutional amendment.

Another important non-compliance issue concerned amendment of the Constitution.

**CP XV** required **special majorities and special procedures** for constitutional amendments. The purpose was to protect the Constitution from rapid or opportunistic amendment. The New Text provided supermajorities, such as two-thirds of the National Assembly for general amendments and 75% for foundational provisions, but it did not provide distinct special procedures. The lecture says the Court reasoned that “special procedures” must mean more than higher voting thresholds; it required additional deliberative and procedural safeguards.

The Bill of Rights was also not sufficiently entrenched. **CP II** required rights to be entrenched and justiciable. The objection was that the Bill of Rights was not more protected than other constitutional provisions. The Court stated that entrenchment requires a higher level of protection than ordinary constitutional provisions, and the New Text did not sufficiently protect the Bill of Rights because it only required a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly.

Woolman and Swanepoel confirm this: the New Text’s amendment provision failed to comply with **CP XV**, which required special procedures as well as special majorities, and also failed to comply with **CP II** because it did not give the Bill of Rights greater protection from amendment than the rest of the Constitution.

### **Exam sentence**

The Court required both numerical and procedural entrenchment: a supreme Constitution must be harder to amend than ordinary law, and the Bill of Rights must receive special protection.

## **L. Other grounds of non-certification**

### **Exam sentence**

The First Certification Judgment was not a general political rejection of the Constitution; it identified specific legal defects in the text’s compliance with the Constitutional Principles.

## **M. Why the First Certification Judgment mattered**

The First Certification Judgment mattered because it proved that certification was real. The Court did not rubber-stamp the New Text. It enforced the negotiated constitutional pact even against a text adopted by the elected Constitutional Assembly.

Woolman and Swanepoel describe this as extraordinary: a judicial body declared a constitutional text “unconstitutional” even though it had been adopted by 86% of the democratically elected Constitutional Assembly.

The case showed:

Constitutional Principles were binding;

constitutional supremacy was real;

the Bill of Rights had to be genuinely entrenched and justiciable;

ordinary legislation could not be immunised from review;

separation of powers did not require strict separation;

the Court could enforce the settlement while respecting democratic choices.

### **Exam sentence**

The First Certification Judgment showed that South Africa’s Constitution was born under law: even the elected constitution-making body had to comply with the binding legal conditions of the transition.

## **N. Critical nuance: the Court’s role was legal, but not value-neutral**

A strong answer should add nuance.

The Court said its role was legal, not political. However, Woolman and Swanepoel note that the Court’s methodology created interpretive difficulties because future interpretations of the Final Constitution had to be consistent with the Court’s certification interpretations, and the Court sometimes placed its own gloss on provisions that were susceptible to multiple interpretations. They argue that despite the Court’s claim that its role was purely legal, some interpretations reflected a preferred political reading of the text.

This does **not** mean the Court acted illegitimately. It means certification involved constitutional interpretation, and constitutional interpretation can never be entirely mechanical.

### **Exam sentence**

The certification role was formally legal, but it inevitably involved constitutional judgment because the Court had to interpret broad Constitutional Principles and apply them to contested constitutional choices.

## **O. Second Certification Judgment: final legal hurdle**

After the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Assembly amended the text. The Second Certification Judgment was delivered on **4 December 1996**. The Constitutional Court unanimously certified that the Amended Text complied with the 34 Constitutional Principles, and the judgment focused on confirming that the shortcomings identified in the First Certification Judgment had been sufficiently addressed.

The lecture describes the Second Certification Judgment as the final legal hurdle for South Africa’s transition to full democracy. After certification, President Nelson Mandela signed the Constitution into law on **10 December 1996** in Sharpeville. The 1996 Constitution took effect on **4 February 1997**, replacing the 1993 Interim Constitution.

Woolman and Swanepoel add that although the entire constitution-making process could theoretically have reopened after non-certification, the major parties confined themselves to amendments designed to satisfy the Court’s concerns.

### **Exam sentence**

The Second Certification Judgment completed the transition by confirming that the Amended Text had cured the defects identified in the First Certification Judgment.

## **P. Conclusion**

The First and Second Certification Judgments show the legal discipline behind South Africa’s negotiated constitutional revolution. The First Certification Judgment confirmed that socio-economic rights could be included in a justiciable Bill of Rights and that separation of powers did not require strict separation between executive and legislature. But it refused certification where provisions failed to comply with the Constitutional Principles, especially where they threatened constitutional supremacy, rights entrenchment, institutional independence, local government structure or provincial powers. The Second Certification Judgment then certified the Amended Text after those defects were addressed. Together, the cases show that the Final Constitution was not merely politically negotiated; it was legally certified as compliant with the solemn pact of the Constitutional Principles.

## **A. Introduction: the big Theme 3 story**

Theme 3 is the story of how South Africa moved from apartheid legality to constitutional democracy. The old order was based on parliamentary sovereignty. The new order is based on constitutional supremacy, universal adult suffrage, a justiciable Bill of Rights, separation of powers, democracy and transformation.

## **B. Old order: parliamentary sovereignty and limited review**

Precision point: South African parliamentary sovereignty was not fully Diceyan. Courts could enforce manner-and-form requirements under the South Africa Act 1909, but this procedural review could only delay, not prevent, substantive constitutional abuse once Parliament complied with the formal rules.

Under parliamentary sovereignty, Parliament was the highest law-maker for substantive purposes. Courts could not invalidate Acts of Parliament merely because they were unjust or rights-violating. However, the old South African model was not fully Diceyan: the South Africa Act 1909 contained enforceable manner-and-form limits for entrenched provisions. Harris I showed that courts could enforce those procedures; Harris II showed Parliament could not create an artificial parliamentary court to override judicial decisions; and Collins showed that once Parliament complied formally, courts would not examine motive or substantive injustice.

This exposed the weakness of apartheid legality: law could be valid in form but unjust in substance.

## **C. Liberation constitutional vision**

The liberation movement did not only resist apartheid. It imagined a different constitutional order. The Defiance Campaign exposed the gap between legality and legitimacy. The Congress of the People and Freedom Charter articulated a vision of equal citizenship, universal suffrage, democracy, rights and socio-economic justice. The lecture identifies the Freedom Charter as a normative precursor to the Final Constitution.

Sharpeville exposed the violence of unchecked state power. Rivonia showed how criminal law could be weaponised against resistance and why entrenched rights were necessary. The ANC Constitutional Guidelines later translated liberation ideals into a more concrete constitutional vision, including constitutionalism and a justiciable Bill of Rights. Woolman and Swanepoel note that the 1988 Guidelines were the ANC’s first public expression of a desire to work towards a negotiated settlement and a commitment to constitutionalism generally.

## **D. Negotiations: CODESA, violence and compromise**

The move to democracy required negotiation because neither side could impose a stable settlement alone. CODESA exposed the legitimacy problem: unelected negotiators lacked the democratic mandate to draft the permanent Constitution. The ANC wanted majority rule and an elected constitution-making body, while the NP wanted power-sharing, strong minority protections and supermajorities.

CODESA II failed in 1992 over voting thresholds and transitional government structure. Negotiations also took place amid violence, including Boipatong, township violence, political assassinations and conflict involving the IFP. The Boipatong Massacre led the ANC to withdraw from negotiations, but the lecture notes that Boipatong clarified that the cost of failure was higher than the cost of compromise.

## **E. Two-stage transition**

The key compromise was the two-stage transition. In Stage 1, unelected negotiating parties drafted and adopted the Interim Constitution. In Stage 2, a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly drafted the Final Constitution after the first democratic election.

This solved the legitimacy problem. Unelected negotiators could create a temporary legal bridge, while the permanent Constitution had to be drafted by elected representatives.

## **F. Interim Constitution as bridge**

Legal-continuity point: the Interim Constitution, the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 200 of 1993, was adopted through the procedures of the old legal order. This is the paradox of the transition: legal continuity was used to create constitutional rupture.

The Interim Constitution was finalised by the unelected MPNF before the first democratic election, adopted late in 1993, became binding after 27 April 1994 and was adopted through the procedures of the 1983 Constitution.

The Interim Constitution was a bridge because it used legal continuity to create constitutional rupture. It moved South Africa from apartheid’s culture of authority to a culture of justification.

## **G. Constitutional Principles and certification**
