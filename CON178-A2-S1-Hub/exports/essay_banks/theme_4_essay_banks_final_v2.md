## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: the big idea

South African constitutionalism must be understood as a response to the abuse of law under colonialism and apartheid. Under the old order, Parliament was treated as the highest legal authority, which meant that oppressive laws could be valid even when they were deeply unjust. The 1993 Interim Constitution formally abolished parliamentary sovereignty in South Africa for the first time, and the 1996 Final Constitution confirmed and deepened constitutional supremacy. The key point is that public power is now only valid if it is authorised by, and consistent with, the Constitution.

The lecturer's feedback makes this historical shift important: parliamentary sovereignty was abolished when the Interim Constitution of 1993 took effect, and the 1996 Final Constitution consolidated that break. Students must understand the move from sovereignty to supremacy rather than merely memorising labels.

### B. Definition of constitutionalism

Constitutionalism is the principle that governmental authority is derived from, structured by and limited by a constitution. In Theme 4, constitutionalism is described as the theory of constitutional law and as the philosophy that rulers must act according to binding constitutional boundaries rather than personal will.

**The most important phrase to memorise is:**

Constitutionalism means the Constitution must both structure and constrain state power.

**This means two things:**

The Constitution must structure power by creating institutions such as Parliament, the executive, the courts, provinces, municipalities and Chapter 9 institutions.

It must constrain power by limiting what those institutions may do, subjecting them to rights, legality, accountability and judicial review.

The Theme 4 slides expressly state that constitutionalism requires a constitution to establish institutions and allocate powers for effective government, while also limiting and dispersing power to prevent abuse.

### D. Descriptive vs prescriptive constitutionalism

This is a very useful 80%+ distinction.

Descriptive constitutionalism merely describes how government institutions are arranged. It focuses on form: what institutions exist, how they interact, and how power is distributed. The problem is that a state may have a constitution in this descriptive sense but still be oppressive. This is why colonial and apartheid South Africa can be discussed as having constitutional arrangements in a descriptive sense, but not true democratic constitutionalism in the stronger sense. Theme 4 describes descriptive constitutionalism as a factual account of structures and institutions without reference to normative values.

Prescriptive constitutionalism asks whether the system complies with constitutional principles and values. It is not satisfied by the mere existence of a constitution. It asks whether power is limited by constitutional supremacy, rule of law, separation of powers, democracy, human rights, judicial independence and transformation. Theme 4 expressly states that this is the approach taken by democratic South Africa, and it identifies constitutional supremacy, limited government, rule of law, separation of powers, democratic self-government, human rights, an independent judiciary and transformative constitutionalism as key aspects of prescriptive constitutionalism.

Exam sentence: South Africa's 1996 Constitution reflects prescriptive constitutionalism because it does not merely organise public power; it evaluates and limits public power according to supreme constitutional values, rights and institutional checks.

### E. Parliamentary sovereignty: the old order

Under parliamentary sovereignty, Parliament was the highest legal authority. Courts could interpret legislation, but they generally could not invalidate Acts of Parliament for substantive injustice simply because legislation was oppressive or contrary to fundamental rights. However, where applicable, courts could still enforce procedural or 'manner and form' requirements, such as special procedures attached to entrenched provisions. This mattered in apartheid South Africa because Parliament could use ordinary law to entrench racial domination.

The A1 feedback on the Westminster system is important here: the South Africa Act's Westminster model did not treat all citizens equally; it favoured wealthy white males and excluded Black and African voices from the legislature.

The key criticism is not simply that Parliament had power. The key criticism is that Parliament had power without sufficient constitutional limits, and the majority of people were excluded from the democratic process that created those laws.

### F. Constitutional supremacy: the new order

Constitutional supremacy means the Constitution is the highest law of the Republic. Section 1(c) makes supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law a founding value, while section 2 gives the doctrine legal force by providing that law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution is invalid and constitutional obligations must be fulfilled.

Theme 4 identifies constitutional supremacy as the most definitive feature of South Africa's post-apartheid constitutional system and as the foundation of the democratic constitutional dispensation. It is both a founding value in section 1(c) and a binding rule in section 2.

This means Parliament is no longer legally unlimited. The executive, legislature, judiciary, provinces, municipalities and all organs of state must act within the Constitution. Even democratically supported law may be invalid if it violates the Constitution.

### G. Judicial review: making supremacy real

Constitutional supremacy would be weak if no institution could enforce it. That is why judicial review is essential. Judicial review is the power of courts to test law or conduct against the Constitution and declare it invalid if it is inconsistent. Section 172 provides that courts must declare any law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution invalid to the extent of the inconsistency.

Theme 4 states that constitutional supremacy renders the Constitution justiciable: any law or conduct can be tested against constitutional provisions and declared unconstitutional and invalid if it fails to comply. This is the practical difference between old and new South Africa. Under parliamentary sovereignty, courts were often limited to procedural review. Under constitutional supremacy, courts may review the constitutional validity of law and conduct, including whether they are substantively consistent with constitutional rights, values and obligations.

### I. Does constitutional supremacy undermine democracy?

A strong critical answer must deal with this. Constitutional supremacy does create a counter-majoritarian difficulty because unelected judges may declare laws supported by elected representatives invalid. Theme 4's separation of powers lecture notes this problem: judicial review under section 172 may require declarations of invalidity and remedies, meaning judges can affect laws supported by a majority.

But in South Africa, judicial review is usually justified as part of constitutional democracy rather than as a rejection of democracy. Majority rule is not the same as unlimited power. The Constitution itself is democratic: it protects universal adult suffrage, regular elections, multi-party democracy, accountability, responsiveness and openness. At the same time, it prevents majorities from destroying dignity, equality, freedom and rights.

**The correct argument is:** Constitutional supremacy does not replace democracy; it constitutionalises democracy. It ensures that democracy operates within the limits of the Constitution.

### J. Case plug-in: EFF v Speaker

In Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly 2016 (3) SA 580 (CC), the Constitutional Court held that the Public Protector's remedial action against President Zuma was binding unless set aside by a court. The President's failure to comply was inconsistent with section 83(b), read with sections 181(3) and 182(1)(c), and invalid. The National Assembly's resolution absolving the President was also inconsistent with sections 42(3), 55(2)(a) and (b) and 181(3), invalid and set aside. Note: section 96 forms part of the broader ethical-obligations context in EFF, but the Constitutional Court's final order found the President's failure inconsistent with section 83(b), read with sections 181(3) and 182(1)(c).

The Court linked the case to the need to break from unchecked abuse of state power and resources, and emphasised accountability, rule of law and constitutional supremacy as foundational values.

Exam use: EFF shows that constitutional supremacy is not abstract. Even the President and National Assembly must comply with constitutional obligations, and unconstitutional conduct may be declared invalid.

### K. Conclusion

South African constitutionalism is best understood as a move from a system in which Parliament could authorise oppression to a system in which all public power must be justified under the Constitution. Constitutional supremacy, section 2, judicial review and the Bill of Rights ensure that law and conduct are no longer valid merely because they come from powerful institutions. They must comply with the supreme Constitution. This is why constitutionalism in South Africa is not only structural, but also prescriptive, value-based and transformative.

## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: what is transformative constitutionalism?

Transformative constitutionalism is the idea that the Constitution is not only a supreme legal framework, but also a project of deep social change. It responds to the fact that colonialism and apartheid did not merely create bad laws; they created structural inequality, racial exclusion, economic disadvantage, symbolic domination and a legal culture that often demanded obedience without justification. The 1996 Constitution therefore provides a framework and normative direction for transforming society, not merely a different set of legal rules.

Theme 4 describes the 1996 Constitution as inherently transformative because its central aim is to move from an unjust past to a just future, and because the founding values in section 1 confirm the intention to break from the past.

### B. Definition: Karl Klare's long-term project

The most important definition comes from Karl Klare. In Theme 4, transformative constitutionalism is defined as a long-term project of constitutional enactment, interpretation and enforcement aimed at transforming South Africa's political and social institutions and power relationships in a democratic, participatory and egalitarian direction.

**This definition has several parts:**

### C. Why the Constitution is transformative

The Constitution is transformative because it was adopted after a deeply unequal and violent history. Apartheid law did not only discriminate formally; it organised land, labour, movement, citizenship, political power, public space and social recognition around racial hierarchy. The Constitution therefore had to do more than create elections. It had to build a new society based on dignity, equality, freedom, non-racialism, non-sexism, accountability and social justice.

Section 1 is central here. It entrenches values such as human dignity, equality, human rights, non-racialism, non-sexism, constitutional supremacy, rule of law, universal suffrage, accountability, responsiveness and openness. Theme 4 specifically teaches that transformation is foundational to constitutional interpretation because section 1 values confirm the intention to break from the past. Section 7(1) also matters because it says the Bill of Rights is the cornerstone of democracy and affirms the democratic values of human dignity, equality and freedom.

### D. The bridge metaphor: Mureinik and Langa

One of the best ways to explain transformative constitutionalism is through the bridge metaphor.

Mureinik explained the Interim Constitution as a bridge away from a culture of authority towards a culture of justification. Under apartheid's culture of authority, law often operated on the basis that Parliament had spoken and people had to obey. Because Parliament was elected only by a minority, the law did not need to justify itself to most of the people governed by it. A culture of justification is the opposite. It requires every exercise of public power to be justified with reasons, rights and values. Mureinik argued that the Bill of Rights is central to this shift because it gives people standards by which they can demand justification from those who govern them.

It is important to note that Mureinik wrote in the context of the Interim Constitution and the Interim Bill of Rights. His argument was about the rights framework as a tool for demanding justification of public power.

Langa CJ develops a related but distinct idea. Writing in the transformative constitutionalism context, Langa argues that under a transformative constitution, judges bear the ultimate responsibility to justify their decisions not only by reference to authority, but also by reference to constitutional ideas and values. He states that it is no longer sufficient for judges to rely on the say-so of Parliament or technical readings of legislation. He also sees the Constitution as a bridge from a deeply divided past to a future based on human rights, democracy, peaceful co-existence and opportunities for all. For Langa, the core idea of transformative constitutionalism is that South Africa must change.

The culture of justification, as Langa develops it, therefore goes beyond the rights framework: it requires a change in legal culture itself, so that legal reasoning becomes value-driven, not merely authority-driven.

Exam sentence: The bridge metaphor shows that the Constitution is both backward-looking and forward-looking: it recognises the injustice of the past, but also commits South Africa to a future based on dignity, equality, freedom and substantive justice.

### E. Substantive equality: transformation is more than formal equality

A major feature of transformative constitutionalism is substantive equality. Formal equality means treating people the same on paper. Substantive equality asks whether people can actually enjoy rights and opportunities in real life, given historical disadvantage and material inequality.

This distinction is crucial in South Africa because apartheid produced systemic inequality. Treating everyone "the same" after apartheid would often preserve the unequal effects of the past. Transformative constitutionalism therefore supports measures that address structural inequality, including affirmative action, socio-economic rights, land reform and broader redistributive justice.

Langa explains that transformative constitutionalism includes the establishment of a truly equal society and the provision of basic socio-economic rights to all. He links transformation to access to housing, food, water, healthcare, electricity, education and opportunities.

Exam sentence: Transformative constitutionalism is therefore not satisfied by formal legal equality; it requires substantive equality that confronts the social and economic effects of colonialism and apartheid.

### F. Socio-economic rights and transformation

Transformative constitutionalism is closely linked to socio-economic rights because apartheid created material deprivation, not only political exclusion. The Constitution therefore protects rights such as access to housing in section 26, healthcare, food, water and social security in section 27, and education in section 29.

The case of Soobramoney v Minister of Health (KwaZulu-Natal) 1998 (1) SA 765 (CC) is important because the Court recognised that South Africa inherited deep poverty, unemployment, inadequate social security, and lack of access to clean water and healthcare. Theme 4 uses Soobramoney to show that the Constitution's commitment to transform society into one based on dignity, freedom and equality lies at the heart of the new constitutional order.

However, Soobramoney also shows the limits of courts. The applicant, who had chronic renal failure and needed dialysis, did not succeed because the hospital's criteria for rationing a scarce resource were found to be reasonable. This makes the case useful for both explaining transformation and criticising the limits of legal enforcement.

### G. Transformation of legal culture: from authority to justification

Transformative constitutionalism does not only require changing material conditions. It also requires changing legal culture. Under a culture of authority, legal actors rely on status, hierarchy or formal authority. Under a culture of justification, legal decisions must be defended through constitutional reasons.

Langa states that it is no longer enough for judges to rely only on Parliament's say-so or technical readings of legislation. Under a transformative Constitution, judges must justify decisions by reference to both authority and constitutional ideas and values. This links directly to section 39. Courts must interpret the Bill of Rights, legislation, common law and customary law in a way that promotes the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. That means interpretation is not mechanical. It must be value-based and transformation-conscious.

Exam sentence: Transformative constitutionalism changes the legal question from "is there legal authority?" to "can the exercise of power be justified in terms of constitutional rights, values and purpose?"

### H. Transformative constitutionalism and symbolic redress: City of Tshwane

City of Tshwane Metropolitan Municipality v Afriforum 2016 (6) SA 279 (CC) is a useful case if the question asks about transformation, the Preamble, belonging, public symbols, ubuntu or historical redress.

The case concerned the City's decision to change old street names in Pretoria. Afriforum argued that the removal of the old names harmed Afrikaner culture, heritage and belonging. The Constitutional Court placed the dispute in historical context, explaining that colonialism and apartheid privileged white history and largely excluded Black leaders, institutions and histories from public recognition.

The Court treated the Preamble as important because it recognises past injustices, commits South Africa to healing divisions, and affirms that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, united in diversity. Mogoeng CJ held that the constitutional project requires moving from exclusivity and racial domination towards inclusivity, social cohesion and equitable access to opportunities. The Court also invoked ubuntu, explaining that belonging is relational and requires appreciation, accommodation and respect for others.

Exam use: City of Tshwane shows that transformation is not only economic or legal. It is also symbolic and cultural. Public spaces, names and symbols can either reproduce exclusion or help create constitutional belonging.

### I. Criticisms of transformative constitutionalism

A 90% answer must not only praise transformative constitutionalism. It must also evaluate it.

## 1. Persistent inequality and slow pace of change

One major criticism is that large-scale social and economic transformation remains unfulfilled despite the transformative constitutional framework. Theme 4 identifies persistent racialised poverty and inequality as evidence critics use to argue that the project has failed to meet popular expectations. Critics also argue that transformative constitutionalism cannot solve deep structural problems such as land reform.

**Evaluation:** This criticism is powerful, but it should not lead to the conclusion that the Constitution itself is meaningless. It may show that constitutional ideals require stronger implementation, political will and institutional capacity.

## 2. Too court-centric

Another criticism is that transformative constitutionalism places too much weight on courts and judges. Theme 4 notes that critics argue the project relies too heavily on the judiciary to achieve social change, placing expectations on courts that cannot be met through adjudication alone. S Sibanda argues that transformative constitutionalism has "not translated into much more than rights or law-based approaches to driving change, with little evidence of other work within the discourse directed at inculcating social, economic or cultural institutional or structural power shifts or innovations beyond the courts" ('Not Yet Uhuru' (2018) 224–225). He contends that TC as a strictly legal and court-centred concept is unable to solve complex questions about structural inequality, such as those raised during the #FeesMustFall protests.

**Evaluation:** Courts are important because they enforce rights and constitutional obligations, but transformation cannot be achieved only through litigation. Parliament, the executive, local government, civil society and private actors must also drive change.

## 3. The liberal compromise

A more radical criticism is that the South African constitutional settlement represents an unacceptable compromise that preserves the apartheid-era socio-economic order rather than achieving a true structural break. From a decolonial perspective, some argue that transformative constitutionalism may still be rooted in Western legal traditions and may not adequately respond to the need to decolonise law, institutions and pedagogy.

**Evaluation:** This critique highlights the limits of law-based transformation, but it does not negate the value of constitutional rights protection. The question is how transformation goes beyond courts and legislation.

## 4. Constitutional fetishism

Some critics identify "constitutional fetishism" or "constitutional worship": treating the Constitution as if it were a quasi-religious document, with its own foundational theology (constitutional values), priests (judges) and legal protocols. The concern is that treating the Constitution as the exclusive horizon for social and political change may silence alternative political and radical ideas for transformation.

**Evaluation:** This critique warns against intellectual closure, but it does not mean the Constitution is unimportant. It means constitutional ideas must be engaged critically, not treated as beyond question.

## 5. Counter-majoritarian concern

Transformative constitutionalism can raise a counter-majoritarian issue where unelected judges invalidate laws or government conduct adopted by elected representatives. Theme 4 links this criticism to the tension between democratic majority rule and judicial review.

**Evaluation:** In South Africa, this concern is answered by the idea of constitutional democracy. Courts do not replace democracy; they enforce the democratic Constitution. But courts must still respect separation of powers and institutional limits.

## 6. The limits of judicial law-making

Langa himself recognises that judges do not have a free hand to decide what the law means. The text of the Constitution still matters, and courts must avoid becoming legislators. He warns that the difficulty lies in finding the line between transformation and legislation.

**Evaluation:** This is the best balanced critique: transformation requires active interpretation, but not unlimited judicial power.

## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: the Constitution is not morally neutral

South Africa's Constitution is a value-based Constitution. This means it is not merely a technical rulebook that creates institutions and procedures. It is a supreme legal order built around moral, political and social commitments. These values matter because South Africa's previous legal order often had formal legality without substantive justice: apartheid laws were "legal" in a narrow sense, but they were unjust, exclusionary and oppressive.

Theme 4 explains that the 1996 Constitution is a supreme, value-based, democratic and transformative constitutional framework created against the background of colonialism, apartheid, parliamentary sovereignty and the abuse of law as an instrument of oppression. Public power must now be exercised lawfully, rationally, accountably and consistently with dignity, equality and freedom.

### B. Definition of value-based constitutionalism

Value-based constitutionalism means that the Constitution is rooted in core values that guide the interpretation, application, limitation and development of law. Courts and legal actors do not only ask, "What does the law literally say?" They must also ask, "Which interpretation best promotes the Constitution's text, structure, rights, principles and values?"

The Theme 4 Final Boss defines value-based constitutionalism as the idea that constitutions are not merely legal or procedural documents, but are rooted in core ethical, moral and social values that guide the interpretation of rights and laws.

Exam sentence: Value-based constitutionalism marks a break from apartheid legality because it rejects the idea that law is valid simply because it was properly enacted; law and public power must now be justified through the constitutional text, structure, rights, principles and values.

### H. S v Makwanyane: the best case for values

S v Makwanyane 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC) is the strongest case for value-based constitutionalism, especially if the question asks about dignity, values, public opinion, proportionality or limitation.

**Important note on constitutional context:** Makwanyane was decided under section 33 of the Interim Constitution of 1993, not under section 36 of the 1996 Constitution. It remains central to the 1996 constitutional order because the First Certification Judgment (para 90) confirmed that section 36(1) of the 1996 Constitution substantially repeats the proportionality approach formulated in Makwanyane.

**Facts:** Makwanyane and Mchunu had been convicted in the Witwatersrand Local Division on four counts of murder each and sentenced to death under section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977. The Constitutional Court had to decide whether the statutory provision authorising capital punishment was constitutional.

**Issue:** Whether the death penalty could be justified in a constitutional order based on rights and values such as life, dignity, equality and freedom.

**Court reasoning:** Chaskalson P held that the limitation of constitutional rights involves weighing up competing values and applying a proportionality analysis. The inquiry must consider the nature and importance of the right, the purpose of the limitation, the extent of the limitation, the relation between the limitation and its purpose, and whether less restrictive means are available.

The state relied on deterrence, prevention, retribution and public safety. But the Court held that these purposes had to be balanced against the rights and values at stake. The death penalty destroys life, assaults dignity, risks arbitrariness and error, and operates in a society shaped by racial and social inequality. The Court was not persuaded that capital punishment was materially more effective than life imprisonment as a deterrent. The Court also made an important value-based point about public opinion: even if public opinion supports the death penalty, public opinion cannot replace the Court's duty to uphold the Constitution.

**Judgment:** The death penalty was unconstitutional.

**Constitutional significance:** Makwanyane shows that values are not decorative. They determine constitutional meaning, guide limitation analysis and may require courts to protect constitutional commitments even against popular sentiment.

Exam use: Use Makwanyane whenever the question asks about dignity, equality, freedom, section 36 (noting it was decided under s 33 IC), proportionality, values, public opinion, constitutional democracy or the difference between law and justice.

### L. Critical engagement: the danger of vague values

A 90% answer should include a balanced critique. Value-based constitutionalism is powerful because it prevents law from becoming a morally empty technical system. It helps courts interpret law in a way that promotes dignity, equality, freedom, transformation, openness and accountability.

But there is also a danger. Values can be broad and contested. Judges may disagree about what dignity, equality or freedom require in a particular case. This can raise concerns about judicial discretion and the counter-majoritarian difficulty, especially where courts invalidate laws supported by elected representatives or public opinion.

The answer is not to abandon values. The Constitution itself requires value-based reasoning through sections 1, 7, 36 and 39. The better view is that courts must use values carefully, transparently and with reasons. This fits the idea of a culture of justification: values must not be used as slogans, but as reasons that justify constitutional interpretation and outcomes.

Exam sentence: The critical issue is not whether courts may use values, because the Constitution requires them to do so; the issue is whether courts justify their use of values in a principled, transparent and context-sensitive way.

The Constitution does not merely create institutions and procedures; it establishes a supreme normative order founded on dignity, equality, freedom, non-racialism, non-sexism, accountability, responsiveness, openness, constitutional supremacy and the rule of law. These values guide the interpretation of rights under section 39, the limitation of rights under section 36 and the broader transformation of law and society. Cases such as Makwanyane (decided under the Interim Constitution but central to the 1996 framework through section 36), Daniels and City of Tshwane show that constitutional values are not abstract decorations. They shape legal reasoning, constitutional remedies and South Africa's movement from apartheid legality to substantive constitutional justice.

## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: why application matters

The Bill of Rights is central to South Africa's transformative constitutional order because it protects dignity, equality and freedom and gives practical effect to constitutional supremacy. However, before a court can decide whether a right has been infringed or limited, it must ask how the Bill of Rights applies.

**The application enquiry asks:**

Which right is involved?

Who is the alleged violator?

Is the relationship vertical or horizontal?

Is the right being applied directly or indirectly?

Which constitutional provision governs the application?

Are there competing rights or justifications that must be balanced?

The Theme 4 Final Boss sets this out as the decision tree: identify the Chapter 2 right, ask direct or indirect, ask vertical or horizontal, identify s 8(1), s 8(2) or s 39(2), and balance competing rights using constitutional values and section 36 where relevant.

### B. Direct application

Direct application occurs when a litigant relies directly on a constitutional right in the Bill of Rights to challenge law or conduct. The Constitution itself is the source of the claim or defence.

Theme 4 defines direct application as a situation where a person, company or organ of state violates a Chapter 2 right, or where legislation, common law or customary law is inconsistent with the Bill of Rights. If applied directly, the Bill of Rights overrides inconsistent law or conduct.

**Example:** If the state arrests a person for lawful protest, the person may rely directly on constitutional rights such as freedom of expression in s 16 and assembly in s 17. If a law authorises the violation and cannot be justified under s 36, the law or conduct may be declared invalid under constitutional supremacy.

Exam sentence: Direct application means the Constitution itself is used as the legal standard to test law or conduct.

### C. Indirect application

Indirect application occurs when the Bill of Rights does not operate as the direct cause of action, but influences the interpretation or development of ordinary law.

The key provision is section 39(2). It requires every court, when interpreting legislation and when developing common law or customary law, to promote the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.

Theme 4 defines indirect application as asking whether ordinary legal rules promote Bill of Rights values; if they do not, courts must interpret legislation or develop common law/customary law to conform with constitutional values.

**Example:** A claimant may sue in delict using the common law, but the court may develop the common law duty of care in light of constitutional values. This is indirect application because the case is formally about delict, but the Constitution shapes the common law rule.

Exam sentence: Indirect application means ordinary law remains the immediate source of the claim, but that law must be interpreted or developed in light of the Bill of Rights.

### D. Vertical application: state versus individual

Vertical application concerns the relationship between the state and a private person. It applies when the alleged rights violator is the state, an organ of state, or a public institution exercising public power.

Section 8(1) provides that the Bill of Rights applies to all law and binds the legislature, executive, judiciary and all organs of state. Section 7(2) adds that the state must respect, protect, promote and fulfil the rights in the Bill of Rights.

**Your tutorial material puts it neatly:** vertical application concerns the relationship between the state and persons, and asks whether law or state conduct unjustifiably infringes a protected right. For example, if a public school, municipality, police official or government department excludes someone from a benefit because of race, that implicates equality and may be attacked as unconstitutional state conduct.

### E. Horizontal application: private person versus private person

Horizontal application concerns private relationships. It applies when one private person, company or private institution allegedly infringes the right of another private person.

Section 8(2) provides that a provision of the Bill of Rights binds a natural or juristic person if, and to the extent that, it is applicable, taking into account the nature of the right and the nature of any duty imposed by the right.

**This is very important:** horizontal application is not automatic. The court must ask whether the particular right is suitable for application in that private relationship.

### F. Direct horizontal application

Direct horizontal application occurs when a private person relies directly on a constitutional right against another private person.

Theme 4 defines direct horizontal application as a situation where the Bill of Rights applies directly to private conduct, allowing a private person to rely directly on a constitutional right in a dispute with another private person.

Section 9(4) is the easiest example. It expressly states that no person may unfairly discriminate directly or indirectly against anyone. This makes equality particularly suitable for horizontal application because the constitutional text itself directly speaks to private persons. So if a private employer or private restaurant discriminates unfairly, the applicant may rely on equality principles. In practice, private equality claims are often given effect through legislation such as PEPUDA or through interpretation/development of ordinary law, even though s 9(4) recognises that private discrimination may have constitutional significance.

### G. Indirect horizontal application

Indirect horizontal application occurs when a private-law dispute is resolved through ordinary law, but the ordinary law is interpreted or developed in light of constitutional values.

Theme 4 defines indirect horizontal application as the influence of the Bill of Rights on private law through s 39(2), requiring courts to interpret legislation or develop the common law in a way that promotes the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.

**Example:** A delictual claim may be decided through common law, but the court may develop the common law duty of care to reflect constitutional values such as dignity, freedom and security of the person. That is indirect horizontal application.

### H. Carmichele: key case for indirect application

Carmichele v Minister of Safety and Security 2001 (4) SA 938 (CC) is the key case for indirect application and development of common law.

**Facts:** The applicant was severely attacked and injured by a man who had previously been released on bail despite the police and prosecutors knowing about his history of violent behaviour. The police and public prosecutors had failed to oppose bail or take steps to warn or protect the applicant. The case raised the question of whether the state owed a delictual duty of care to members of the public who were foreseeably at risk from such a person.

**Issue:** Whether courts must develop the common law of delict to promote the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights under section 39(2).

**Court reasoning:** The Constitutional Court confirmed that courts have a duty under s 39(2) to develop the common law where necessary to align it with constitutional values. The common law duty of care could be developed to hold the state liable for failing to take reasonable steps to protect individuals from foreseeable harm, in line with constitutional values of dignity, freedom and security of the person.

**Judgment:** The matter was sent back so the common law could be developed or applied consistently with constitutional values.

**Constitutional significance:** It is a foundational case for indirect horizontal application and constitutional development of common law.

Exam use: Use for s 39(2), indirect application, common law development, horizontal effect and transformative constitutionalism.

### I. Grootboom: key case for vertical application

Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom 2001 (1) SA 46 (CC) is a strong vertical application example.

**Facts:** The respondents, including many children, had been evicted from their informal homes and shacks and were living in appalling conditions with inadequate shelter. They challenged the state's housing programme under section 26.

**Issue:** Whether the state had taken reasonable measures to realise the right of access to adequate housing.

**Court reasoning:** The Court required a reasonable housing programme that made provision for people in crisis and desperate need, not only long-term housing plans.

**Judgment:** The state's programme was unreasonable because it did not adequately provide for people in emergency and desperate need.

**Constitutional significance:** An important vertical socio-economic rights case showing how communities can hold the state constitutionally accountable.

Exam use: Use for vertical application, socio-economic rights, reasonableness review and transformative constitutionalism. Do not use Grootboom as a horizontal application example — the state was the respondent.

### J. First Certification Judgment: horizontal application is valid but not unlimited

The First Certification Judgment — Certification of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 1996 (4) SA 744 (CC) is useful for a 90% answer because it shows that horizontal application was controversial during certification.

Objectors argued that horizontal application would let courts interfere with private law and legislative functions. The Constitutional Court rejected this. It reasoned that courts have always developed the common law, so there is no separation of powers objection to courts continuing to do so in a constitutional era. The Court also rejected the argument that balancing competing rights is not a proper judicial role.

Most importantly, the Court confirmed that horizontal application is not unqualified. Section 8(2) is conditioned by the phrase "if, and to the extent that, it is applicable", taking into account the nature of the right and the nature of the duty. It also linked the development of customary law to s 39(2), requiring courts to promote the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.

Exam sentence: The First Certification Judgment shows that horizontal application is constitutionally legitimate, but carefully controlled by the nature of the right, the nature of the duty and the court's ordinary role in developing common law and customary law.

Horizontally, it may bind private persons where the nature of the right and duty justify it. Direct application allows reliance on the Constitution itself, while indirect application allows constitutional values to shape ordinary law. This framework gives effect to the transformative Constitution by ensuring that dignity, equality and freedom influence both public power and private legal relationships.

## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: rights are fundamental, but not absolute

The Bill of Rights is the cornerstone of South African constitutional democracy. It protects rights and affirms the democratic values of dignity, equality and freedom. However, rights are generally not absolute. In a constitutional democracy, rights may sometimes conflict with other rights or with important public purposes such as safety, equality, public order, health, or the protection of vulnerable people.

Section 36 is the mechanism that controls this process. It ensures that rights are not limited casually or merely because the state finds a right inconvenient. A limitation must be authorised by law of general application and justified through proportionality. Within s 36, one factor asks about the relation between the limitation and its purpose, but this must not be collapsed into the separate legality doctrine, where rationality is a minimum requirement for the exercise of public power.

**Theme 4 makes this very clear:** rights are not absolute, but the Constitution permits their limitation only under strict conditions.

### B. Definition of the limitation clause

A limitation clause allows constitutionally valid limitation of rights in certain circumstances. Section 36 provides that rights in the Bill of Rights may be limited only:

in terms of law of general application, and

to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable,

in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom,

taking into account all relevant factors, including the five listed factors in s 36(1)(a)–(e).

Exam sentence: Section 36 does not weaken rights; it protects rights by ensuring that any limitation must be justified through constitutional values and proportionality.

### C. The two-stage enquiry

Do not start by applying s 36 immediately. The correct method is a two-stage enquiry.

**StageQuestionWhat you doStage 1:** Infringement / limitationHas a right in the Bill of Rights been limited?Identify the right, explain its content, and show how the facts interfere with it.Stage 2: JustificationIs the limitation reasonable and justifiable under s 36?Apply the s 36 factors and conclude whether the limitation is constitutional.

If there is no limitation of a right, there is no need to justify anything under s 36. If there is a limitation, the burden shifts to justification: the limiting party, usually the state, must show that the limitation is constitutionally acceptable.

### D. "Law of general application"

Before proportionality, section 36 requires that the limitation must be in terms of law of general application. This means the limitation must be authorised by a legal rule that applies generally, rather than by an arbitrary individual command. It can include legislation, common law or another valid legal norm, but it must be accessible, non-arbitrary and general enough to satisfy the rule of law.

Exam sentence: The "law of general application" requirement links section 36 to the rule of law because rights may not be limited by arbitrary or ad hoc exercises of power.

### E. The five section 36 factors

Precision note: The s 36 proportionality enquiry should not be collapsed into legality rationality review. By contrast, rationality review under the rule of law / principle of legality is a separate minimum requirement for the exercise of public power.

### F. Proportionality: the heart of section 36

The real enquiry under s 36 is proportionality. It is not a mechanical checklist. A court must weigh the importance of the right against the importance of the limitation's purpose and the severity of the limitation.

The First Certification Judgment 1996 (4) SA 744 (CC) (para 90) confirms this. It states that limitation of constitutional rights involves weighing competing values and ultimately an assessment based on proportionality. It also confirms that section 36(1) of the 1996 Constitution substantially repeats the approach formulated in S v Makwanyane, and that the criteria in s 36 conform to internationally accepted standards of proportionality or balancing.

Exam sentence: Proportionality asks whether the constitutional harm caused by limiting the right is justified by the importance and effectiveness of the purpose pursued.

### G. De Vos and Freedman five-stage proportionality guide

Theme 4 includes a useful De Vos and Freedman five-stage proportionality guide (pages 453–455):

### H. S v Makwanyane: the foundational case

S v Makwanyane 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC)

**Important note on constitutional context:** Makwanyane was decided under section 33 of the Interim Constitution of 1993, not section 36 of the 1996 Constitution. It remains the foundational case for limitation analysis under the 1996 Constitution because the First Certification Judgment (para 90) confirmed that section 36(1) substantially repeats the proportionality approach formulated in Makwanyane.

**Facts:** Makwanyane and Mchunu had been convicted in the Witwatersrand Local Division on four counts of murder each and sentenced to death under section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977. The appeal raised a constitutional challenge based on rights including life, dignity and protection against cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment.

**Issue:** Whether the death penalty was compatible with the Interim Constitution and, if it limited rights, whether that limitation was justified.

**Court reasoning:** Chaskalson P held that the limitation of constitutional rights involves the weighing up of competing values and an assessment based on proportionality. There is no absolute standard for reasonableness and necessity; the enquiry must be conducted case by case, considering the nature and importance of the right, the purpose of the limitation, the extent of the limitation, the relation between the limitation and its purpose, and whether less restrictive means are available.

**The state relied on important purposes:** deterrence of violent crime, prevention of further murders, retribution and public safety. But those purposes had to be balanced against the rights and values at stake. The death penalty destroys life, assaults dignity, involves risks of arbitrariness and error, and operates in a criminal justice system shaped by historical social and racial inequality.

The Court was not persuaded that capital punishment was materially more effective than life imprisonment as a deterrent. Prevention could be achieved through long-term or life imprisonment. Retribution was accepted as a legitimate purpose of punishment, but it could not outweigh life and dignity. Public opinion also could not replace the Court's duty to uphold the Constitution.

**Judgment:** The death penalty was declared unconstitutional. Section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act was held to be inconsistent with the Constitution to the extent that it authorised the death penalty for murder.

**Constitutional significance:** Makwanyane established South African limitation analysis as a proportionality enquiry. In every limitation case, the state must show that the limitation serves a sufficiently important purpose, is properly connected to that purpose, impairs the right no more than is constitutionally acceptable, and is proportionate in light of dignity, equality and freedom.

### I. Public opinion and constitutional democracy

One of the most examinable points in Makwanyane is the Court's treatment of public opinion. The Court accepted that many people may have supported the death penalty, but constitutional rights cannot depend only on majority opinion. Courts have a duty to uphold the Constitution, including when doing so is unpopular. This links to value-based constitutionalism and the counter-majoritarian role of courts.

Exam sentence: Makwanyane shows that public opinion may be relevant background, but it cannot determine whether a rights limitation is constitutional.

### J. Relationship between s 36 and s 39

SectionFunctions 36Determines whether a limitation of a right is reasonable and justifiable.s 39Guides interpretation of rights and ordinary law through dignity, equality and freedom.

Section 36 is the justification stage, while section 39 shapes the meaning of rights and law before and during that justification enquiry. South African constitutional adjudication is not mechanical: courts must ask whether law and conduct are consistent with the values of an open and democratic society based on dignity, equality and freedom.

### K. Balancing competing rights: PE Municipality and Fourie

Port Elizabeth Municipality v Various Occupiers 2005 (1) SA 217 (CC): competing claims between property interests and occupiers facing eviction. Sachs J emphasised that fairness required considering both owners' rights and occupiers' dignity, especially in the context of South Africa's history of forced removals. Use where a limitation or balancing question involves property, housing, dignity, eviction, homelessness or historical disadvantage.

Minister of Home Affairs v Fourie 2006 (1) SA 524 (CC): exclusion of same-sex couples from marriage violated equality and dignity. Use where a limitation or balancing question involves equality, dignity, religion, marriage, pluralism or social transformation.

### L. Critical engagement: limitation is necessary but dangerous

**Why limitation is necessary:** Rights can conflict with other rights or important social goals — expression vs dignity, property vs housing, privacy vs public safety. A constitutional democracy needs a limitation clause.

**Why limitation is dangerous:** Limitation can become a tool for weakening rights if courts accept vague purposes too easily or allow "public interest" to be used without careful analysis. This is why s 36 insists on law of general application, dignity, equality, freedom, proportionality and less restrictive means.

Critical exam sentence: Section 36 is both a permission and a protection: it permits rights to be limited where justified, but protects rights by requiring the state to give strong constitutional reasons for the limitation.

### A. Introduction: why rule of law matters

The rule of law is one of the foundational values of South Africa's constitutional democracy. It is listed in section 1(c) together with constitutional supremacy. This tells us something important: the rule of law is not just an ordinary legal doctrine. It is part of the foundation of the Republic.

In the South African context, the rule of law responds to a history where law was used to authorise racial domination, repression and abuse of public power. The new constitutional order requires more than obedience to legal form. Public power must now be authorised by law, rational, accountable and consistent with the Constitution.

Theme 4's source map identifies EFF v Speaker as authority for the rule of law, Public Protector remedial action, Chapter 9 institutions, accountability, separation of powers and constitutional obligations.

### B. Definition of the rule of law

The rule of law means that public power must be exercised according to law, not arbitrary will. It requires legality, rationality, accountability and respect for constitutional limits.

**Put simply:** Government may only do what the law authorises, and it must exercise that power rationally and accountably.

The rule of law is closely linked to constitutional supremacy. Section 1(c) makes both the supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law founding values. Section 2 gives this practical force by providing that the Constitution is supreme, inconsistent law or conduct is invalid, and constitutional obligations must be fulfilled.

### C. Principle of legality

The principle of legality is the minimum enforceable requirement of the rule of law. It means that every exercise of public power must:

have a source in law;

remain within the limits of that legal power;

be rationally connected to a legitimate governmental purpose.

Theme 4 expressly describes legality as a minimum requirement of the rule of law, even where no specific right is directly invoked. State action must still have legal authority, the decision-maker must act within power, and the action must be rational.

Exam sentence: The rule of law is the broader constitutional value; legality is the concrete doctrine courts use to test whether public power is authorised and rational.

### H. EFF v Speaker: the key case

Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly 2016 (3) SA 580 (CC)

**Facts:** The Public Protector investigated security upgrades at President Jacob Zuma's private residence at Nkandla. She found that some features were non-security related and that the President and his family had unduly benefited. She ordered remedial action, including that the President repay a reasonable portion of the costs and that the ministers involved be reprimanded.

For well over one year, neither the President nor the National Assembly did what they were required to do in terms of the remedial action. Instead, the National Assembly adopted a resolution that effectively absolved the President of liability. The Economic Freedom Fighters and the Democratic Alliance approached the Constitutional Court.

**Issue:** The main issues were:

Was the Public Protector's remedial action binding?

Did the President breach his constitutional obligations by failing to comply?

Did the National Assembly breach its constitutional obligations by failing to hold the President accountable?

Could the National Assembly validly set aside or ignore the Public Protector's remedial action?

**Court reasoning:** The Constitutional Court, per Mogoeng CJ, linked the case to South Africa's need to break from unchecked abuse of state power and resources. ### A. Introduction: democracy is more than voting

Democracy is one of the central ideas of the 1996 Constitution. However, South African democracy must not be reduced to voting every few years. The Constitution creates a richer democratic model that includes representative institutions, participatory processes, limited direct democratic mechanisms and constitutional limits on majority power.

### B. Definition of democracy: Theunis Roux

The lectures use Theunis Roux's idea that the core of democracy is that decisions affecting members of a political community should be taken by the members themselves, or at least by elected representatives whose power ultimately derives from those members. This definition captures both direct and representative democracy and lends itself to understanding that democracy, as found in the 1996 Constitution, embodies different conceptions.

Democracy can therefore operate in two main ways:

Directly — people make decisions themselves.

Representatively — people elect representatives to make decisions on their behalf.

But in South Africa, this is still incomplete unless we add:

Participatory democracy — people must have meaningful opportunities to be heard.

Constitutional democracy — all democratic power is limited by the Constitution.

### E. Representative democracy

Representative democracy is the dominant institutional form of South African democracy. It means citizens do not usually make laws themselves. Instead, they elect representatives who exercise public power through constitutional institutions.

Section 42(3) says the National Assembly is elected to represent the people and to ensure government by the people under the Constitution. ### F. Multi-party democracy and electoral reform

South Africa's representative democracy is also a multi-party democracy. Section 1(d) expressly refers to a multi-party system of democratic government.

Historically, political parties were the main way citizens were represented in Parliament, especially under proportional representation. But this model raised questions about whether citizens could participate outside party structures.

New Nation Movement NPC v President of the Republic of South Africa 2020 (6) SA 325 (CC)

The Electoral Act had allowed only political parties to contest national and provincial elections. The issue was whether excluding independent candidates violated political rights under section 19. The Constitutional Court held that excluding independent candidates violated citizens' political rights and declared the Electoral Act unconstitutional to that extent.

The case is significant because it expanded democratic participation beyond party-based representation and led to electoral reform.

Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023 allows independent candidates not affiliated with political parties to contest elections for the National Assembly and provincial legislatures. It came into effect for the 2024 elections.

Exam sentence: New Nation Movement shows that representative democracy is not frozen in a party-only model; political rights under section 19 may require electoral arrangements that allow citizens to stand as independent candidates.

This case shows that democracy is not exhausted by voting; it also requires continuing public involvement in law-making.

South Africa is finally a constitutional democracy. This means democratic power is exercised under a supreme Constitution and limited by rights, values, judicial review and the rule of law. Democracy is therefore not unlimited majority rule. Elected institutions must comply with the Constitution, including s 2, the Bill of Rights, s 36, s 39 and judicial review under s 172. Theme 4 defines constitutional democracy as decision-making under a Constitution with features such as constitutional supremacy, founding values, human rights, democracy and judicial review.

Critically, constitutional democracy creates a tension between democracy and judicial review. Courts may invalidate laws supported by elected representatives, raising the counter-majoritarian difficulty. Theme 4 notes that this is a concern because judges are not democratically elected representatives. However, judicial review can also enhance democracy by ensuring that majority power remains subject to rights, participation, accountability and constitutional values.

In conclusion, South Africa should not be described as only direct or only representative democracy. It is primarily a representative constitutional democracy, enriched by participatory obligations and limited direct democratic mechanisms. Its democratic model responds to apartheid's exclusion by combining universal suffrage, political rights, elected institutions, public participation, rights protection and constitutional limits on majority power.

## 4. Full Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: separation of powers as a limit on public power

Separation of powers is a constitutional doctrine that limits public power by dividing governmental authority between different institutions. It is part of constitutionalism because it prevents public power from being concentrated in one person or institution. This matters especially in South Africa because colonialism and apartheid showed how concentrated state power can be used to oppress, exclude and control.

Theme 4 explains that constitutions limit public power in both substantive and procedural ways. Rights are substantive limits because they restrict what the state may do to people. Separation of powers is a procedural limit because it controls the way public power is exercised, which institution may act, and how institutions check one another.

Exam sentence: Separation of powers does not only ask whether government may act; it asks which branch may act, how it may act, and which institution may check that action.

### B. Definition of separation of powers

Separation of powers divides government into three branches:

Legislature — makes law.

Executive — implements law and policy.

Judiciary — interprets and applies law, resolves disputes and enforces the Constitution.

Theme 4 states that separation of powers divides government into the executive, legislature and judiciary, and emphasises that the branches are not a hierarchy.

This is important. Separation of powers does not mean the judiciary is "above" Parliament, or that Parliament is "above" the courts. It means each branch has distinct constitutional functions and must act within constitutional limits.

### C. Purpose of separation of powers

**The doctrine has three main purposes:**

Theme 4 explains that separation of powers avoids concentration of power, promotes specialisation, and links modern separation of powers closely to human rights because procedural limits help protect substantive rights.

### D. The four principles of separation of powers

Exam sentence: The doctrine is not only about separating institutions; it is also about giving them constitutional tools to check abuse of power.

### E. Classical model vs South African model

The classical model imagines three distinct branches with clear separation between them — sometimes called the trias politica. However, South Africa does not follow a rigid or absolute separation. The South African model is flexible, contextual and historically shaped.

If an exam asks whether South Africa follows separation of powers, the answer is: Yes, but not in a rigid classical sense. South Africa follows a flexible constitutional model with overlap, checks and balances, judicial independence and Chapter 9 accountability institutions.

### F. The textual basis: separation of powers in the 1996 Constitution

The Final Constitution does not contain a single provision expressly labelled "separation of powers," but the doctrine is embedded in its structure. Section 43 vests legislative authority separately in Parliament at the national level, provincial legislatures, and municipal councils. Section 85 vests national executive authority in the President together with Cabinet. Section 165 vests judicial authority in the courts. These three provisions separately allocate the core functions of government to distinct institutions, giving the doctrine a clear textual foundation.

Constitutional Principle VI of the Interim Constitution also required separation of powers with appropriate checks and balances to ensure accountability, responsiveness and openness. The Constitutional Court has consistently treated the doctrine as a fundamental principle of the 1996 constitutional order, and this links directly to section 1(d) of the 1996 Constitution, which protects accountability, responsiveness and openness as founding values.

### G. Legislature in South Africa

The legislature is mainly governed by Chapter 4 of the Constitution. Parliament consists of the National Assembly and the National Council of Provinces under s 42. Legislative authority at the national level is dealt with in s 43.

The legislature makes law, represents the people, deliberates on public issues, holds the executive accountable and exercises oversight. The National Assembly's oversight function is especially important. Section 55(2) requires the National Assembly to provide mechanisms to ensure that executive organs of state are accountable to it and to maintain oversight over the exercise of national executive authority.

Theme 4 notes that the National Assembly exercises oversight, can hold the executive accountable, and can initiate serious political accountability mechanisms such as votes of no confidence or impeachment processes.

### H. Executive in South Africa

The executive is governed mainly by Chapter 5, especially ss 84 and 85. Section 85 vests executive authority in the President, exercised together with Cabinet.

The executive develops and implements policy, administers state affairs, prepares and implements legislation, coordinates government, and exercises powers such as commissions of enquiry. There is overlap between the legislature and executive because Cabinet ministers are generally drawn from the National Assembly. Theme 4 explains that this overlap is inherited from the Westminster system and reflects the policy-to-legislation relationship.

**This overlap is important for exams:** South Africa does not have a strict separation of personnel between legislature and executive.

### I. Judiciary in South Africa

The judiciary is governed by Chapter 8. Section 165 provides that judicial authority is vested in the courts, courts are independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law, and organs of state must assist and protect the courts to ensure their independence, impartiality, dignity, accessibility and effectiveness.

The judiciary decides disputes, interprets law, applies the Constitution, reviews law and conduct and issues binding orders. Judicial review is central to the judiciary's role. Section 172 requires courts to declare law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution invalid to the extent of its inconsistency. Section 167(5) gives the Constitutional Court final authority on whether an Act of Parliament, provincial Act or conduct of the President is constitutional.

### J. Judicial independence

Judicial independence is especially protected because courts must be able to decide disputes without fear, favour or political pressure. Theme 4 says there is no overlap with the judiciary in the same way as between the legislature and executive because judicial independence must be protected.

Judicial independence is supported by section 165, the judicial appointment process under s 174, the limited grounds for removal under s 177 (incapacity, gross incompetence or gross misconduct), and Judicial Service Commission involvement.

South African Association of Personal Injury Lawyers v Heath 2001 (1) SA 883 (CC)

The Constitutional Court confirmed that appointing a judge to perform executive-type investigative functions may threaten judicial independence and separation of powers. Theme 4 specifically mentions this case in relation to judicial independence and impartiality.

Exam use: Use SAAPIL v Heath to show that judicial independence is not only about judges deciding cases; it also prevents judges from being placed in roles that compromise the judiciary's institutional independence.

### K. Delegation and flexibility: Executive Council Western Cape

In Executive Council of the Western Cape Legislature v President of the Republic of South Africa 1995 (4) SA 877 (CC), the Court considered delegation of law-making authority. Theme 4 explains that delegation may be permissible to some degree, but its constitutionality depends on the nature and extent of the delegation.

This case shows that separation of powers is flexible but not unlimited. Delegation may support efficiency and specialisation, but excessive delegation may undermine democracy by allowing the executive to take over core legislative power.

Exam sentence: Executive Council Western Cape shows that South African separation of powers permits practical overlap, but still guards against excessive concentration of law-making power in the executive.

### L. De Lange v Smuts: no universal model

In De Lange v Smuts NO 1998 (3) SA 785 (CC), the Constitutional Court recognised separation of powers as part of South Africa's constitutional structure, but not as a rigid universal formula. The South African model must be understood with reference to the text, history and structure of the Constitution.

Theme 4 uses De Lange for the proposition that South Africa's separation of powers must be understood contextually and historically, not by mechanically importing a foreign model.

Exam sentence: De Lange supports the view that separation of powers is not a rigid formula; its content depends on each constitutional system's history, text and institutional design.

### M. Chapter 9 institutions as an extra accountability layer

Chapter 9 institutions complicate the classical three-branch model. They do not fit neatly into the legislature-executive-judiciary triangle. They are independent institutions supporting constitutional democracy.

Examples include the Public Protector, the South African Human Rights Commission, the Commission for Gender Equality, the Auditor-General, and the Electoral Commission. Section 181 provides that these institutions are independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law, and that organs of state must assist and protect them. Theme 4 says Chapter 9 institutions are a unique set of institutions outside the classic model, exercise oversight, occupy a unique position, and introduce another layer of checks and balances.

**Link to EFF v Speaker:** In Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly 2016 (3) SA 580 (CC), the Public Protector ordered remedial action against President Zuma. The President did not comply, and the National Assembly adopted a resolution absolving him. The Constitutional Court held that the Public Protector's remedial action was binding unless set aside by a court, and that both the President and National Assembly failed to fulfil constitutional obligations. Theme 4 explains that EFF links rule of law, Chapter 9 institutions, parliamentary oversight, executive accountability and judicial enforcement.

### O. Counter-majoritarian dilemma

The counter-majoritarian dilemma is the tension created when unelected judges invalidate laws or conduct by elected representatives. This is unavoidable in a system of constitutional supremacy. Under s 172, courts may declare law or conduct invalid. This means courts can invalidate law supported by a democratic majority. Theme 4 explains that judicial review can require declarations of invalidity and remedies, and this creates practical problems because judges can affect law despite not being democratically elected representatives.

**Why it is a problem:**

Judges are not elected.

Courts may invalidate decisions of elected branches.

Courts may appear to "make law" through remedies.

Judicial decisions may conflict with political will.

**Why judicial review is still justified:** Theme 4 gives several responses: safeguards exist such as stare decisis, s 39, appeal and review; majoritarian politics is not always truly representative; South Africa has pluralism and proportional representation; human rights protect everyone, including minorities and vulnerable groups; and judicial review can be a necessary and legitimising limit within democracy.

Exam sentence: The counter-majoritarian dilemma is real, but in South Africa judicial review is generally treated as a necessary and legitimising feature of constitutional democracy rather than a contradiction of democracy.

### P. Deference

Deference means that courts should respect the institutional competence and democratic role of the legislature and executive, while still enforcing the Constitution. Deference does not mean courts must avoid difficult constitutional questions. It means courts must enforce constitutional boundaries without taking over policy-making or administration where the Constitution leaves choices to the political branches.

Theme 4 defines deference as the idea that courts should respect the institutional competence and democratic role of other branches while still enforcing the Constitution. It warns that courts must not abdicate their role, but must also avoid prescribing policy details where discretion belongs to another branch.

**EFF v Speaker and deference:** The Court held that the National Assembly failed in its constitutional obligations, but it also recognised that courts should not prescribe exactly how the National Assembly must conduct oversight or design its internal mechanisms. The Court enforced the constitutional boundary without taking over Parliament's internal accountability design.

Exam sentence: Deference requires courts to respect institutional roles, but not to permit constitutional violations in the name of institutional respect.

### Q. Critical engagement

A high-mark answer must avoid treating separation of powers as rigid isolation. South Africa's model requires balance: too much separation can prevent effective government; too much overlap can concentrate power; too much judicial intervention can threaten democratic legitimacy; too much judicial deference can allow constitutional violations; Chapter 9 institutions strengthen accountability but may create institutional tensions.

The correct South African approach is contextual. Courts must ask what the Constitution requires, what function each institution performs, and whether the conduct threatens accountability, independence, democracy or constitutional supremacy.

Exam sentence: The South African doctrine of separation of powers is not a wall between branches; it is a constitutional design for coordinated but limited power, institutional accountability and protection against abuse.

## 4. Full Integrated Exam-Ready Study Answer

### A. Introduction: the Constitution as a complete constitutional order

The 1996 Constitution is not merely a technical legal document. It is a supreme, value-based, democratic and transformative constitutional order. It was adopted after a history in which colonialism and apartheid used law to entrench exclusion, racial hierarchy, unequal citizenship and concentrated public power. The Constitution therefore performs two connected functions: it limits public power and it promotes transformation.

The central point is that South Africa moved from a system where law could be used as an instrument of oppression to a system where all law and public power must be justified under a supreme Constitution. Theme 4 describes the Constitution as a framework that structures power, limits power, transforms society, protects rights, embeds democracy and makes public power accountable.

### B. Constitutionalism: power must be structured and constrained

The first concept is constitutionalism. Constitutionalism means that government authority is derived from and limited by a constitution. In Theme 4, the key phrase is that a constitution must both structure and constrain state power. It structures power by creating institutions such as Parliament, the executive, the courts, provincial and local government, and Chapter 9 institutions. It constrains power by limiting what those institutions may do.

This is important because a state can have a constitution in a descriptive sense while still being oppressive. The stronger South African idea is prescriptive constitutionalism: the Constitution does not merely describe institutions; it judges public power according to constitutional values, rights, legality, accountability and transformation.

**Link to exam:** If the question asks how public power is limited, constitutionalism is your starting point: power is not free-floating; it is allocated, structured and limited by the Constitution.

### C. Constitutional supremacy: the Constitution is the highest law

The second concept is constitutional supremacy. Parliamentary sovereignty was abolished when the Interim Constitution of 1993 took effect; the 1996 Final Constitution confirmed and deepened this. Section 1(c) lists supremacy of the Constitution and the rule of law as founding values, while section 2 provides that the Constitution is supreme, that inconsistent law or conduct is invalid, and that constitutional obligations must be fulfilled.

Constitutional supremacy becomes practical through judicial review. Section 172 requires courts to declare law or conduct inconsistent with the Constitution invalid to the extent of its inconsistency.

Integrated point: Constitutional supremacy limits public power because no institution is above the Constitution. It also promotes transformation because constitutional values and rights can be enforced against old laws, new laws and unconstitutional conduct.

### D. Transformative constitutionalism: the Constitution points from past to future

The Constitution does not only limit power; it aims to change society. This is transformative constitutionalism.

Karl Klare's definition describes transformative constitutionalism as a long-term project of constitutional enactment, interpretation and enforcement aimed at transforming South Africa's political and social institutions and power relationships in a democratic, participatory and egalitarian direction.

Mureinik, writing in the Interim Constitution context, explained the shift from a culture of authority to a culture of justification: every exercise of public power must be justified through reasons, rights and values. Langa CJ developed this further in the transformative constitutionalism context: under a transformative constitution, judges must justify decisions not only by reference to authority, but also by reference to constitutional values and ideas. He describes the Constitution as a bridge from a deeply divided past to a future based on human rights, democracy, peaceful co-existence and opportunities for all.

Integrated point: Transformative constitutionalism explains why the Constitution is not satisfied with formal legality. Law must help dismantle the continuing effects of apartheid and build substantive equality, participation and belonging.

### E. Foundational values: the Constitution is value-based

The Constitution is also value-based. Section 1 entrenches values such as dignity, equality, human rights, non-racialism, non-sexism, constitutional supremacy, rule of law, universal adult suffrage, regular elections, multi-party democracy, accountability, responsiveness and openness. Section 7(1) confirms that the Bill of Rights is the cornerstone of democracy and affirms the democratic values of human dignity, equality and freedom. These values are repeated in s 36, which governs limitation of rights, and s 39, which governs interpretation.

S v Makwanyane 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC) — decided under section 33 of the Interim Constitution, but central to the 1996 constitutional order because the First Certification Judgment (para 90) confirmed that section 36(1) substantially repeats the Makwanyane proportionality approach — is the strongest case example. The Court held the death penalty unconstitutional after weighing values such as life, dignity, equality, deterrence, retribution and proportionality, and held that public opinion cannot replace the Court's duty to uphold the Constitution.

Integrated point: Foundational values connect transformation, rights, democracy and limitation. They ensure that the Constitution is not morally neutral.

### F. Application of the Bill of Rights: rights bind both public and sometimes private power

Section 7(2) requires the state to respect, protect, promote and fulfil rights. Section 8(1) binds the legislature, executive, judiciary and all organs of state. This is vertical application.

Section 8(2) allows rights to bind natural and juristic persons where applicable, considering the nature of the right and duty. This is horizontal application. Section 8(3) allows courts to apply or develop the common law when giving horizontal effect to rights. Section 39(2) supports indirect application by requiring courts to interpret legislation and develop common law or customary law in a way that promotes the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. Carmichele v Minister of Safety and Security 2001 (4) SA 938 (CC) confirms that courts must develop common law under s 39(2) to align it with constitutional values.

Integrated point: The Bill of Rights does not only restrain the state. It can also reshape ordinary law and private relationships where dignity, equality, freedom or power imbalance require it.

### G. Limitation of rights: rights are strong, but not absolute

Section 36 provides that rights may be limited only by law of general application and only to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom. The limitation analysis has two stages: first, has a right been limited; and second, is the limitation justified under s 36, applying proportionality through the five listed factors.

Integrated point: Section 36 shows that the Constitution does not create absolute individualism. It creates a structured culture of justification: if rights are limited, the limitation must be constitutionally justified.

### H. Rule of law and legality: public power must be lawful and rational

The rule of law is a founding value in s 1(c). It means public power must be exercised according to law, not arbitrary will. The principle of legality is the minimum enforceable requirement of the rule of law: public power must have legal authority, remain within that authority, and be rationally related to a legitimate governmental purpose. Fedsure 1999 (1) SA 374 (CC) confirms lawful authority; Pharmaceutical Manufacturers 2000 (2) SA 674 (CC) confirms rationality.

But rule of law must not be treated as merely formal. Apartheid had law, but not justice. South Africa's rule of law must be understood together with dignity, equality, constitutional supremacy, rights and transformation.

Integrated point: Rule of law limits public power by preventing arbitrariness. Transformative constitutionalism deepens rule of law by requiring justification, not mere formal legality.

### I. Democracy: people govern, but under the Constitution

South Africa is a representative constitutional democracy, strengthened by participatory democracy and limited direct democratic elements. Section 1(d) founds South Africa on universal adult suffrage, a national common voters roll, regular elections and a multi-party system of democratic government to ensure accountability, responsiveness and openness. Section 19 protects political rights. Section 42(3) provides that the National Assembly represents the people and ensures government by the people under the Constitution.

South Africa is also participatory. Sections 59, 72 and 118 require public involvement in legislative processes. In Doctors for Life International v Speaker of the National Assembly 2006 (6) SA 416 (CC), the Court held that representative and participatory democracy are mutually supportive and that public participation gives vitality to representative democracy. In New Nation Movement NPC v President of the Republic of South Africa 2020 (6) SA 325 (CC), the Court held that excluding independent candidates from national and provincial elections violated political rights under s 19.

Integrated point: Democracy under the Constitution is not unlimited majority rule. It is government by the people under a supreme Constitution, with rights, participation, accountability and judicial review.

### J. Separation of powers: public power is divided and checked

Separation of powers is a procedural limit on public power. It divides authority between the legislature, executive and judiciary. The doctrine rests on four principles: division of government power, separation of functions, separation of personnel and checks and balances. South Africa follows a flexible model, not a rigid classical model. Sections 43, 85 and 165 separately vest legislative, executive and judicial authority in distinct institutions, giving the doctrine a clear textual foundation. Judicial independence is strongly protected by s 165. Courts enforce constitutional supremacy through judicial review under s 172 and the Constitutional Court's final authority under s 167(5).

Constitutional Principle VI required separation of powers with checks and balances to ensure accountability, responsiveness and openness.

Integrated point: Separation of powers prevents concentration of power, but checks and balances ensure that branches remain accountable to the Constitution and to each other.

### K. Chapter 9 institutions and accountability: EFF v Speaker

Chapter 9 institutions add an extra accountability layer beyond the classical three branches. Section 181 provides that these institutions are independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law, and that organs of state must assist and protect them. The Public Protector's power to take appropriate remedial action comes from s 182(1)(c).

Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly 2016 (3) SA 580 (CC) is the key integrated case. The Public Protector ordered remedial action against President Zuma regarding Nkandla. The President failed to comply, and the National Assembly adopted a resolution absolving him. The Constitutional Court held that the Public Protector's remedial action was binding unless set aside by a court, that the President's failure was inconsistent with s 83(b) read with ss 181(3) and 182(1)(c) and invalid, and that the National Assembly's resolution was inconsistent with ss 42(3), 55(2)(a) and (b) and 181(3), invalid and set aside.

The case connects constitutional supremacy, rule of law, accountability, presidential obligations, Parliament's oversight duties, Chapter 9 institutions, judicial review and separation of powers in a single judgment.

Integrated point: EFF shows that constitutional accountability is enforceable. Even the President and National Assembly must comply with constitutional obligations.

### L. Critical tension: courts, democracy and deference

The Constitution gives courts strong review powers, but courts are unelected. This creates the counter-majoritarian dilemma: judges may invalidate laws or conduct supported by elected representatives. This tension is real, but South African constitutional law generally treats judicial review as a necessary part of constitutional democracy. Courts protect rights, enforce accountability, ensure participation and prevent abuse of power.

However, courts must also show deference by respecting the institutional competence of Parliament and the executive. Deference does not mean abdication: courts should not prescribe policy details where discretion belongs to another branch, but they must enforce constitutional limits where the Constitution is violated. EFF illustrates this balance precisely.

Integrated point: The Constitution requires balance: courts must not govern, but political branches must not violate the Constitution.

### M. Conclusion: the integrated answer

The 1996 Constitution limits public power and promotes transformation at the same time. Constitutionalism structures and constrains power. Constitutional supremacy makes the Constitution the highest law. The Bill of Rights protects dignity, equality and freedom. Section 36 permits only justified limitations. Section 39 ensures value-based interpretation. The rule of law and legality prevent arbitrary public power. Democracy gives people political voice but subjects majority power to constitutional limits. Separation of powers divides authority and creates checks and balances. Chapter 9 institutions support accountability. Together, these doctrines create a constitutional order that moves South Africa from apartheid's culture of authority to a culture of justification, where all public power must be lawful, rational, accountable, democratic, rights-respecting and transformative.
