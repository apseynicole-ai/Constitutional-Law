• Value-based constitutionalism is a legal concept that emphasises
that constitutions are not just legal, procedural documents but
are rooted in core ethical, moral, and social values.
• It prioritises these underlying principles to guide the
interpretation of rights and laws.
• Constitutional values are the foundational principles that underpin
a democratic state's legal framework and guide its governance.Value based constitutionalism
S1 Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996
The Republic of South Africa is one, sovereign, democratic state founded on the
following values:
(a) Human dignity, the achievement of equality and the advancement of human
rights and freedoms.
(b) (b) Non-racialism and non-sexism.
(c) c) Supremacy of the constitution and the rule of law.(
(d) d) Universal adult suffrage, a national common voters' roll, regular elections and a
multi-party system of democratic government, to ensure accountability,
responsiveness and opennessValue based constitutionalism
Some Core Values and Application
• Human Dignity: Often positioned as the core value,
underpinning the right to self-worth.
• Equality: Demands substantive equality - addressing
structural barriers and historic disadvantage - rather
than just formal, equal treatment.
• Freedom: Includes liberty of thought, expression, and
action.Value based constitutionalism
• The values of human dignity, equality and freedom (and others) permeate the
legal system
• What does this mean in practice?
• Courts do not only ask “ what does the law say”
• They also ask “what outcome will best reflect the constitutional values
• Once again represents a break from the injustices of the past
• The Constitution aims to build a just, inclusive, and democratic society
(refer back to discussion on Transformative Constitutionalism)Value based constitutionalism
• Value-based constitutionalism, therefore, contrasts with
strict legal positivism
• Constitutional interpretation in South Africa is therefore seen
to be more moral, purposive, and transformative.Value based constitutionalism

The limitations Clause
• Rights are not absolute – they can in certain circumstances, be limited
• S36 contains a limitation clause – that ALLOWS for the
constitutionally valid limitation of rights in certain circumstances
• Limitation is determined by a two stage enquiry
• First court must established a right has been infringed upon
(limited)
• Second the court must determine whether it is justifiableValue based constitutionalism
The limitations Clause
S36 Constitution, 1996 (S33 (1) IC)
• S36: The rights in the Bill of Rights may be limited only in terms of the law of
general application, to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable
in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality, and
freedom
• Factors to guide whether the limitation is reasonable and justifiable
• Nature of the right
• Importance and relationship between the purpose of limitation and the
limitation
• There are no less restrictive means to achieve the purposeValue based constitutionalism
The limitations Clause
Re: S v Walters [para 26-27] : This is essentially a two-stage exercise. First, there is the threshold
enquiry aimed at determining whether or not the enactment in question constitutes a limitation on one or
other guaranteed right. This entails examining (a) the content and scope of the relevant protected right(s)
and (b) the meaning and effect of the impugned enactment to see whether there is any limitation of (a) by
(b). Subsections (1) and (2) of section 39 of the Constitution provide guidance on the interpretation of both
the rights and the enactment, essentially requiring that they be interpreted to promote the value system
of an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom. If upon such
analysis no limitation is found, that is the end of the matter. The constitutional challenge is dismissed there
and then.
If there is indeed a limitation, however, the second stage ensues. This is ordinarily called the limitations
exercise. In essence this requires a weighing-up of the nature and importance of the right(s) that are limited
together with the extent of the limitation as against the importance and purpose of the limiting enactment.Value based constitutionalism

● When litigating the Bill of Rights, the Court
has to determine whether the BOR applies
○ Directly or Indirectly
○ Vertically or HorizontallyThe Application of the Bill of Rights
● Direct application:
○ Direct application of the Bill of Rights occurs when a person, company, or state
organ violates a constitutional right found in chp 2
○ Or when the ordinary rules of law (Legislation, common law, customary law) are
not consistent with the Bill of Rights
○ If they are not, the BOR if applied directly will override these rules of law – ie law
will be declared invalid by the courts
○ Example: company firing a woman due to pregnancy (violating section 9
equality rights), would be unconstitutional and invalid
○ Example: S v Mawanyane– Death Penalty declared unconstitutionalThe Application of the Bill of Rights
● Indirect application: the purpose is to determine whether the ordinary rules of the
law promote the values of the BOR
○ If they do not, the court will use the BOR to develop the rules of law (s39(2))
○ Example: the development of common law to align with constitutional values, as
seen in Carmichelev Minister of Safety and Security . (2001) CC: The Court used
s39(2) to interpret the law of delict to promote rights and constitutional values,
rather than directly invalidating a statute.
■ IE: Instead of declaring a law invalid (direct application), courts interpret
legislation or develop common law/customary law to conform with the Bill
of RightsThe application of the Bill of Rights
● The next question is then, is it a vertical or horizontal
application?
○ This refers to the different relationships and legal

Vertical application
● Governed by s8(1) – looks at the relationship between the state and private
individuals
● Imposes obligations on the state to respect, protect and fulfil rights in the BOR
● Acts an additional check on state power – therefore binds the legislative, executive,
judiciary and all organs of state
● Protects citizens from abuse of power by the government
● Example:
○ A person brings a claim against the police for unlawful arrest, or someone
who challenges a government policy that they claim violates their rightsThe application of the Bill of Rights
Examples of vertical application of rights :
● Right to Equality (Section 9): An individual sues a government department for
refusing to hire or promote them based on race, gender, or sexual orientation.
● Freedom of Expression (Section 16): A journalist or citizen challenges the police
for arresting them for participating in a lawful public protest.
● Right to Access Information (Section 32): A person uses the PAIA act to force a
public body, eg DHA, to disclose records held by the state.The application of the Bill of Rights
● Socio-economic Rights (Sections 26 & 27): Communities challenging the
government for failing to provide adequate, basic water services or, as in
the Grootboom case, reasonable access to housing.
● Right to Fair Trial (Section 35): An accused person argues that their rights were
violated by the police or prosecutor during their arrest or detention.
● Property Rights (Section 25): A landowner challenges the state's expropriation of
their property, arguing it was not done for a public purpose or without fair
compensation.The application of the Bill of Rights
Horizontal application
● Governed by s8(2) – requires considering the nature of the right and duty
imposed
● When applied horizontally, this imposes obligations between private persons
(individuals or companies) to respect the rights of the BOR (in certain
circumstances)
○ Protects citizens from violations of rights by private parties such as unfair
discrimination by an employer
○ IE you don’t have to rely on the common law or legislation – you can say
“this person has violated my constitutional right”The application of the Bill of Rights
Direct Horizontal application
● Governed by s8(2): The BOR applies directly to the conduct of private
individuals, allowing them to rely directly on a constitutional right for a
claim
Indirect Horizontal Application
● S39(2): The BOR influences private law by requiring courts to interpret
legislation or develop the common law in a way that promotes the sprite,
purport, and object of the BORThe application of the Bill of Rights
● Question to the class: Why is it important to have
direct horizontal application of the Bill of Rights?The application of the Bill of Rights
Example 1: Equality (S9)
● Scenario: A private employer refuses to hire someone based on their race,
gender, or disability.
● Even though this is a private employer (not the state) the right to equality
can apply directly
● The private individual can argue, “my constitutional right to equality has
been infringed by this private actor”
● Equality is a right that clearly makes sense in private relationships
(employment, services etc)
● Courts recognised that discrimination is not only a state problemThe application of the Bill of Rights
Example 2: Freedom of Expression (s16)
Scenario: A private media company refuses to publish an opinion article
● The private individual might try to rely on freedom of expression
● BUT the court will have to balance
○ The writers freedom of expression
○ The companies freedom to choose what it publishes
○ Rights can apply directly but can compete with other rights in
private relationshipsThe application of the Bill of Rights
Example 3: Right to housing (s26)
Scenario: A private land owner evicts people living on their land without following the legal
processes
● Those evicted can rely directly on the right to access housing
● They can argue “this eviction violates my right to housing”
● Rights will still apply between the private individuals
● The Court will ask
○ Is eviction just and equitable
○ What processes were followed
○ What is the impact on the vulnerable people
● Again we see competing rights of private individuals as the land owner has his own proterty
rights protected by s25The application of the Bill of Rights
Example 4: Right to education (s29)
Scenario: Private school refuses to admit a learner without a fair and lawful
reason (arbitrary exclusion)
● The learner or parent may rely directly on s29 (right to basic education)
● They can argue “The decision unjusrifably limites my right to education”
● Private schools are private individuals (not the state)The application of the Bill of Rights

○ The nature of the right

JUDGMENT
MOGOENG CJ (Moseneke DCJ, Bosielo AJ, Jafta J, Khampepe J, Madlanga J,
Mhlantla J, Nkabinde J and Zondo J concurring)
Introduction
[1] This case concerns a restraining order granted in favour of Afriforum and
Mr Evert Van Dyk (Afriforum)1 against the City of Tshwane Metropolitan
Municipality (Council). The Council was ordered to stop removing the old street
names in the Pretoria area and bring back those that had been removed already. For a
proper understanding of the issues in this matter, a historical perspective and the
implications of the underlying constitutional vision need to be outlined. This is
accentuated by Afriforum’s reliance on the Preamble to the Constitution.
1 The respondents will be referred to as “Afriforum” throughout the judgment. This should by no means be
misunderstood as a sign of disrespect or disregard for Mr Van Dyk. It is done purely for convenience.
2MOGOENG CJ
Essential context
[2] South Africa is literally the last African country to be liberated from the system
that found nothing wrong with the institutionalised oppression of one racial group by
another for no other reason but the colour of their skin, shape of their nose and the
length or texture of their hair. The underlying reason advanced for this irrational
differentiation was that African people in particular and black people in general, were
intellectually inferior, lazy and lesser beings in every respect of consequence. As a
result, there hardly was any city, town, street, or institution of note that bore a name
that sought to give honour to black people’s leaders or recognition to their institutions
or treasured history. Everything about the oppressed was dismissively branded as
backward and inconsequential. Virtually all recognition and honour was thus
respectively given to and bestowed upon white history and their heroes and heroines.
The system was all about the entrenchment of white supremacy and privilege and
black inferiority and disadvantage. No wonder the United Nations resolved that that
system was a crime against humanity.
[3] More than three centuries from the inception of that system, South Africans of
all races took it upon themselves to create a platform for the normalisation or
harmonisation of race relations, democratisation of their country and attainment of
peace and social cohesion. Against all odds, the nation has admirably come to the
point where impunified violence, racial hatred or subjugation in all its manifestations
is unlike before seldom openly and proudly practised.
2
[4] That said, colonialism or apartheid is a system so stubborn that its divisive and
harmful effects continue to plague us and retard our progress as a nation more than
two decades into our hard-earned constitutional democracy. Almost all cities, towns
and street names continue to reverberate with great sounds of veneration for the
architects of apartheid, heroes and heroines of our oppressive and shameful colonial
past. Virtually no progressive or potentially conciliatory change to city, town or street
2 There has been numerous but comparatively fewer and less frequent open or public incidents of blatant racism
over the years.
3MOGOENG CJ
names goes unchallenged. There are fairly regular challenges to the equitable
distribution of honour to heroes of all cultural or racial groups and a concomitant
determination to preserve exclusivity to privilege and meaningful control. This
highlights the crucial role of the Preamble to our Constitution, relied on by Afriforum.
[5] A preamble is after all a succinct expressionary statement that sets out a
constitution’s purpose and underlying philosophy.By design and like all others, our
Preamble captures the essential principles by which we the people seek to govern our
affairs. It is such a crucial part of our Constitution that, if only every citizen were to
internalise it and live according to its terms, our aspirations would most likely be
expeditiously realised. Ours reads in relevant part:
“We, the people of South Africa,
Recognise the injustices of our past;
Honour those who suffered for justice and freedom in our land;
Respect those who have worked to build and develop our country; and
Believe that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, united in our diversity.
We therefore, through our freely elected representatives, adopt this Constitution as
the supreme law of the Republic so as to—
Heal the divisions of the past and establish a society based on democratic values,
social justice and fundamental human rights.”
[6] Knowing just how deep and engrafted the distrust, divisions and injustices were
in the very being of some of our people from the days of apartheid, we have made a
solemn undertaking to embark on an all-inclusive constitutional project, geared at
achieving national unity and reconciliation. The injustices of the past are not to be
pampered or approached with great care or understanding or sympathy. And the
immeasurable damage racism or cultural monopoly has caused requires that stringent
measures be taken to undo it. That approach will help us move away from exclusivity
to opportunities, racial domination and intolerance to inclusivity, social cohesion and
equitable access to opportunities.
4MOGOENG CJ
[7] The normalised demonisation and stigmatisation of heroes and heroines of our
struggle for justice, peace and freedom is now a thing of the past. We the people of
South Africa promise to honour them, presumably the same way heroes and heroines
have been venerated in this country and around the world. Just as important is the
need to respect white and black South Africans who played a crucial role in building
and developing South Africa into the modern country of note it now is. All of us must
embrace and internalise the constitutional reality that this country belongs to all of us
who live in it. Diversity thus ought to highlight the need for unity rather than
reinforce the inclination to stand aloof and be separatist. An appreciation of the
value-addition or special contribution of diversity, as in other countries, should
strengthen our collective resolve to unite and tap into the special skills and
experiences of all diverse groups in this country, for the betterment of all.
[8] As a people who were not only acutely divided but were also at war with
themselves primarily on the basis of race, one of several self-imposed obligations is
healing the divisions of the past. The effects of the system of racial, ethnic and tribal
stratification of the past must thus be destroyed and buried permanently. But the
healing process will not even begin until we all make an effort to connect with the
profound benefits of change. We also need to take steps to breathe life into the
underlying philosophy and constitutional vision we have crafted for our collective
good and for the good of posterity. That would be achieved partly by removing from
our cities, towns, “dorpies”, streets, parks, game reserves and institutions, names that
exalt elements of our past that cause grief to other racial groups or reopen their
supposedly healing wounds. Also, by removing even some innocuous names that give
recognition only to the history, language, culture or people of one race, so as to make
way for the heritage and deserving heroes and heroines of the previously excluded.
This is to be done sensitively and in pursuit of inclusivity, unity in diversity and
recognition of the need for a sense of belonging for all. We all have the duty to
transform our society. And all, black and white, are an essential part of “we the
people of South Africa” that shoulder the burden to do so.
5MOGOENG CJ
[9] Our shared values that underpin our constitutional vision cannot be achieved
when one race almost always has its way or a near-absolute monopoly of respect and
honour. That is a recipe for the illegitimate retention of exclusive privilege,
undeserved domination of the past and future hostilities as opposed to inclusivity,
reconciliation and the unity in diversity we have undertaken to pursue and achieve.
No measure of sophistry, contortion, or strategy ought to be allowed to entrench any
form of racial domination or exclusivity to privilege, honour and opportunities. For
that is inconsistent with our foundational values and constitutional vision. South
Africans of all races must unite to secure a brighter, peaceful, stable and prosperous
tomorrow by allowing the previously excluded groups, to also be honoured in their
own land. They too should at long last have a sense of belonging.
[10] This case highlights the need to familiarise ourselves with our vision in the
Preamble to our Constitution. It also sounds a clarion call to South Africans of all
races to take to heart the foundational values of our Constitution like human dignity,
equality, the advancement of human rights and freedoms, non-racialism and
non-sexism. When our actions are informed and driven by these facets of our
constitutional project, then a proposed change of names of landmarks, streets and
institutions would only attract constitutionally-inspired and constructive opposition.
Strife and the consequential deepening of the divisions of the past would thus be most
likely avoided or minimised.
[11] All peace and reconciliation-loving South Africans whose world-view is
inspired by our constitutional vision must embrace the African philosophy of
“ubuntu”. “Motho ke motho ka batho ba bangwe” or “umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu”
(literally translated it means that a person is a person because of others). The African
world-outlook that one only becomes complete when others are appreciated,
accommodated and respected, must also enjoy prominence in our approach and
attitudes to all matters of importance in this country, including name-changing. White
South Africans must enjoy a sense of belonging. But unlike before, that cannot and
should never again be allowed to override all other people’s interests. South Africa no
6MOGOENG CJ
longer “belongs” to white people only. It belongs to all of us who live in it, united in
our diversity. Any indirect or even inadvertent display of an attitude of racial
intolerance, racial marginalisation and insensitivity, by white or black people, must be
resoundingly rejected by all South Africans in line with the Preamble and our values,
if our constitutional aspirations are to be realised.
[12] South Africa still looks very much like Europe away from Europe. A very
insignificant number of names of our cities, towns and streets gives recognition to the
indigenous people of this country and other black people. Very little recognition or
honour is given to their heritage, history, heroes and heroines in their own motherland.
This does not reflect but rather belies a commitment by all to the spirit of genuine
unity, transformation and reconciliation.
[13] In this country, names of places and institutions of importance generally
celebrate one-sidedness and at times resonate with the legacy of our oppressive past
with unbelievable boldness and alacrity. Hopefully, this does not signify a
disinclination to change for fear of protestations by retentionists that mere change to
what they cherish, however miniscule, not only poses a threat to them and their
environment but also denies them a sense of belonging. For, that position would
inadvertently be saying to those who are victims of colonialism and apartheid, that
they have no legitimate claim to any sense of belonging whatsoever.
[14] Our constitutional vision militates against a never-ending determination to
oppose change to city, town or street names. Through the Preamble and the entire
Constitution we imposed on ourselves the duty to transform. Recognition of the
injustices of the past is neither a slogan nor an empty or meaningless assertion of
recognition. It heralds an obligation to actively participate not in the perpetuation but,
in the eradication of the injustices of the past. Honouring those who suffered for
justice and freedom like Dr Beyers Naude and Advocate Bram Fischer promises,
amongst other things, the naming of streets and institutions of importance after them.
Respecting those who worked and developed South Africa recognises the role of our
7MOGOENG CJ
black and white compatriots who toiled over the years to make our country better than
it was before. For indeed South Africans across racial lines worked hard to change the
country for the better, even during apartheid. A belief that South Africa belongs to all
who live in it united in our diversity, is the antithesis of any obsession with or
exaggeration of the role and importance of one racial group above all others.
[15] Thoroughgoing introspection is thus called for. Both black and white
South Africans need to strive for the attainment of our shared values and
constitutional aspirations. It is impermissible to ever adopt an attitude that seems to
suggest that some of our people can afford to endure the pain and torture induced and
symbolised by instruments of the colonial and apartheid legacy, probably because they
have endured them long enough to find them tolerable, if not somewhat acceptable.
This is even more so where others are categorical about their total inability to tolerate
progressive and inclusive instruments like streets bearing names of leaders from other
cultural groups even temporarily.
[16] Nothing that objectively encourages or seeks to perpetuate the stereotypes,
prejudice or discriminatory practices of the past is to be tolerated. Inclusivity, unity in
diversity, recognition of the culture and history of white and black South Africans and
reconciliation are our chosen paths to the prosperous future. They accelerate social
cohesion and the process of healing the divisions of the past. This national project
demands that we reject everything that sustained, entrenched and still promotes racial
discrimination.
[17] Ours is a country with great potential for enduring peace, stability and
sustainable economic growth. Much will however depend on how we manage our
differences as individuals, groups and as a nation. Our utterances and actions must
always take cue from the foundational values of and Preamble to our Constitution.
Our commitment to reject the injustices of the past, the disunity and pain they brought
about must be unwavering and matched by our actions. That way and in harmony, we
will be able to replace old names that celebrate people and things that divided and
8MOGOENG CJ
caused us deep and incalculable pain with new ones that recognise the previously
ignored and unify the nation.
[18] Our peculiarity as a nation impels us to remember always, that our Constitution
and law could never have been meant to facilitate the frustration of real justice and
equity through technicalities. The kind of justice that our constitutional dispensation
holds out to all our people is substantive justice. This is the kind that does not ignore
the overall constitutional vision, the challenges that cry out for a just and equitable
solution in particular circumstances and the context within which the issues arose and
are steeped. We cannot emphasise enough, that form should never be allowed to
triumph over substance. Our Constitution was never meant to be a selectively
recognised weapon, conveniently produced and used by some of us only when it could
help advance illegitimate sectarian interests through legal stratagems. It was designed
to facilitate justice and equity for all. That said, legitimate individual or sectarian
rights and interests may always be vindicated and appropriately addressed within the
prism of this constitutional dispensation.
[19] This then sets the scene for the resolution of a challenge to the order that seeks
to preserve street names that Council believes are irreconcilable with our
constitutional project.
Background
[20] In 2002, Council adopted policy guidelines relating to the possible change of
street names and heritage sites in the city of Pretoria and the surrounding areas. One
of the guidelines that stood out was that a street name would not be changed unless
fifty one percent of the inhabitants of the ward in which it is located, agrees. How it
could ever have been possible to change names in suburbs or industrial areas that are
dominated by those who see nothing wrong with them, however objectively offensive
the names might be, remains a mystery.
9MOGOENG CJ
[21] In 2007, Council passed a resolution to replace those guidelines with new ones.
And it was purportedly in terms of the new policy guidelines that a decision was
ultimately taken to change 25 of the more than 100 old street names. The final
decision was preceded by some consultative meetings held in areas located in 10 of
the 76 wards of greater Pretoria.
[22] Council in effect says that this change was necessitated by the dictates of
inclusivity, unity in diversity, overdue recognition of and bestowal of honour to the
previously dishonoured as well as the need to heal the divisions of our past.
Afriforum has consistently opposed the mooted changes from the beginning all the
way through to the implementation of Council’s resolution to replace old street names
like Dr Hendrik Verwoerd, Louis Botha and Walker with new ones like President
Nelson Mandela, Chief Justice Ismail Mohamed, Solomon Mahlangu and Steve Biko.
Council’s decision viewed in context seems indeed to have been intended to shed
Pretoria of its colonial and apartheid legacy and to introduce those names that
symbolise the pursuit of justice, peace, unity, reconciliation, fundamental human
rights and freedoms for all our people. This should, however, never be misunderstood
to mean that the end will always justify the means.
[23] When Afriforum learnt that Council had resolved to replace some of the old
street names with the new, it brought an urgent application to restrain Council from
doing so. That application came before Tuchten J. He did not have to make any order
because Council undertook not to take any of the measures objected to by Afriforum.
The lifespan of this self-restraint was six months. Afriforum also undertook to bring
an application to have the decision to change the old street names reviewed within ten
days of Council’s undertaking. Whereas Council honoured its undertaking, Afriforum
did not launch its review application as promised.
[24] Long after the expiration of the self-imposed six months moratorium, Council
decided to and did implement its resolution to change street names. This prompted
Afriforum to launch an urgent application. Even then, Afriforum only launched its
10MOGOENG CJ
review proceedings seven months after the expiration of the ten day period. This it
says was a consequence of its hope that an amicable solution to the street
name-change issue could still be found.
[25] Council never promised to put that project on hold forever or indefinitely. Nor
did it undertake not to implement its decision without first informing Afriforum. It is
therefore inappropriate to accuse Council of deliberately taking long to respond to
Afriforum’s inquiry on whether it was going to replace old names anytime soon, with
a view to ensuring that it would have completed that project before Afriforum could
take legal steps. Afriforum’s footprints appear to be all over Pretoria. It is highly
unlikely that all the street names could have been removed and replaced without any
of its constituents becoming aware of it and alerting Afriforum. The decision to
challenge the name-changing process has always been Afriforum’s to make. And so
was the timing entirely in its hands. Its indecision and inaction cannot properly be
blamed on Council.
[26] Having passed the resolution to change some of the names arguably linked to
the colonial and apartheid legacy, Council embarked on the process of implementing
its resolution. Afriforum, however, sees even a temporary removal of the old street
names as doing violence to what defines the very being of the Afrikaner people as
well as their healthy and peaceful existence. To them it was an assault on their
treasured history and heritage which could not be left unchallenged. As a result, they
launched a fresh urgent application for an order restraining Council from removing the
old names and directing it to restore those names that had already been removed. The
order was granted by Prinsloo J in those terms, pending the finalisation of the review
proceedings.
[27] The rationale behind the grant of the interim order is essentially this. The old
street names are an historical treasure and a heritage so intimate to the very being of
the Afrikaner people that their removal would constitute an infringement of their right
to enjoy their culture as envisaged by section 31 of the Constitution. In other words,
11MOGOENG CJ
what reigns supreme in Afriforum’s opposition to the notion that old names be
removed, is that they are an integral part of an irreplaceable and much-cherished
history, heritage and culture of the Afrikaner people. So dear and invaluable are the
old street names to them, that even their temporary displacement would not only give
rise to inestimable emotional hurt but also to irreparable harm. The replacement of
old names with those of black people would according to Afriforum somehow toxify
the environment to the point of jeopardising the health of like-minded residents of
Pretoria. The temporary retention of the old names would, they say, give them a sense
of place and a sense of belonging.
[28] Additionally, the temporary removal of the old names would cause them to be
forgotten, with the result that by the time litigation processes connected to the review
are finalised, courts would in all likelihood conclude that the horse has already bolted3
and that no meaningful purpose would be served by bringing back the old names.
Furthermore, confusion would reign consequent upon the placement of only new
names since tourists, residents and business people would find it difficult to locate
their destinations pending the drafting of new directional maps with new street names
or updating the GPS. Business people would also have to change their stationery at
great expense and if the review succeeds change it back to what it was.
[29] Aggrieved by Prinsloo J’s order, Council unsuccessfully sought leave to appeal
against it. It then petitioned the Supreme Court of Appeal which granted it leave to
appeal to the Full Court.

[30] The Council’s case is that Afriforum has not satisfied the requirements for the
interim interdict they were granted. In particular, that it stood to suffer irreparable
harm in the event of the interim order not being granted5 and that the balance of

[140] In cases where money is not at stake, the harm consists, when interim relief is
considered, in the applicant’s temporary disablement from enjoying the right.90
During the oral hearing, counsel for the Municipality was asked whether a parent
claiming access to a child for a weekend a fortnight before the trial determination of
the parents’ respective rights of access would suffer irreparable harm if that access
was thwarted. He said No. Consonantly with his case, he had to.
[141] But the answer was wrong. The harm is irreparable. This follows from the
nature of interim interdict proceedings. The first requisite is that a “right” must be
established, even if open to doubt. If not, then there can be no interim interdict, just as
there cannot be a final interdict in due course. If the right is established, albeit open to
doubt, then the next question arises. The second requisite is that that right is being
breached, or that a breach of it is anticipated. If no breach, again, no interdict. If a
breach is occurring, or is anticipated, the next question arises. The third requisite is
that the grant of a final interdict or other relief in the main proceedings will not be
able to make good the interference with that right in the period until the right is finally
established. It is in this sense that the harm must be irreparable.
[142] This question arises only because there is a right, and because it is being
breached, or its breach is feared, with the consequence that an interdict will be
granted. Without the first two findings there can be no interdict.
[143] The third question – about irreparability – arises only because it has already
been found, albeit open to some doubt, that there has been an unlawful act that will
warrant a final interdict in due course. So the third question is not: is there harm?

The Court had to decide whether every provision of the New Text complied with the 34 Constitutional Principles in Schedule 4 to the Interim Constitution.
	Issue
	Whether the Constitutional Court could certify that all provisions of the proposed Final Constitution complied with the Constitutional Principles agreed during the negotiated transition.
	Court reasoning / legal reasoning
	The Court explained that certification was a judicial, not political, function. It compared the New Text to the Constitutional Principles, not to political preferences. The Court held that the CPs had to be interpreted purposively, holistically and without technical rigidity. The Court first asked whether the basic structure and premises of the New Text accorded with the CPs; then it examined whether the detailed provisions complied. The Court accepted the basic structure but found some details non-compliant.
	Judgment / outcome
	The Court refused to certify the first text as it stood because several provisions did not comply with the Constitutional Principles. The text had to go back to the Constitutional Assembly for amendment.
	Constitutional significance
	This judgment is central to the legitimacy of the 1996 Constitution. It proves that the Final Constitution was not merely a political deal; it was measured against a binding legal pact. It also shows how the two-stage transition balanced democratic legitimacy with negotiated safeguards.
	Exam use
	Use this case whenever asked about: the two-stage transition; the role of the Constitutional Court; the purpose of the 34 Constitutional Principles; why certification mattered; why the 1996 Constitution has special legitimacy; or why the Court’s task was legal rather than political.

4. Legislation / Constitutional Instrument Docket
📜 Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 200 of 1993 (“Interim Constitution” / IC)
Element
	Detailed notes
	Nature / status
	Interim constitutional statute; assented to 25 January 1994; commenced 27 April 1994; later repealed/replaced by the 1996 Constitution.
	Key provisions / content
	Key provisions for Theme 3 include: Preamble and Postamble; s 4 supremacy of the Constitution; Chapter 3 Bill of Rights; Chapter 5 ss 68-74 on the adoption of the new Constitution; s 71 certification; Chapter 7 creating the Constitutional Court; Schedule 4 Constitutional Principles; Government of National Unity provisions.
	Role in Theme 3
	The IC is the legal bridge between apartheid parliamentary sovereignty and the Final Constitution. It governed the country after the 1994 election and set the rules for how the Final Constitution would be drafted and certified.
	Exam use
	Use in any Theme 3 answer on the two-stage transition, the “bridge”, the Constitutional Assembly, certification, Constitutional Principles, and the shift from parliamentary sovereignty to constitutional supremacy.
	Warning / nuance
	Do not confuse the Interim Constitution with the Final Constitution. The IC was drafted by unelected negotiators; the Final Constitution was drafted by an elected Constitutional Assembly and certified by the Constitutional Court.

📜 Chapter 5 of the Interim Constitution: ss 68-74
Element
	Detailed notes
	Nature / status
	Specific chapter regulating the adoption of the new constitutional text.
	Key provisions / content
	s 68 created the Constitutional Assembly by requiring the National Assembly and Senate to sit jointly for constitution-making. s 71 required Constitutional Court certification. s 73 regulated adoption of the new text, including the two-thirds majority requirement and fallback mechanisms. s 74 protected the Constitutional Principles and Chapter 5 from easy amendment.
	Role in Theme 3
	Chapter 5 is the legal engine of the second stage of the transition. It converts the political compromise into legal procedure.
	Exam use
	Use when asked to explain the rules for drafting the Final Constitution. Mention Constitutional Assembly = NA + Senate sitting jointly; two-year deadline; two-thirds support; certification; referendum fallback if relevant.
	Warning / nuance
	Exam trap: do not say CODESA drafted the Final Constitution. CODESA/MPNF produced the negotiated framework and IC; the elected Constitutional Assembly drafted the Final Constitution.

📜 Schedule 4 to the Interim Constitution: the 34 Constitutional Principles
Element
	Detailed notes
	Nature / status
	Binding principles agreed during negotiations and entrenched in the IC.
	Key provisions / content
	The Principles required, among other things: one sovereign democratic state; common citizenship; fundamental rights protected by entrenched and justiciable provisions; prohibition of discrimination; constitutional supremacy; equality before the law; separation of powers; independent judiciary; representative government; universal adult suffrage; common voters’ roll; proportional representation; freedom of information; formal legislative procedures; language and cultural diversity; traditional leadership and customary law; multi-level government; provincial and local powers; fiscal arrangements; independent institutions; non-partisan public service; security forces acting in the national interest; GNU until 1999; no national election before 30 April 1999 unless specific conditions arose; and self-determination possibilities.
	Role in Theme 3
	They solved the legitimacy/minority-fear deadlock: the elected body would draft the Final Constitution, but it had to remain within negotiated binding principles.
	Exam use
	Use as the heart of the two-stage transition answer. They are the legal benchmark used by the Constitutional Court in certification.
	Warning / nuance
	Critical phrase: the CPs were a “solemn pact”. Do not describe them as optional guidelines.

5. Constitutional Principles Bank
🧠 What the 34 Constitutional Principles did

They were not ordinary political promises. They were legally binding benchmarks in Schedule 4 of the Interim Constitution. The Constitutional Assembly could choose among permissible models, but it could not draft a Final Constitution inconsistent with the Constitutional Principles. The Constitutional Court was the independent arbiter.

Principle(s)
	Exam-ready meaning
	I
	One sovereign state, common citizenship, democratic government, equality between men and women and all races.
	II
	Universally accepted fundamental rights, freedoms and civil liberties protected by entrenched and justiciable constitutional provisions.
	III
	Prohibition of racial, gender and other discrimination; promotion of racial and gender equality and national unity.
	IV
	Constitution as supreme law, binding all organs of state at all levels.
	V
	Equality before the law and equitable legal process, including affirmative measures.
	VI
	Separation of powers with checks and balances for accountability, responsiveness and openness.
	VII
	Independent, impartial judiciary with power to safeguard the Constitution and rights.
	VIII
	Representative government, multi-party democracy, regular elections, universal adult suffrage, common voters’ roll and proportional representation.
	IX-XV
	Open administration, formal legislative procedures, language/culture protection, civil society rights, traditional leadership, minority participation and special amendment procedures.
	XVI-XXVII
	Government structured at national, provincial and local levels; allocation of powers; provincial autonomy; local government; fiscal powers and equitable share.
	XXVIII-XXXI
	Labour rights, independent institutions, public service, and security forces serving the national interest.
	XXXII-XXXIV
	GNU until 1999, no early national election unless specified, and possible community self-determination within the broader national self-determination framework.

6. Exam Use: How to Write Theme 3 Answers
✍️ Model answer skeleton: two-stage transition

Intro: South Africa’s transition was not a simple handover of power; it was a negotiated constitutional revolution structured through a two-stage transition.
Point 1: Explain legitimacy problem at CODESA - unelected negotiators lacked democratic mandate.
Point 2: Explain minority fear problem - old government wanted safeguards, power-sharing and supermajorities.
Point 3: Explain compromise - Stage 1 IC by negotiators; Stage 2 Final Constitution by elected Constitutional Assembly.
Point 4: Explain safeguards - 34 CPs + Constitutional Court certification.
Conclusion: The 1996 Constitution’s legitimacy comes from both democratic election and legal compliance with negotiated principles.

✍️ Model answer skeleton: First Certification

Intro: The First Certification judgment was the legal mechanism that ensured the Final Constitution complied with the negotiated settlement.
Facts/context: Constitutional Assembly adopted New Text; it was sent to the Constitutional Court.
Issue: Did all provisions comply with the 34 Constitutional Principles?
Reasoning: The Court’s task was judicial, not political; it interpreted CPs purposively and holistically; it assessed both structure and details.
Outcome: Basic structure complied, but some detailed provisions did not; the Court refused certification.
Significance: Certification protected constitutional supremacy, democratic legitimacy and negotiated safeguards.

Use this for any answer on foundational values, limitation of rights, dignity, judicial review, constitutional supremacy, or value-

Legal theme Value-based constitutionalism; limitation of rights; human dignity,
equality and freedom; judicial review of legislation.
Facts Makwanyane and Mchunu were convicted of murder and
sentenced to death under s 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure
Act 51 of 1977. They challenged the constitutionality of the death
penalty under the 1993 Interim Constitution. The case became the
first major constitutional review of legislation by the new
Constitutional Court.
Issue Whether the death penalty was compatible with the rights to life,
dignity and freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment,
and whether it could be justified under the limitation clause.
Court reasoning The Court treated the Constitution as a value-based document. It
did not only ask what the statute allowed; it asked whether the
death penalty could survive a constitutional system founded on
dignity, equality and freedom. The State relied on deterrence,
prevention, retribution and public opinion. The Court held that
public opinion cannot decide constitutional rights. Rights must be
interpreted through constitutional values. The death penalty
objectifies the condemned person and places the person outside
the protection of humanity.
Judgment / outcome The death penalty was declared unconstitutional and invalid.
Section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act was invalid to the
extent that it authorised capital punishment.
Constitutional significance Foundational case for value-based constitutionalism. It shows that
courts can invalidate legislation inconsistent with the Constitution;
rights are not absolute but limitations must be reasonable and
justifiable; human dignity is central to constitutional interpretation.

General Purpose of a Constitution

* Definition and Function: A constitution is defined as the fundamental law of a state. Its primary functions include:
   * Outlining Structures: Establishing the framework and structure of the government.
   * Defining and Limiting Power: Clearly articulating the powers of government branches and setting boundaries on those powers.
   * Guaranteeing Rights: Protecting and ensuring the fundamental rights of citizens.

Form: Codified vs. Uncodified
* Codified Constitutions:
   * Definition: A written constitution where the fundamental laws are contained within a single, formal document.
   * Examples: South Africa, USA, and France.
* Uncodified Constitutions:
   * Definition: Also referred to as an unwritten constitution. The constitutional laws are not found in one document but are spread across various legislations, customs, and common law.
   * Examples: United Kingdom (UK) and New Zealand.
Amendment Process: Rigid vs. Flexible
* Rigid Constitutions:
   * Definition: A constitution that requires special, often difficult, procedures to be amended.
   * Correction of Misconception: "Rigid" does not mean the constitution cannot be amended at all; it simply means the process is more rigorous than that of ordinary legislation.
   * Examples: USA and South Africa.
* Flexible Constitutions:
   * Definition: A constitution that can be amended through the same process used for ordinary legislation or by ordinary legislation itself.
   * Procedural Similarity: There is no special legal distinction between the process for natural law-making and constitutional amendment.
Other Categories
* Power Structures: Constitutions can be classified as Unitary or Federal based on how power is distributed geographically and institutionally.
* Application/Status: Constitutions can be classified as Supreme or Subordinate based on their legal authority relative to other laws.

Comprehensive Scope of the A2 S1 Assessment

Specific Examinable Content and Guest Lectures
* Guest Lectures: Content delivered by guest lecturers is fully examinable and should not be overlooked.
* Separation of Powers: Students must specifically review the guest lectures concerning the Separation of Powers.
* Constitutional Knowledge: The assessment will require a deep pull from knowledge of the Constitution of South Africa.
* Constitutional Rights: Students must study the various rights outlined within the Constitution.
* Case Law Requirements:
   * All cases mentioned during the course are examinable to the extent they were discussed in class.
   * Prescribed readings and cases, such as the EFF (Economic Freedom Fighters) case, are mandatory for the assessment.
   * Students are instructed to complete all prescribed readings to ensure full preparation for case-based questions.
Assessment Structure and Question Categories
* Format Consistency: The structure of the A2 is very similar to the A1 assessment.
* Question Types: The exam will consist of multiple-classifications of questions, including:
   * Multiple Choice: Testing broad knowledge and specific facts.
   * Knowledge-Based Questions: Assessing the memorization and understanding of core principles.
   * Case-Based Questions: Requiring the application of legal principles to specific sets of facts or previous court rulings.
   * Critical Engagement Questions: Testing the student's ability to analyze, critique, and provide deeper insights into the course material.
Administrative Procedures and Communication
* Inquiry Etiquette: Any questions sent via email must be polite. The lecturer noted receiving rude emails regarding the scope and reaffirmed that the scope is non-negotiable and cannot be changed.
* Correction of Marks (Technical Questions):
   * If there are technical issues regarding the calculation or adding up of marks from previous assessments, students must contact the module assistant.
   * Deadline: The final deadline for mark changes or technical inquiries is Wednesday, May 20.
* Content Consultations:
   * The lecturer is open to discussions and consultations regarding course content up until the date of the A2.
   * The Caveat for Consultations: Students must arrive with specific questions. The lecturer will not "reteach" entire concepts (e.g., a student cannot simply say they "do not understand transformative constitutionalism" without specific points of confusion).
* Exam Logistics: The exam date and time have been communicated by the central university administration.
