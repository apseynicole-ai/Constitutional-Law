A. The Purpose of a Constitution
1. Purpose of a constitution
A constitution serves as the supreme legal framework of a state.[1] A constitution sets out government structures, defines the powers and activities of the state’s institutions, determines how public authority must be executed and it limits governmental power by subjecting all law and conduct to constitutional control. [2]
2. Types Of Constitutions
Flexible V Rigid

Constitutions may be classified according to the ease with which they can be amended.[3] A flexible constitution can be amended through ordinary legislative processes and does not require special procedures or enhanced majorities. By contrast, a rigid (or inflexible) constitution requires special amendment procedures, such as special majorities or referenda, before it may be changed.[4] The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa (1996) is a rigid constitution and sets out how amendments can be made in section 74.[5]

3. Constitutional Law V Other Branches Of Law In South Africa

Constitutional law differs from other branches of law, because it is the supreme law of the Republic and regulates the structure, powers and limits of the state itself.[6] Section 2 of the Constitution states that any law or conduct inconsistent with it is invalid.[7] Unlike private law, which governs relationships between individuals, constitutional law governs public power and ensures that all other branches of law operate within constitutional limits. [8]

B. Constitutionalism
4. Define Constitutionalism
Constitutionalism is the principle that governmental power comes from and is limited by the constitution, meaning that the government is not above the law and is constrained by a higher legal framework.[9] Constitutionalism requires all public authority to comply and uphold the law and if do not act in accordance with the law they can be subject to sanctions, which thereby prevents the abuse of power.[10]

5. Explain what it means in the South African context.
In the South African context, constitutionalism reflects the shift from parliamentary supremacy under apartheid to constitutional supremacy under the 1996 Constitution.[11] The South African Constitution states that all public powers must now comply with the Constitution and its foundational values of dignity, equality and freedom.[12]
6. Explain the concept of constitutional supremacy.
Constitutional supremacy means that the Constitution is the highest law of the Republic.[13] Section 2 of the constitution states that any law or conduct that is inconsistent with the constitution is invalid.[14] Courts are empowered to review legislation and executive action to ensure compliance all is compliant with the Constitution.[15] This places the Constitution above Parliament and all other branches of government.
7. Briefly explain why South Africa is sometimes described as ‘quasi-federal’.
South Africa is sometimes described as quasi-federal because the South African governmental authority is divided into national, provincial and local spheres that are interdependent and interrelated.[16] Provinces in South Africa have been allocated constitutional powers, but sovereignty ultimately rests in the Constitution rather than in the provinces themselves.[17] The system therefore contains federal features without constituting a classic federation and that is why it can be called ‘quasi-federal’.

C. Separation of Powers and State Institutions
8. Three branches of government in South Africa.
The three branches of government in South Africa are the legislature, executive, and judiciary.[18]
9. The primary function of each branch of government.
The legislature branch makes legislation and overseas the executive branch.[19] The executive branch adopts and implements government policy and administers state affairs.[20] The judiciary branch interprets and applies the law, including enforcing the compliance to the constitution when law or conduct is unconstitutional.[21]
10. Principle of separation of powers.
The principle of the separation of powers is that government power is divided among three branches so that each performs different functions and remains institutionally independent, which prevents the misuse of power in one branch.[22] This division also supports accountability because each branch can act as a check on the others, reducing the risk of authoritarian control or abuse.[23]

D. The Preamble and the Injustices of the Past (6)
11. Purpose of a preamble (with reference to City of Tshwane Metropolitan Municipality v Afriforum.)
In City of Tshwane, the Court describes a preamble as a summarised statement that sets out a constitution’s purpose and underlying philosophy.[24] In the South African constitution, the Preamble is used to express the Constitution’s commitments, such as recognising past injustice and committing to healing divisions.[25]
12. What is meant by the “injustices of the past” (2)
“Injustices of the past” refers to the systemic harms created by colonialism and apartheid, including racial domination, exclusion, and the privileging of one group’s history and identity over others.[26]
13. How have these injustices influenced the structure, values and rights contained in the Constitution? (2)
The structure, values and rights contained in the Constitution have been influenced by the injustices of the past which can be seen in the Preamble which explicitly recognises past injustices and commits the state to healing divisions, social justice, and fundamental human rights.[27] Second, they influence the Constitution’s structure and legal design, including constitutional supremacy (so that abuses of public power can be challenged and struck down) and a rights framework aimed at transforming society rather than maintaining historical inequality.[28]

1 Journey to the 1996 Constitution
The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996, was the product of a
negotiated constitutional transition and was structured through a two-stage process to
balance democratic legitimacy and political compromise.
The historic transition began with the 1st stage of the constitutional process. During
the 1st stage, the Interim Constitution of 1993 was adopted through negotiations
between the apartheid government, the National Party, and the liberation movements
to facilitate the transition. The transition occurred due to South Africa’s history as a
deeply divided society characterised by conflict, injustice and racial domination, as
recognised in the Certification Judgement. 1 The Negotiations formally began in 1990
to avoid civil conflict and establish a democratic order. 2
During negotiations, a deadlock arose because the negotiating parties were not
democratically elected, and minority groups feared domination by majority rule. 3 As
explained in the Certification judgment, this impasse was resolved through a
compromise. The compromise provided that an interim government would govern the
country, while a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly would draft the final
Constitution. 4
The Interim Constitution also included binding Constitutional Principles, which
ensured that the final Constitution would reflect agreed fundamental values and
protect minority interests. 5 The Constitutional Assembly, elected through universal
suffrage, drafted and adopted the 1996 Constitution, which only came into force after
certification by the Constitutional Court. 6
The Constitution therefore resulted from a negotiated and legally structured
transition, balancing democratic legitimacy with political compromise.

2 Certification Of The Constitution
In Certification of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 (4) SA 744
(CC), the Constitutional Court made it clear that its role in the certification process was
strictly legal rather than political because it possessed a judicial and not a political mandate,
which makes the certification task a strictly legal exercise.
The Court held that its opinion on the political process was irrelevant because its
task was limited to determining whether the new constitutional text complied with the
Constitutional Principles.7
The new constitutional text’s authority is derived from the Interim Constitution, which
required it to assess compliance with pre-agreed legal standards rather than the
fairness or desirability of the negotiations that produced the Constitution.8
The Court emphasised that certification required an objective legal evaluation that
was independent of the political compromises underlying the drafting process.9 It
further stressed that all provisions of the Constitution had to comply with the
Constitutional Principles, regardless of the level of political agreement or support.10 In
this sense, the Court’s role was to enforce what it described as the “solemn pact”
embodied in the Constitutional Principles.11
This approach reflects the principle of constitutional supremacy, ensuring that the
validity of the Constitution is determined by legal standards rather than political
considerations. The Court therefore excluded the political process from its reasoning
in order to act as an independent legal arbiter ensuring strict compliance with the
Constitutional Principles.

3 The constitution’s transformative nature
The Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996, is widely regarded as a
transformative constitution because it seeks to fundamentally reshape South African
society rather than merely regulate it.
First, the Constitution represents a decisive break from apartheid, replacing a
system of racial domination with one based on human rights, democracy and dignity.
As emphasised by Langa, the Constitution provides a framework for transforming
society and addressing the inequalities of the past.12
Second, the Constitution embodies the idea of a “historic bridge” from a deeply
divided past to a democratic and inclusive future. Mureinik describes this transition as
a movement from a culture of authority to a culture of justification, in which all exercises
of public power must be justified.13
Third, the Constitution promotes substantive equality, requiring not only formal
equality before the law but also the redress of past disadvantage and the redistribution
of power and resources.14
Fourth, the Constitution mandates the transformation of state institutions and
society, requiring active efforts to restructure power relations and promote democratic
participation.15
Finally, transformation is understood as a long-term project that requires continuous
development to achieve dignity, equality and social justice.16
The Constitution is therefore transformative because it provides a framework for
deep structural change aimed at achieving equality, dignity and democratic
participation in South African society.

4 The constitution’s transformative nature
In Certification of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996 1996 (4) SA
744 (CC), the Constitutional Court was required to determine whether the new
constitutional text complied with the Constitutional Principles set out in the Interim
Constitution.
The Court found that not all provisions of the new constitutional text complied with
the Constitutional Principles, which was a requirement for certification.17 It emphasised
that complete compliance was required, rather than mere substantial compliance.18
The Court identified several structural deficiencies, including concerns relating to
the separation of powers, the allocation of powers between national and provincial
government, and the adequacy of institutional safeguards.19 It also stressed that its
task required it to evaluate every provision both individually and collectively against
the Constitutional Principles.20
Because these requirements were not fully satisfied, the Court refused to certify the
Constitution. This decision demonstrates the Court’s role in strictly enforcing the
Constitutional Principles as binding legal standards.

1 Vertical v Horizontal Application of the Bill of Rights
Vertical application concerns the relationship between the state and persons. In this
setting, the Bill of Rights binds the legislature, the executive, the judiciary and all
organs of state, as stated in section 8(1) of the Constitution.
1 The question this poses
is, therefore, whether the law or state conduct unjustifiably infringes a protected right.
For example, if a public school, municipality, police official or government department
excludes a person from a benefit because of race, that conduct implicates the right to
equality. It may be attacked as unconstitutional state conduct.
2
Horizontal application concerns the relationship between private persons or private
entities. Section 8(2) of the Constitution recognises that a provision of the Bill of Rights
can bind a natural or juristic person if, and to the extent that, it is applicable, having
regard to the nature of the right and the nature of the duty imposed by that right.
3
Section 8(3) then instructs courts, when applying a right horizontally, to give effect to
that right by applying or, where necessary, developing the common law consistently
with constitutional values.4
The right to equality is a useful example. Vertically, a person may rely on section 9
where the state discriminates unfairly. Horizontally, a person may rely on equality
where a private actor or entity discriminates unfairly, because section 9(4) expressly
states that no person may unfairly discriminate directly or indirectly against anyone.5

2 Representative and Participatory Democracy in South Africa
South Africa is primarily a representative constitutional democracy, but the
Constitution also contains participatory and limited direct democratic features.
Representative democracy is the dominant institutional form, meaning citizens do not
ordinarily make national laws themselves, but elect representatives who exercise
public power through constitutional institutions.
The representative character of the South African democracy is clear from section
1(d), which founds the Republic on universal adult suffrage, a national common voters’
roll, regular elections and a multi-party democratic government system.7 Section 19
gives every citizen political rights, including the right to vote in elections and stand for
public office.8 Parliament represents the people and provides a national forum for

3 Value-Based Constitutionalism and the Culture of Justification
Value-based constitutionalism means that the Constitution is not merely a set of
institutional rules, but a normative legal order structured around constitutional values.
These values guide the creation, interpretation, application and limitation of law.
Section 1 entrenches founding values such as human dignity, the achievement of
equality, the advancement of human rights and freedoms, non-racialism, non-sexism,
constitutional supremacy, the rule of law, universal adult suffrage, accountability,
responsiveness and openness.13 The Bill of Rights further affirms the democratic
values of human dignity, equality and freedom.
