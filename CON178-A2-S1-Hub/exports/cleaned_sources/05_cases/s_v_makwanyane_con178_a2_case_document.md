**Case:** *S v Makwanyane and Another* 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC); 1995 (6) BCLR 665 (CC)

**Case number:** CCT 3/94
**Heard:** 15–17 February 1995
**Delivered:** 6 June 1995
**Primary judgment:** Chaskalson P
**Context:** Interim Constitution of the Republic of South Africa Act 200 of 1993

## 2. One-sentence case summary

*Makwanyane* held that the death penalty, as authorised by section 277 of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977, was unconstitutional because it amounted to cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment and could not be justified under the Interim Constitution’s limitation clause.

---

## 3. Quick case box

- Element CON178 A2 answer
- **Facts** The accused were convicted of serious offences, including four counts of murder, and sentenced to death. Their appeal raised whether the death penalty was compatible with the 1993 Interim Constitution.
- **Issue** Whether section 277 of the Criminal Procedure Act, which allowed capital punishment, was consistent with the Interim Constitution, especially rights to life, dignity, equality, and freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment.
- **Holding** The death penalty was unconstitutional. Section 277(1)(a), (c), (d), (e) and (f), and corresponding provisions, were invalid to the extent that they authorised capital punishment.
- **Core reasoning** The death penalty destroys life, annihilates dignity, creates risks of arbitrariness and error, and cannot be justified merely by public opinion, deterrence, prevention, or retribution.
- **Exam significance** The case is the foundation of South African proportionality analysis and shows that constitutional rights must be interpreted through values, not through formal legality or majoritarian preference.

---

## 4. Facts and procedural background

The two accused, **Themba Makwanyane** and **M Mchunu**, were convicted in the Witwatersrand Local Division of the Supreme Court on:

- four counts of murder;
- one count of attempted murder; and
- one count of robbery with aggravating circumstances.

They were sentenced to death on each murder count and received long imprisonment terms on the other counts. They appealed to the Appellate Division. The Appellate Division dismissed the conviction appeals and considered the murders serious enough to attract the heaviest lawful sentence. However, because the 1993 Interim Constitution had come into force after the trial and sentence, the Appellate Division postponed the death-sentence aspect so that the Constitutional Court could decide the constitutional issues.

The key statutory provision was **section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977**, which made the death penalty a competent sentence for murder.

The core constitutional provisions under the Interim Constitution were:

- **section 8** — equality before the law and equal protection of the law;
- **section 9** — right to life;
- **section 10** — dignity;
- **section 11(2)** — prohibition of torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment;
- **section 33** — limitation clause;
- **section 35** — interpretation of Chapter 3 rights;
- **section 241(8)** — transitional issue about whether Chapter 3 could apply to pending proceedings.

---

## 5. Issues before the Constitutional Court

The case raised four major issues:

1. **Constitutionality of the death penalty:** whether capital punishment was inconsistent with Chapter 3 of the Interim Constitution.
2. **Rights implicated:** whether the death penalty violated life, dignity, equality, and the prohibition of cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment.
3. **Limitation:** if those rights were limited, whether the limitation was justifiable under section 33 of the Interim Constitution.
4. **Transitional effect:** whether people sentenced before the Interim Constitution could rely on Chapter 3 to prevent execution after the Constitution came into force.

For A2, the most important issue is not the criminal-law detail. The key issue is:

> **Can the state intentionally take life as punishment in a constitutional order founded on human dignity, equality, freedom and constitutional supremacy?**

---

## 6. Applicants’ argument

The applicants argued that the death penalty was unconstitutional because it violated several fundamental rights:

- the **right to life**;
- the **right to dignity**;
- the prohibition of **physical, mental and emotional torture**;
- the prohibition of **cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment or punishment**.

They argued that these violations could not be justified under section 33 because the death penalty:

1. **negated the essential content** of the rights to life and dignity;
2. was **not necessary**;
3. was **not reasonable and justifiable** in an open and democratic society based on freedom and equality;
4. was **arbitrary**, because the decision between life and death could depend on chance factors in investigation, prosecution, defence quality, or judicial attitude;
5. was **unequal**, because race, poverty, and social inequality shaped who was most vulnerable in capital cases;
6. was **fallible**, because a wrongful execution cannot be corrected;
7. did **not use the least drastic means**, because life imprisonment could serve the legitimate aims of punishment without killing the offender;
8. had a **disproportionately severe effect** because it permanently destroyed all rights of the person executed.

They also relied on the international trend away from the death penalty and on the growing constitutional emphasis on life, dignity, and protection against cruel punishment.

### Applicant-side exam formulation

> The applicants’ strongest point was that death is different. Ordinary punishment may limit rights, but capital punishment extinguishes the rights-holder permanently. Because execution is final and irreversible, ordinary safeguards cannot make it constitutionally acceptable.

---

## 7. Respondent’s argument

The respondent, represented by the Attorney-General, defended the constitutionality of the death penalty.

The respondent argued that:

1. the death penalty had developed from a mandatory punishment into a discretionary sentence;
2. post-1990 reforms created safeguards, including consideration of mitigating and aggravating factors and automatic appeal or review mechanisms;
3. the death penalty served legitimate aims of punishment, especially **deterrence**, **prevention**, and **retribution**;
4. South African society, given high violent crime levels, still regarded the death penalty as necessary in extreme cases;
5. the Constitution did not expressly abolish the death penalty, so the issue should not be treated as automatically resolved against retention;
6. public confidence in the criminal justice system required serious punishment for serious crimes;
7. the rights of victims and society should not be ignored merely because the condemned prisoner also had constitutional rights.

A central respondent theme was that the question was not merely whether death was abhorrent, but whether South African society could afford to do without it in extreme cases. The respondent’s heads also argued that if the Court invalidated capital punishment against prevailing social values, society might lose faith in the criminal justice system and resort to private vengeance.

### Respondent-side exam formulation

> The respondent’s strongest point was that constitutional rights do not exist in a vacuum: society also has an interest in protection from serious violent crime, and the criminal justice system must retain legitimacy by responding adequately to the gravest offences.

---

## 8. Court’s approach to constitutional interpretation

The Court treated the case as an early test of the new constitutional order. The Interim Constitution created a break from parliamentary sovereignty and established a supreme Constitution with justiciable rights.

Key interpretive principles:

1. **The Constitution is not ordinary legislation.** It is the source of public power and the standard against which law and conduct are measured.
2. **Rights must be interpreted generously and purposively.** The Court followed *S v Zuma* in adopting a rights-protective approach aimed at giving individuals the full benefit of constitutional protection.
3. **Rights must be read contextually.** Section 11(2) could not be interpreted in isolation. It had to be understood in the context of the rights to life, dignity, equality and the Constitution’s historical purpose.
4. **International and foreign law may assist but does not control.** The Court considered comparative law because section 35 allowed reference to public international law and comparable foreign case law, but emphasised that the South African Constitution must be interpreted according to South Africa’s own text, structure, history and values.

### Why this matters for A2

*Makwanyane* is not just a death-penalty case. It is a case about **how courts reason under a value-based Constitution**. Courts do not merely ask whether Parliament authorised something. They ask whether the law is consistent with constitutional values and rights.

---

## 9. The rights analysis

### 9.1 Cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment

The Court found that death is the most extreme punishment. It is final and irrevocable. It ends the right to life and all other personal rights. It is cruel because the condemned prisoner waits under uncertainty on death row. It is inhuman because it denies the humanity of the person executed. It is degrading because the state treats the person as an object to be eliminated.

The Court did not simply rely on ordinary dictionary meaning. It asked whether capital punishment was cruel, inhuman or degrading **in the constitutional sense**, interpreted through the rights and values of the Interim Constitution.

### 9.2 Life

The right to life was central. The Interim Constitution protected life in unqualified terms. The Court treated life as one of the most important rights because it is the source of all other personal rights.

### 9.3 Dignity

Dignity was equally central. Capital punishment strips the condemned person of dignity by reducing them to an object of state punishment. The state cannot model respect for life and dignity by deliberately destroying life and dignity.

### 9.4 Equality and arbitrariness

The Court recognised that capital cases are vulnerable to inequality and arbitrariness. Factors such as poverty, race, legal representation, prosecutorial approach, judicial attitude and investigative quality can affect who receives the death penalty. Imperfections exist in all criminal justice systems, but the Court emphasised that death is qualitatively different because errors cannot be corrected after execution.

---

## 10. Public opinion and constitutional supremacy

The Attorney-General relied heavily on public support for capital punishment. Chaskalson P was prepared to assume, for purposes of argument, that the majority of South Africans favoured the death penalty in extreme murder cases. But the Court held that this did not resolve the constitutional question.

The constitutional issue was not:

> What does the majority want?

The constitutional issue was:

> What does the Constitution permit?

This is one of the most examinable parts of the case.

### Rule

Public opinion may be relevant, but it is not a substitute for constitutional adjudication. If public opinion were decisive, rights protection could be left to Parliament or a referendum. That would return South Africa to parliamentary sovereignty and undermine the purpose of constitutional supremacy.

### Exam use

Use this point whenever a question asks about:

- constitutional supremacy vs parliamentary sovereignty;
- courts and democracy;
- counter-majoritarian difficulty;
- judicial review;
- rights protection for marginalised persons;
- whether courts should follow majority sentiment.

### Strong exam sentence

> *Makwanyane* shows that constitutional democracy is not simple majoritarianism: courts must uphold entrenched rights even when doing so may conflict with popular opinion.

## 11. The limitation analysis: section 33 and section 36

*Makwanyane* was decided under **section 33 of the Interim Constitution**, not section 36 of the 1996 Constitution. However, it remains central to section 36 because the First Certification judgment later explained that section 36 substantially reflects the limitation approach developed in *Makwanyane*.

### 11.1 The proportionality test

Chaskalson P held that limiting constitutional rights involves:

- weighing competing values;
- assessing proportionality;
- considering the nature and importance of the right;
- considering the purpose and importance of the limitation;
- considering the nature and extent of the limitation;
- considering the relation between the limitation and its purpose;
- considering whether less restrictive means could achieve the same purpose.

This became the backbone of modern South African limitation analysis.

### 11.2 Section 36 link

Under the 1996 Constitution, section 36 asks whether a limitation is reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on human dignity, equality and freedom, taking into account relevant factors such as:

1. the nature of the right;
2. the importance of the purpose of the limitation;
3. the nature and extent of the limitation;
4. the relation between the limitation and its purpose;
5. less restrictive means to achieve the purpose.

### 11.3 Application in Makwanyane

- Limitation factor Application to the death penalty
- **Nature of the right** Life and dignity are among the most important rights. Life is the source of all personal rights. Dignity is central to the new constitutional order.
- **Purpose of limitation** The state relied on deterrence, prevention, retribution, and public safety. These are legitimate and important aims.
- **Nature and extent of limitation** The limitation is total and irreversible: execution destroys life and all remaining rights.
- **Relation between means and purpose** The connection between death and deterrence was not proven to be materially stronger than life imprisonment.
- **Less restrictive means** Life imprisonment can protect society and punish severely without extinguishing life.
- **Overall proportionality** The state failed to make the clear and convincing case required to justify the death penalty.

---

## 12. The purposes of punishment

The Court considered the traditional purposes of punishment:

1. **Deterrence**
2. **Prevention**
3. **Retribution**
4. **Reformation**

### 12.1 Deterrence

The state argued that the death penalty deterred murder. The Court was not persuaded that death was materially more effective than life imprisonment as a deterrent. Because the right at stake was life itself, speculative deterrence could not justify the irreversible destruction of life.

### 12.2 Prevention

The state argued that execution prevents the offender from killing again. The Court accepted that prevention is important, but held that life imprisonment can also protect society by removing the offender from the community.

### 12.3 Retribution

The Court accepted that retribution is a recognised purpose of punishment. However, it gave retribution less constitutional weight than life and dignity. Constitutional punishment may express moral outrage, but it may not collapse into vengeance.

### 12.4 Reformation

Execution makes reformation impossible. A constitutional order committed to dignity must remain aware that punishment is not only about destruction or retaliation.

---

## 13. Why the death penalty failed the limitation test

The death penalty failed because the Court weighed the state’s aims against the constitutional cost of execution.

### Factors against constitutionality

- Death permanently destroys life.
- Death annihilates dignity.
- Death is final and irreversible.
- Error cannot be corrected after execution.
- Capital sentencing contains risks of arbitrariness.
- Race, poverty and inequality affect criminal justice outcomes.
- Life imprisonment is a severe alternative punishment.
- Deterrence was not proven to be materially stronger than life imprisonment.
- Retribution cannot outweigh life and dignity.
- Public opinion cannot override constitutional rights.

### Core conclusion

The state did not make a clear and convincing case that the death penalty was a reasonable and necessary limitation of rights.

---

## 14. The order

The Court declared invalid:

- section 277(1)(a), (c), (d), (e) and (f) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977; and
- corresponding provisions in force elsewhere in the national territory,

because they authorised capital punishment and were inconsistent with the Constitution.

The Court also ordered that:

- the State and all its organs were forbidden from executing anyone already sentenced to death under the invalid provisions; and
- those persons would remain in custody until their sentences were lawfully set aside and replaced with lawful punishments.

The Court did **not** decide the proper substituted sentence for Makwanyane and Mchunu. That was for the Appellate Division.

## 15. Concurring judgments: what to know for A2

The case contains many concurring judgments. For CON178 A2, you do not need to memorise every separate judgment in detail, but you should understand the most examinable themes.

### 15.1 Chaskalson P

The main judgment provides the core legal framework:

- generous and purposive interpretation;
- life, dignity and equality as giving meaning to cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment;
- public opinion is not decisive;
- proportionality under the limitation clause;
- death penalty unjustifiable.

### 15.2 Langa J and ubuntu

Langa J linked ubuntu to life, dignity, communality, mutual respect and co-responsibility. Ubuntu does not justify vengeance. It emphasises that every person has dignity and value, including those who commit serious crimes.

Use Langa J for:

- ubuntu;
- dignity;
- value-based constitutionalism;
- transformation;
- rejection of vengeance.

### 15.3 Mahomed J and the transformative Constitution

Mahomed J emphasised that the South African Constitution is not merely a technical legal instrument. It represents a break from a painful past and commits the country to a different moral and legal future. This connects strongly to transformative constitutionalism.

Use Mahomed J for:

- constitutionalism as a new ethos;
- transition from past injustice;
- values disciplining state power;
- the Constitution as a moral and legal break from apartheid.

### 15.4 Mokgoro J and human dignity

Mokgoro J emphasised human dignity and ubuntu as constitutional values. Her reasoning helps connect *Makwanyane* to the broader idea that the Constitution must be interpreted through values rooted in South Africa’s own context.

### 15.5 Sachs J and constitutional history

Sachs J placed the case in historical context. His judgment shows why the new constitutional order rejected practices that brutalised society and diminished respect for life.

Use Sachs J for:

- history and constitutional values;
- rejection of state brutality;
- life and dignity after apartheid;
- constitutionalism as a response to abuse of power.

   - relation between limitation and purpose;

## 18. Common mistakes to avoid

### Mistake 1: Saying Makwanyane was decided under section 36

Incorrect. It was decided under **section 33 of the Interim Constitution**. But it remains central to section 36 because section 36 substantially reflects the proportionality approach developed in the case.

### Mistake 2: Saying the Court ignored victims

Incorrect. The Court recognised deterrence, prevention, retribution and public safety as legitimate aims. It held that those aims did not justify the irreversible destruction of life and dignity.

### Mistake 3: Saying public opinion is irrelevant

Too broad. Public opinion may be relevant, but it is **not decisive**. Courts must enforce the Constitution.

### Mistake 4: Treating the case as only about death penalty

Too narrow. The case is also about constitutional interpretation, limitation, dignity, life, public opinion, democracy, constitutional supremacy and transformation.

### Mistake 5: Forgetting the alternative of life imprisonment

The availability of life imprisonment was central to the proportionality analysis because it showed that the state had a severe but less rights-destructive alternative.

### Mistake 6: Ignoring arbitrariness and error

The Court did not only say death is harsh. It also focused on the risk that capital punishment may operate unequally or mistakenly in a society shaped by poverty, race and unequal access to legal resources.

## 19. Exam answer templates

### 19.1 Five-mark answer: limitation analysis

*Makwanyane* is the leading case on proportionality in South African limitation analysis. Although decided under section 33 of the Interim Constitution, it informs section 36 of the 1996 Constitution. Chaskalson P held that limiting rights requires weighing competing values and assessing proportionality. Relevant factors include the nature and importance of the right, the purpose and importance of the limitation, the extent of the limitation, the relation between the limitation and its purpose, and whether less restrictive means are available. Applied to the death penalty, the Court held that deterrence, prevention and retribution could not justify the destruction of life and dignity, especially because life imprisonment was available as a severe alternative.

### 19.2 Ten-mark answer: death penalty and constitutional values

In *S v Makwanyane*, the Constitutional Court considered whether section 277 of the Criminal Procedure Act, which allowed capital punishment, was consistent with the Interim Constitution. The accused had been sentenced to death after being convicted of murder, and the Appellate Division referred the constitutional issue to the Constitutional Court. The Court held that the death penalty implicated the rights to life, dignity, equality and freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment. Chaskalson P reasoned that death is final and irreversible, destroys all personal rights, strips the offender of dignity, and carries risks of arbitrariness and error. Although the state relied on deterrence, prevention, retribution and public opinion, the Court held that public opinion cannot replace constitutional adjudication and that retribution cannot carry the same weight as life and dignity. Applying proportionality, the Court found that the state had not shown that the death penalty was materially more effective than life imprisonment. The death penalty was therefore unconstitutional. The case is significant because it established proportionality as the core of limitation analysis and confirmed South Africa’s break from parliamentary sovereignty to value-based constitutional supremacy.

### 19.3 Critical engagement answer: public opinion and democracy

*Makwanyane* is especially important for understanding the relationship between democracy and rights. The Attorney-General argued that many South Africans supported the death penalty, especially in serious murder cases. The Court accepted that public opinion may be relevant but held that it cannot be decisive. If rights depended only on majority preference, constitutional adjudication would be unnecessary and South Africa would return to parliamentary sovereignty. The purpose of a supreme Constitution and a justiciable Bill of Rights is to protect all people, including unpopular and marginalised persons. This does not make the Court anti-democratic. It reflects constitutional democracy: majority rule operates within limits set by dignity, equality, freedom and the rule of law.
