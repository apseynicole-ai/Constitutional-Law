# 3. Detailed case bank

## ⚖️ S v Makwanyane 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for any answer on foundational values, limitation of rights, dignity, judicial review, constitutional supremacy, or value-based interpretation.

- **Legal theme** Value-based constitutionalism; limitation of rights; human dignity, equality and freedom; judicial review of legislation.
- **Facts** Makwanyane and Mchunu were convicted of murder and sentenced to death under s 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977. They challenged the constitutionality of the death penalty under the 1993 Interim Constitution. The case became the first major constitutional review of legislation by the new Constitutional Court.
- **Issue** Whether the death penalty was compatible with the rights to life, dignity and freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment, and whether it could be justified under the limitation clause.
- **Court reasoning** The Court treated the Constitution as a value-based document. It did not only ask what the statute allowed; it asked whether the death penalty could survive a constitutional system founded on dignity, equality and freedom. The State relied on deterrence, prevention, retribution and public opinion. The Court held that public opinion cannot decide constitutional rights. Rights must be interpreted through constitutional values. The death penalty objectifies the condemned person and places the person outside the protection of humanity.
- **Judgment / outcome** The death penalty was declared unconstitutional and invalid. Section 277(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act was invalid to the extent that it authorised capital punishment.
- **Constitutional significance** Foundational case for value-based constitutionalism. It shows that courts can invalidate legislation inconsistent with the Constitution; rights are not absolute but limitations must be reasonable and justifiable; human dignity is central to constitutional interpretation.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Interim Constitution ss 9, 10, 11(2), 33; Constitution 1996 ss 10, 11, 12, 36, 39; Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977 s 277(1)(a).
- **How to write it in the exam** “In S v Makwanyane, the Constitutional Court used the values of human dignity, equality and freedom to hold that the death penalty could not be justified in a constitutional democracy, even if public opinion supported it.”

## ⚖️ S v Walters

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this when asked “explain the limitation clause” or “how do courts decide if a limitation is reasonable and justifiable?”.

- **Legal theme** Limitation clause; two-stage enquiry; interpretation of rights and impugned law.
- **Facts** The Court considered whether a statutory provision limiting constitutional rights could survive constitutional scrutiny. The case is used in the lecture for the mechanics of the limitation enquiry.
- **Issue** How must a court determine whether a right has been limited and, if so, whether that limitation is constitutionally justifiable?
- **Court reasoning** The Court explained a two-stage process. First, identify the content and scope of the right and the meaning/effect of the impugned law to see whether there is a limitation. If there is no limitation, the challenge fails. If there is a limitation, the second stage is the limitations exercise: weigh the nature and importance of the right and the extent of limitation against the purpose and importance of the limiting law.
- **Judgment / outcome** The case provides the analytical structure for s 36 limitation analysis rather than merely stating an outcome for Theme 4 purposes.
- **Constitutional significance** High-yield because it gives the exam method for limitation questions: right infringed first, justification second.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 36 and 39.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Following S v Walters, the limitation analysis is a two-stage enquiry: first determine whether the right is limited; then determine whether the limitation is reasonable and justifiable under s 36.”

## ⚖️ Daniels v Campbell 2004 (5) SA 331 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for value-based constitutionalism, statutory interpretation, diversity, equality and religious/cultural inclusion.

- **Legal theme** Value-based interpretation of legislation; s 39(2); equality, tolerance and diversity.
- **Facts** The case concerned whether the word “spouse” in the Intestate Succession Act and the Maintenance of Surviving Spouses Act included a surviving spouse from a Muslim marriage.
- **Issue** Whether statutes should be interpreted broadly and inclusively to recognise a surviving spouse in a Muslim marriage.
- **Court reasoning** Justice Sachs reasoned that constitutional values of equality, tolerance and respect for diversity supported a broad and inclusive interpretation of “spouse”. The Court used constitutional values to interpret ordinary legislation in a way that promoted the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.
- **Judgment / outcome** The Court held that “spouse” included a surviving spouse to a Muslim marriage for the purposes of the relevant Acts.
- **Constitutional significance** Shows s 39(2) in action: courts interpret legislation through constitutional values instead of reading statutes narrowly in a way that excludes vulnerable groups.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 s 39(2); Intestate Succession Act 81 of 1987; Maintenance of Surviving Spouses Act 27 of 1990.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Daniels v Campbell illustrates that s 39(2) requires courts to interpret legislation in a way that promotes equality, tolerance and diversity.”

## ⚖️ Port Elizabeth Municipality v Various Occupiers 2005 (1) SA 217 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for values, limitation/balancing, housing, property, evictions, and the class task about municipal land.

- **Legal theme** Balancing competing rights through constitutional values; property vs housing; eviction justice.
- **Facts** A municipality sought the eviction of unlawful occupiers. The dispute required the Court to consider the interests of landowners/municipality and the occupiers’ vulnerability and potential homelessness.
- **Issue** How should courts balance property rights and housing/dignity interests when deciding whether eviction is just and equitable?
- **Court reasoning** Justice Sachs emphasised that eviction law must be interpreted in light of South Africa’s history of forced removals and homelessness. Fairness requires attention to both the owners’ rights and the occupiers’ dignity. The Constitution does not allow mechanical eviction; it requires a humane, context-sensitive balancing exercise.
- **Judgment / outcome** Eviction must only occur where it is just and equitable after considering all relevant circumstances.
- **Constitutional significance** Excellent case for showing that constitutional values operate when rights conflict. It connects dignity, housing, property, transformative constitutionalism and historical context.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 25 and 26; especially s 26(3) on evictions.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Port Elizabeth Municipality shows that courts balance competing rights by asking which outcome best reflects dignity, equality, freedom and South Africa’s history of dispossession.”

## ⚖️ Minister of Home Affairs v Fourie 2006 (1) SA 524 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for transformative constitutionalism, value-based interpretation, equality, dignity and the role of courts in protecting minorities.

- **Legal theme** Transformative constitutionalism; equality and dignity; social inclusion; values against majoritarian exclusion.
- **Facts** Same-sex couples were excluded from the institution of civil marriage. The applicants argued that this exclusion violated their rights to equality and dignity.
- **Issue** Whether excluding same-sex couples from marriage was constitutionally permissible.
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that exclusion from marriage rendered same-sex couples outsiders to a central social institution of love and commitment. Constitutional democracy requires equal concern and respect. Religious objections could not justify state exclusion because South Africa requires coexistence between sacred and secular spheres.
- **Judgment / outcome** The exclusion of same-sex couples from marriage was unconstitutional. Parliament was given time to correct the defect, leading to the Civil Union Act.
- **Constitutional significance** Shows values driving social transformation. It links equality, dignity, freedom, minority protection and the counter-majoritarian role of courts.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 9, 10, 15, 36, 39; Civil Union Act 17 of 2006 as legislative response.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Fourie shows that the Constitution aims not merely to regulate society but to transform social institutions so that previously excluded groups enjoy equal dignity and belonging.”

## ⚖️ Carmichele v Minister of Safety and Security 2001 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for direct vs indirect application and s 39(2) common-law development.

- **Legal theme** Indirect application of Bill of Rights; development of common law; state duties to protect rights.
- **Facts** Ms Carmichele was attacked by a man who had been released despite prior charges and warning signs. She claimed that police and prosecutors had failed to protect her. The question was whether the common law of delict should recognise liability in such circumstances.
- **Issue** Whether courts must develop the common law of delict to reflect constitutional rights and values.
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that courts have a duty under s 39(2) to develop the common law where it is deficient and inconsistent with the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. The Constitution influences private/common-law rules indirectly by requiring their development in light of rights.
- **Judgment / outcome** The matter was sent back for reconsideration in light of the constitutional duty to develop the common law.
- **Constitutional significance** Core case for indirect application. It shows that constitutional rights transform ordinary law without necessarily invalidating a statute.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 7(2), 8, 12, 39(2).
- **How to write it in the exam** “Carmichele is the key example of indirect application: instead of invalidating law, the Court required the common law to be developed to promote the Bill of Rights.”

## ⚖️ Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom 2000 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for vertical application, socio-economic rights, housing, reasonableness and transformative constitutionalism.

- **Legal theme** Vertical application of socio-economic rights; housing; reasonableness review.
- **Facts** A group of people living in desperate conditions sought emergency shelter and access to housing. They challenged the government’s housing programme.
- **Issue** Whether the state had complied with its constitutional obligation to take reasonable measures to progressively realise the right of access to adequate housing.
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that socio-economic rights are justiciable. The state does not have to provide a house immediately to everyone, but its programme must be reasonable and must make provision for people in crisis or desperate need.
- **Judgment / outcome** The government’s housing programme was constitutionally defective because it failed to provide for those in desperate need.
- **Constitutional significance** Core vertical application example. It shows courts reviewing state policy against the Bill of Rights and demanding reasonableness while respecting institutional limits.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 26 and 7(2).
- **How to write it in the exam** “Grootboom shows that socio-economic rights are enforceable and that government programmes must be reasonable, especially for those in desperate need.”

## ⚖️ Soobramoney v Minister of Health, KwaZulu-Natal 1997 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use this for transformative constitutionalism and the socio-economic context of the Constitution.

- **Legal theme** Transformative constitutionalism; socio-economic context; scarce resources; dignity, freedom and equality.
- **Facts** Mr Soobramoney suffered from chronic renal failure and sought access to dialysis at a public hospital. The hospital had limited resources and criteria for treatment.
- **Issue** Whether refusal to provide dialysis violated the rights to emergency medical treatment and access to health care.
- **Court reasoning** The Court recognised the severe inequality and poverty existing at the birth of the Constitution. It held that transformation lies at the heart of the constitutional order, but courts must consider resource constraints and institutional competence when assessing socio-economic rights.
- **Judgment / outcome** The claim failed because the treatment did not fall within emergency medical treatment and the hospital’s resource-based policy was not shown to be unconstitutional.
- **Constitutional significance** Often used for the famous statement that the Constitution is committed to transforming society into one based on human dignity, freedom and equality. It shows the promise and limits of socio-economic rights litigation.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 27 and 36; also linked to values in s 1.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Soobramoney shows that the transformative Constitution is both backward-looking and forward-looking: it responds to existing poverty while aiming toward dignity, freedom and equality.”

## ⚖️ Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly 2016 (CC) [Nkandla / EFF 1]

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use for rule of law, accountability, constitutional supremacy, legality, Chapter 9 institutions and separation of powers/accountability.

- **Legal theme** Rule of law; constitutional supremacy; accountability; Public Protector remedial action.
- **Facts** The Public Protector investigated spending on upgrades at President Zuma’s Nkandla residence and directed remedial action. The President and National Assembly did not comply and attempted to replace or second-guess the remedial action.
- **Issue** Whether the Public Protector’s remedial action was binding and whether the President and National Assembly acted inconsistently with the Constitution.
- **Court reasoning** The Court linked the rule of law, constitutional supremacy and accountability. It held that no decision grounded in the Constitution or law may be ignored merely because a person disagrees with it; recourse must be had to a court. Ignoring binding remedial action would amount to self-help, which is inconsistent with the rule of law.
- **Judgment / outcome** The remedial action was binding. The President’s non-compliance and the National Assembly resolution absolving him were unconstitutional and invalid.
- **Constitutional significance** Major Theme 4 rule of law case. Shows that even the President must obey the Constitution and lawful constitutional decisions.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 1(c), 2, 42(3), 55(2), 83(b), 181, 182.
- **How to write it in the exam** “EFF v Speaker confirms that constitutionalism, accountability and the rule of law prevent public officials from treating constitutional obligations as optional.”

## ⚖️ Fedsure Life Assurance Ltd v Greater Johannesburg Transitional Metropolitan Council 1998 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use for rule of law and legality definition.

- **Legal theme** Rule of law; legality; authorised public power.
- **Facts** A local government body exercised public powers affecting rates/levies. The case became an early authority on the principle that all public power must be sourced in law.
- **Issue** Whether organs of state may exercise powers beyond those conferred by law.
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that the rule of law includes the principle of legality: the legislature and executive in every sphere may exercise no power and perform no function beyond that conferred by law.
- **Judgment / outcome** The legality principle was affirmed as a constitutional control on public power.
- **Constitutional significance** Foundational legality case. It gives the basic exam formula: public power must be authorised by law.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 1(c) and 2.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Fedsure establishes that legality is part of the rule of law: every exercise of public power must be authorised by law.”

## ⚖️ Affordable Medicines Trust v Minister of Health 2005 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use when explaining that legality is not just administrative-law language; it is constitutional law.

- **Legal theme** Legality; control of public power; rationality.
- **Facts** Regulations and licensing powers in the medicines/health context were challenged. The case is used in Theme 4 for the general rule that public power must comply with the Constitution and legality.
- **Issue** What constitutional controls apply to the exercise of public power?
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that the exercise of public power must comply with the Constitution and the doctrine of legality. Legality is an incident of the rule of law and one of the constitutional mechanisms for controlling public power.
- **Judgment / outcome** The Court affirmed legality as a foundation for the control of public power.
- **Constitutional significance** Use with Fedsure and Albutt to build the rule of law/legality line of authority.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 1(c), 2 and 172.
- **How to write it in the exam** “Affordable Medicines confirms that legality is an incident of the rule of law and forms part of the constitutional control of public power.”

## ⚖️ Albutt v Centre for the Study of Violence and Reconciliation 2010 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use for legality, rationality, procedural fairness and public power.

- **Legal theme** Legality and rationality; procedural rationality; presidential pardons.
- **Facts** The President created a special process for pardons of offenders convicted of politically motivated crimes who had not received amnesty through the TRC process. Victims and families wanted to make representations before pardons were granted, but were excluded.
- **Issue** Whether victims had to be afforded an opportunity to be heard before the President could grant pardons under the special dispensation.
- **Court reasoning** The Court held that all public power must comply with legality and rationality. Because the purpose of the special dispensation was linked to national reconciliation, excluding victims from the process was irrational. Rationality can require a fair procedure where the process chosen must be rationally connected to the purpose.
- **Judgment / outcome** Victims had to be given an opportunity to make representations before pardons were granted.
- **Constitutional significance** Key rule of law case for rationality. It shows that legality is not only “is there authorisation?” but also whether the means chosen rationally connect to a legitimate governmental purpose.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 1(c), 2 and 84(2)(j).
- **How to write it in the exam** “Albutt shows that the exercise of public power must be rationally connected to its purpose; if reconciliation is the aim, excluding victims from the pardon process is irrational.”

## ⚖️ S v Dodo 2001 (3) SA 382 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use for separation of powers, judicial function and comparative law.

- **Legal theme** Separation of powers; sentencing; judicial independence; foreign law under s 39.
- **Facts** The case concerned mandatory minimum sentences prescribed by legislation and whether such sentencing laws impermissibly interfered with judicial sentencing discretion.
- **Issue** Whether mandatory minimum sentences violate the separation of powers by limiting the judiciary’s sentencing function.
- **Court reasoning** The Court considered comparative foreign law and observed that many constitutional democracies permit the legislature to set sentencing frameworks. Mere legislative involvement in sentencing does not automatically violate separation of powers. The key question is whether judicial power is undermined to an unconstitutional degree.
- **Judgment / outcome** Mandatory minimum sentencing does not, merely by existing, violate separation of powers.
- **Constitutional significance** Useful for separation of powers and s 39(1) foreign law. It shows that separation of powers does not require absolute separation; some overlap is constitutionally permissible.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 ss 12, 36, 39; Criminal Law Amendment Act minimum sentencing framework as background.
- **How to write it in the exam** “S v Dodo shows that separation of powers is not absolute: legislative regulation of sentencing is permissible if it does not destroy the judiciary’s constitutional role.”

## ⚖️ H v Fetal Assessment Centre 2015 (2) SA 193 (CC)

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use only if a question mentions international/foreign law or comparative interpretation.

- **Legal theme** Use of foreign law; development of common law; s 39.
- **Facts** The case concerned whether South African law should recognise a child’s claim relating to negligent failure to detect serious foetal abnormalities. It is used in the textbook extract for principles on using foreign law.
- **Issue** How should foreign law be used in constitutional interpretation and common-law development?
- **Court reasoning** The Court explained that foreign law can be useful when approaching constitutional problems, but it must be used carefully and contextually. South African courts must not transplant foreign rules without considering local constitutional values and conditions.
- **Judgment / outcome** The case developed the law cautiously while clarifying principles for comparative legal reasoning.
- **Constitutional significance** Not a core lecture case, but useful if a question asks about s 39(1)(c) and the role of foreign law.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Constitution 1996 s 39(1)(c) and s 39(2).
- **How to write it in the exam** “H v Fetal Assessment Centre reminds us that foreign law may assist constitutional reasoning, but South African courts must adapt it to local constitutional values.”

## ⚖️ Ex Parte Chairperson of the Constitutional Assembly: Certification judgments

- ---
- **High-yield exam use**  Use when linking Theme 3 to Theme 4: the doctrines in Theme 4 were not accidental; they were constitutional commitments certified by the Court.

- **Legal theme** Background link to 1996 Constitution; constitutional supremacy; separation of powers; legitimacy.
- **Facts** The Constitutional Court had to certify whether the constitutional text complied with the Constitutional Principles agreed during the transition. The Theme 4 lectures refer back to certification as part of understanding how the 1996 Constitution came into force.
- **Issue** Whether the Final Constitution complied with the binding Constitutional Principles.
- **Court reasoning** The Court treated certification as a legal/judicial task rather than a political preference exercise. The text had to comply with agreed principles such as constitutional supremacy, justiciable rights, democracy and separation of powers.
- **Judgment / outcome** The first text was not fully certified; after amendment, the constitutional text was certified and the Constitution came into force on 4 February 1997.
- **Constitutional significance** Useful background case for why the 1996 Constitution has legal legitimacy and why its doctrines are binding.
- **Legislation / sections linked** Interim Constitution Schedule 4 Constitutional Principles; Constitution 1996 generally.
- **How to write it in the exam** “The Certification judgments show that the 1996 Constitution’s core principles were legally tested against agreed Constitutional Principles before the Constitution came into force.”

# 4. Legislation, constitutional sections and legal instruments bank

## 📜 Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Central legal source for Theme 4. Prescribed sections: 1, 2, 8, 9, 10, 36, 39, 172. Lecture examples also use ss 7, 12, 16, 25, 26, 27, 32, 35, 74, 83, 84, 181 and 182.
- **What it says / does** Supreme law of the Republic; creates values, rights, institutions, limitations, interpretation duties and remedies for invalid law/conduct.
- **Why it matters** Theme 4 is essentially about how the 1996 Constitution structures, constrains and transforms public and private power.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Daniels, PE Municipality, Fourie, Carmichele, Grootboom, Soobramoney, EFF, Fedsure, Affordable Medicines, Albutt, Dodo.
- **Exam sentence** “The Constitution is both a founding-value document and a binding supreme law: s 1 states the values, while s 2 gives them enforceable supremacy.”

## 📜 Section 1 - Founding values

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Value-based constitutionalism and rule of law sections.
- **What it says / does** South Africa is founded on dignity, equality, human rights, non-racialism, non-sexism, supremacy of the Constitution, rule of law, universal adult suffrage, regular elections, multi-party democracy, accountability, responsiveness and openness.
- **Why it matters** These values permeate interpretation, limitation, democracy, rule of law and separation of powers.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Soobramoney, Fourie, EFF, Fedsure.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 1 makes constitutional values foundational, meaning courts must interpret law consistently with dignity, equality, freedom, accountability and the rule of law.”

## 📜 Section 2 - Supremacy of the Constitution

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Constitutional supremacy.
- **What it says / does** The Constitution is the supreme law; law or conduct inconsistent with it is invalid; obligations imposed by it must be fulfilled.
- **Why it matters** Makes judicial review possible and separates South Africa from parliamentary sovereignty.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, EFF, Fedsure, Affordable Medicines, Albutt.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 2 converts constitutional supremacy into a binding rule: inconsistent law or conduct is invalid.”

## 📜 Section 7 - Rights

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Bill of Rights framework.
- **What it says / does** Bill of Rights is a cornerstone of democracy; the state must respect, protect, promote and fulfil rights; rights are subject to limitations.
- **Why it matters** Links transformative constitutionalism and the state’s positive obligations.
- **Cases linked** Grootboom, Carmichele, Soobramoney.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 7(2) means the state must not only avoid violating rights but must actively protect, promote and fulfil them.”

## 📜 Section 8 - Application of the Bill of Rights

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Direct/indirect and vertical/horizontal application.
- **What it says / does** The Bill of Rights applies to all law and binds the legislature, executive, judiciary and organs of state. It may bind natural and juristic persons depending on the nature of the right and duty.
- **Why it matters** Core section for explaining vertical and horizontal application.
- **Cases linked** Carmichele; equality examples; private discrimination examples.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 8 is the gateway provision for deciding whether the Bill of Rights applies vertically, horizontally, directly or indirectly.”

## 📜 Section 9 - Equality

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Value-based constitutionalism; horizontal application examples.
- **What it says / does** Everyone is equal before the law; unfair discrimination by the state and by private persons is prohibited; national legislation must prevent/prohibit unfair discrimination.
- **Why it matters** Shows substantive equality and direct horizontal application.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Fourie, Daniels; linked to PEPUDA.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 9 is especially important because it expressly applies to private persons through s 9(4), supporting horizontal application.”

## 📜 Section 10 - Human dignity

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Foundational value/right; Makwanyane; Fourie; PE Municipality.
- **What it says / does** Everyone has inherent dignity and the right to have dignity respected and protected.
- **Why it matters** Often treated as a core value in South African constitutionalism.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Fourie, PE Municipality, Soobramoney.
- **Exam sentence** “Dignity is both a right and an organising value that shapes interpretation, limitation and remedies.”

## 📜 Section 36 - Limitation of rights

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Limitations clause.
- **What it says / does** Rights may be limited only by law of general application to the extent reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society based on dignity, equality and freedom. Factors include nature of right, purpose, extent, relationship between limitation and purpose, and less restrictive means.
- **Why it matters** Core exam structure for limitation questions.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Walters, Fourie, Dodo.
- **Exam sentence** “A s 36 answer must first identify the infringement, then weigh whether the limitation is reasonable and justifiable.”

## 📜 Section 39 - Interpretation of the Bill of Rights / legislation / common law / customary law

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Value-based interpretation; indirect application.
- **What it says / does** When interpreting the Bill of Rights, courts must promote dignity, equality and freedom; must consider international law; may consider foreign law. When interpreting legislation or developing common/customary law, courts must promote the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.
- **Why it matters** Central to value-based constitutionalism and indirect application.
- **Cases linked** Daniels, Carmichele, H v Fetal Assessment Centre, S v Walters.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 39(2) is the bridge between constitutional values and ordinary law.”

## 📜 Section 172 - Constitutional invalidity and remedies

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Constitutional supremacy and judicial review.
- **What it says / does** When deciding a constitutional matter, a court must declare inconsistent law/conduct invalid to the extent of inconsistency and may make a just and equitable order.
- **Why it matters** This is the remedial consequence of constitutional supremacy.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, EFF, Affordable Medicines.
- **Exam sentence** “Section 172 gives courts the remedial power that makes constitutional supremacy practical.”

## 📜 Interim Constitution of 1993

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Makwanyane and s 33 limitation comparison.
- **What it says / does** The transitional constitution containing an interim Bill of Rights and limitation clause.
- **Why it matters** Several early Constitutional Court cases, including Makwanyane, were decided under the Interim Constitution, but the principles remain foundational.
- **Cases linked** Makwanyane, Certification judgments.
- **Exam sentence** “The Interim Constitution introduced justiciable rights and began the shift from parliamentary sovereignty to constitutional supremacy.”

## 📜 Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977 - s 277(1)(a)

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** S v Makwanyane.
- **What it says / does** Authorised the death penalty for certain crimes before constitutional invalidation.
- **Why it matters** Shows how old-order legislation was tested against the new constitutional order.
- **Cases linked** S v Makwanyane.
- **Exam sentence** “Makwanyane invalidated the death penalty provision in the Criminal Procedure Act because it was inconsistent with constitutional rights and values.”

- **Why it matters** Shows values-based statutory interpretation and inclusion.
- **Cases linked** Daniels v Campbell.
- **Exam sentence** “Daniels shows that ordinary statutory words must be interpreted through constitutional values where possible.”

## 📜 Disaster Management Act 57 of 2002 - s 27

- **Where it appears in Theme 4** Rule of law lecture example on lockdown regulations.

# 6. What to memorise before a Theme 4 test

- ---
- **Memory list**  Constitutionalism = government authority is derived from and limited by a constitution; it must structure and constrain state power.  Constitutional supremacy = Constitution is supreme; inconsistent law/conduct is invalid; courts can review law/conduct.  Rule of law / legality = public power must be authorised by law and exercised rationally.  Values = dignity, equality, freedom, non-racialism, non-sexism, accountability, responsiveness, openness and rule of law permeate interpretation and limitation.  s 36 method = right limited? If yes, is limitation reasonable and justifiable? Apply nature of right, purpose, extent, relation and less restrictive means.  s 39 method = interpret rights and ordinary law to promote dignity, equality and freedom and the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights.  Application = direct/indirect + vertical/horizontal. Always identify both in a rights problem.  Use Makwanyane for values and limitation; Daniels for s 39 interpretation; Carmichele for common-law development; EFF/Fedsure/Affordable Medicines/Albutt for rule of law; PE Municipality/Fourie for balancing and transformation.

# 7. Mini model answer templates

- ---
- **5-mark: Explain constitutional supremacy**  Constitutional supremacy means the Constitution is the highest law. Section 2 provides that law or conduct inconsistent with it is invalid and that constitutional obligations must be fulfilled. This makes the Constitution justiciable: courts may test laws and conduct against it. It also rejects parliamentary sovereignty because Parliament is bound by the Constitution. Cases such as S v Makwanyane and EFF v Speaker show that courts may invalidate old legislation or unlawful conduct when inconsistent with constitutional rights, values and obligations.

- ---
- **5-mark: Explain rule of law and legality**  The rule of law is a founding value in s 1(c). At minimum, it requires that public power must be authorised by law and exercised rationally. Fedsure states that the legislature and executive may exercise no power beyond that conferred by law. Affordable Medicines confirms that legality is part of the constitutional control of public power. Albutt shows that rationality may require a fair process where the process must be connected to the purpose. EFF v Speaker shows that no constitutional decision may simply be ignored without court challenge.

- ---
- **10/15-mark: Application of the Bill of Rights structure**  1. Identify the right and section involved.  2. State whether the Bill of Rights applies directly or indirectly.  3. State whether it applies vertically or horizontally.  4. If direct: explain that the right itself is relied upon and inconsistent law/conduct may be invalid.  5. If indirect: use s 39(2) to interpret legislation or develop common/customary law.  6. If vertical: state actor/state organ is bound by s 8(1).  7. If horizontal: private actor may be bound under s 8(2), considering nature of right and duty.  8. Add example/case: Makwanyane for direct invalidity; Carmichele for indirect development; Grootboom for vertical socio-economic rights; equality examples/PEPUDA for horizontal discrimination.
