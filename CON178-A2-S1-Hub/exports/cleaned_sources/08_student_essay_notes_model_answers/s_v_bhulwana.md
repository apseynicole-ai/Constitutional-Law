S v Bhulwana; S v Gwadiso (1995) was a landmark Constitutional Court of South Africa case that established key principles regarding the presumption of innocence in the post-apartheid legal system.

Core Facts and Legal Issue

* The Law: Section 21(1)(a)(i) of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act, 1992, created a "reverse onus" provision. If a person was found in possession of more than 115 grams of dagga, they were presumed guilty of dealing in dagga, and had to prove their innocence in court.
* The Cases: Mr. Bhulwana and Mr. Gwadiso were convicted of drug dealing based on this presumption, rather than direct evidence of selling.
* Constitutional Issue: The High Court referred the matter to the Constitutional Court to decide if this "reverse onus" violated the right to a fair trial, specifically the presumption of innocence under the Interim Constitution.


Judgment and Reasoning (29 November 1995)

* Unanimous Decision: The court, with the judgment delivered by Justice Kate O'Regan, held that the provision was unconstitutional.
* Violation of Rights: The court found that forcing an accused to prove their innocence (reverse onus) violated the presumption of innocence.
* No Justification: The state failed to prove that the presumption was necessary or that it effectively helped to combat drug trafficking.
* Result: Section 21(1)(a)(i) was declared invalid, and the cases were sent back to the High Court.

Impact
This case affirmed that the state must prove all elements of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt, and strengthened the protection of accused individuals against unfair burdens of proof

The following explanation is provided to assist the media in reporting this case and is not binding on the Constitutional Court or any member of the Court.

Legal Issue: Section 21 (1)(a)(i) of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act 140 of 1992 provides that if an accused has been found in possession of more than 115 grams of dagga, he or she will be presumed to have been dealing in dagga and will be convicted of the offence of dealing (as opposed to the lesser offence of possession) unless that person proves that he or she has not been dealing in dagga.

Application In This Case: Considering a reference to the issue of the validity of the section from the Supreme Court, the Constitutional Court held the section to be unconstitutional with effect from the date of the judgment. The Court considered the section to be an infringement of the right of an accused person to be presumed innocent in s 25(3)(c) of the Constitution. The Court found that the section was not justifiable in terms of s 33(1) of the Constitution, holding that the State had failed to prove that the presumption substantially furthered the aim of combating the trafficking of illegal drugs. The Court ordered that the declaration of invalidity should invalidate the use of the presumption in any criminal trial in which the verdict was given after the Constitution came into operation and in which, at the time of the judgment, either an appeal or review is pending or the time for noting an appeal has not yet expired. The judgment of the Court was delivered by O'Regan J and was concurred in by all the other members of the Court.

# **Case Summary Example**

### **Case**

*S v Bhulwana* 1996 (1) SA 388 (CC)

## **1. Facts**

* The accused, Bhulwana, was charged with **dealing in cannabis (dagga)**.
* The **Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act** contained a provision stating that if a person was found **in possession of more than a certain quantity of cannabis**, the court would **presume that the person intended to deal in it**.
* This meant the accused had to **prove that they were not dealing**, rather than the state proving that they were.
* Bhulwana challenged this provision, arguing that it violated his constitutional rights.

## **2. Legal Issue**

Whether the **statutory presumption that possession of dagga implies intent to deal** violates the **constitutional right to be presumed innocent** before guilty.

## **3. Court’s Reasoning**

* The Constitutional Court considered **section 25(3)(c) of the Interim Constitution**, which protected the **right to be presumed innocent**.
* Normally, the **state must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt**.
* The presumption in the Act **shifted the burden of proof to the accused**, forcing them to prove their innocence.
* The court held that this **undermined the presumption of innocence** because an accused person could be convicted **without the state proving intent to deal**.

## **4. Judgment**

The Constitutional Court declared the statutory presumption **unconstitutional and invalid** because it violated the **right to be presumed innocent**.

## **5. Significance**

* The case strengthened the **constitutional protection of the presumption of innocence**.
* It confirmed that **laws which shift the burden of proof to the accused may be unconstitutional**.
* The case became an important precedent in **South African constitutional criminal procedure**.

Case Summary

Case Details:

* **Citation:** S v Bhulwana; S v Gwadiso 1996 (1) SA 288 (CC)
* **Court:** Constitutional Court of South Africa
* **Heard on:** 12 September 1995
* **Judgment Date:** 29 November 1995

Case Facts:

* The accused, Bhulwana, was found in possession of 850g of dagga (cannabis) near Kleinmond on 20 May 1994
* Bhulwana was subsequently charged with dealing in cannabis (dagga) and fined R500,00 with the alternative of six months’ imprisonment, in addition to a twelve month prison sentence was suspended for five years on condition that he was mot found guilty do dealing in drugs during that period.
* The case came into matter before the Cape Provincial Division of the supreme court on automatic review. Marais J held that there was insufficient evidence to convict Mr Bhulwana of dealing in drugs as there was an absence of the presumption contained in section 21(l)(a)(i) of the Act
* The basis of the attack on section 21(l)(a)(i) of the Act is that the section imposes a burden of proof on the accused, a so-called 'reverse onus' provision, which is contrary to the provisions of section 25(3) of the Constitution. Section 25(3) provides that: 'Every accused person shall have the right to a fair trial, which shall include the right - t, (c) to be presumed innocent and to remain silent during plea proceedings or trial and not to testify during a trial;'

Legal Issue:

The legal issue in *S v Bhulwana* 1996 (1) SA 388 (CC) stated by O’Reagan J. was wheather the provisions of **Section 21(1)(a)(i) of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act 140 of 1992 conflicted with the** Republic of South Africa Constitution Act, 200 of 1993. **Section 21(1)(a)(i) of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act 140 of 1992 created a statutory presumption that anyone found in possession of more than 115 grams of cannabis (dagga) was presumed to be dealing in it, unless they could prove otherwise. The basis of the conflict with Section 21(1)(a)(i) of the  Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act 140 of 1992 (shall be referred to as ‘the Act’) is that the section imposes a burden of proof on the accused, which is in conflict with the provisions of section 25(3) of the Constitution. Section 25(3) provides that: 'Every accused person shall have the right to a fair trial, which shall include the right - , (c) to be presumed innopcent and to remain silent during plea proceedings or trial and not to testify during a trial;'**

**From (**[**https://collections.concourt.org.za/bitstream/handle/20.500.12144/2009/Full%20judgment%20Official%20version%2029%20November%201995.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y**](https://collections.concourt.org.za/bitstream/handle/20.500.12144/2009/Full%20judgment%20Official%20version%2029%20November%201995.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)**) Is the imposition of this burden a breach of the presumption of innocence as enshrined in section 25(3)(c)? As this court held in S v Zuma 1995(2) SA 642 (CC); 1995(4) BCLR 401 (CC) at para 33, the presumption of innocence is not new to our legal system. As early as 1883, in R v Benjamin 3 EDC 337 at 338, Buchanan J noted that: 'But in a criminal trial there is a presumption of innocence in favour of the accused, which must be rebutted. Therefore there should not be a conviction unless the crime charged has been clearly proved to have been committed by the accused. Where the evidence is not reasonably inconsistent with the prisoner's innocence, or where a reasonable doubt as to his guilt exists, there should be an acquittal.**

**[15] As was held in Zumcs case, supra, at para 33, the presumption of innocence is an established principle of South African law which places the burden of proof squarely on the prosecution. The entrenchment of the presumption of innocence in section 25(3)(c) must be interpreted in this context. It requires that the prosecution bear the burden of proving all the elements of a criminal charge. A presumption which relieves the prosecution of part of that burden could result in the conviction of an accused person despite the existence of a reasonable doubt as to his or her guilt. Such a presumption is in breach of the presumption of innocence and therefore offends section 25(3)(c). Section 21(l)(a)(i) is such a presumption. The answer to the threshold enquiry is therefore that section 21 (l)(a)(i) clearly gives rise to a breach of section 25(3)(c) of the Constitution.**
