Harris and Others v Minister of the Interior and Another
▸
1952 (2) SA 428 (A) Appellate Division, 1952
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Parliamentary supremacy, entrenched clauses, coloured voters, 1950s constitutional crisis
I. Facts

Parliament passed the Separate Representation of Voters Act 46 of 1951 to remove coloured voters from the common roll through an ordinary majority procedure, bypassing the special two-thirds joint-sitting majority required to amend entrenched provisions of the South Africa Act 1909.

II. Legal Issue

Could Parliament validly remove coloured voters from the common roll without complying with the entrenched joint-sitting procedure?

III. Decision

The Separate Representation of Voters Act was declared invalid because it had not been passed in the constitutionally required joint-sitting way.

IV. Reasoning

Parliament was powerful but not free to ignore the constitutional procedure that created its legislative power. Where the South Africa Act prescribed a particular 'manner and form' for amending entrenched provisions, that procedure had to be followed. The court struck it down on purely procedural, not substantive, grounds.

Ratio Decidendi

Even under parliamentary supremacy, Parliament must comply with manner and form requirements for amending entrenched constitutional provisions. This creates a narrow procedural check, not substantive constitutional review.

Why It Matters

Shows the limits of pre-1994 judicial review. Courts could check procedure but not test legislation against rights or values. Key distinction: procedural review vs substantive constitutional review.

Exam Use

Use for: 1950s constitutional crisis; manner and form entrenchment; limits of judicial review under Union Constitution; difference between procedural and substantive review; why pre-1994 SA lacked genuine constitutional supremacy.

Model Paragraph

In Harris I, the Appellate Division held that Parliament could not remove coloured voters from the common roll without complying with the special entrenched procedure. The case confirmed that even under parliamentary supremacy, courts could enforce manner and form requirements.

Memory Sentence

Harris I = Parliament must follow the constitutional rules.

Minister of the Interior and Another v Harris and Others
▸
1952 (4) SA 769 (A) Appellate Division, 1952
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
High Court of Parliament Act, judicial review under Union Constitution, separation of powers
I. Facts

After losing in Harris I, the government passed the High Court of Parliament Act 35 of 1952, creating a body composed of MPs with power to review and overturn Appellate Division decisions, effectively making Parliament the final court in constitutional matters.

II. Legal Issue

Could Parliament validly create a High Court of Parliament with power to review and overturn judgments of ordinary courts?

III. Decision

The High Court of Parliament Act was declared invalid.

IV. Reasoning

The Appellate Division held that Parliament could not confer judicial power on itself in a way that contradicted the constitutional structure. Judicial power was vested in courts, not Parliament sitting as a political body. Even under parliamentary sovereignty, a separation between legislative and judicial functions existed.

Ratio Decidendi

Parliament cannot use ordinary legislation to make itself judge in its own cause. The formal constitutional structure and judicial role must be protected even under parliamentary supremacy.

Why It Matters

A strong assertion of judicial independence within the Union Constitution's limited framework. But still not full constitutional supremacy — the court was protecting formal structure, not testing legislation against a Bill of Rights.

Exam Use

Use for: 1950s constitutional crisis; separation of powers under Union Constitution; limits of parliamentary supremacy; why courts could protect judicial function but not substantive rights; government attempts to overcome Harris I.

Model Paragraph

In Harris II, Parliament attempted to bypass the Appellate Division's decision in Harris I by creating a High Court of Parliament with power to review court judgments. The Appellate Division held the Act invalid because Parliament could not assume judicial power and overturn court decisions through ordinary legislation. The case confirmed a limited structural separation between Parliament and the courts, even under parliamentary supremacy.

Memory Sentence

Harris II = Parliament cannot turn itself into a court.

Collins v Minister of the Interior and Another
▸
1957 (1) SA 552 (A) Appellate Division, 1957
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Senate-packing crisis, parliamentary supremacy, collapse of meaningful judicial resistance
I. Facts

After Harris I and II, the National Party lacked the required two-thirds majority. It expanded the Senate to obtain the majority at a joint sitting, then passed the constitutional amendment removing coloured voters from the common roll. The case challenged the validity of the Senate-packing legislation.

II. Legal Issue

Could courts invalidate the Senate-packing legislation because Parliament had manipulated its own composition to secure the special majority?

III. Decision

The Appellate Division upheld the validity of the Senate Act and the constitutional amendments.

IV. Reasoning

Parliament had complied with the formal manner and form requirements. Once those formal requirements were satisfied, the court would not inquire into Parliament's motives or political strategy behind the legislation.

Ratio Decidendi

Once Parliament formally complies with the required procedure, courts will not investigate political motives or substantive injustice of the law. This confirms the dominance of parliamentary supremacy.

Why It Matters

Shows the weakness of pre-1994 constitutionalism. Harris I and II looked like judicial resistance, but Collins confirmed courts could only test formal procedure — making constitutional supremacy and a justiciable Bill of Rights necessary.

Exam Use

Use for: final victory of parliamentary supremacy in the 1950s; why manner and form review was insufficient; why SA needed constitutional supremacy; failure of courts to protect oppressed groups under apartheid.

Model Paragraph

In Collins, the Appellate Division upheld the Senate-packing legislation because Parliament had formally complied with the required amendment procedure. The court refused to examine Parliament's political motives or the substantive injustice of the law. The case confirmed the dominance of parliamentary supremacy and showed that pre-1994 courts could only perform limited procedural review, not substantive rights-based review.

Memory Sentence

Collins = If Parliament follows the rules, courts cannot question the substance.

S v Makwanyane and Another
▸
[1995] ZACC 3; 1995 (3) SA 391 (CC); 1995 (6) BCLR 665 (CC); 1995 (2) SACR 1 (CC) Constitutional Court, 1995
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178 Foundations
Death penalty, dignity, life, ubuntu, constitutional values, transformative constitutionalism
I. Facts

Two accused were convicted of murder and sentenced to death. The case came before the newly established Constitutional Court under the Interim Constitution to decide if capital punishment was compatible with a human-rights-based order.

II. Legal Issue

Was the death penalty constitutional under the Interim Constitution?

III. Decision

The death penalty was declared unconstitutional.

IV. Reasoning

The Court held the death penalty was inconsistent with the rights to life and human dignity, and the prohibition on cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment. The Court used value-based, purposive, transformative interpretation. Ubuntu emphasised shared humanity and compassion — pushing against retribution as pure revenge. On limitation: even accepting deterrence and public safety goals, the punishment was final, irreversible and destructive of human dignity.

Ratio Decidendi

The death penalty is unconstitutional because it violates the rights to life, dignity and freedom from cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment. Constitutional interpretation must be value-based, purposive and transformative. Public opinion does not determine constitutional rights.

Why It Matters

One of the most important cases in South African constitutional law. Established that the Constitution requires a new legal culture based on dignity, life and accountability. Foundational for transformative constitutionalism and ubuntu.

Exam Use

Use for: dignity; right to life; ubuntu; limitation analysis; transformative constitutionalism; break from apartheid legal culture; value-based interpretation; constitutional supremacy in practice.

Model Paragraph

In S v Makwanyane, the Constitutional Court held that the death penalty violated the rights to life and dignity and amounted to cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment. The Court used a value-based interpretation of the Interim Constitution, emphasising ubuntu, human dignity and the need to break from South Africa's violent past. The case is foundational authority for transformative constitutional interpretation and the centrality of constitutional values.

Memory Sentence

Makwanyane = no death penalty because dignity and life are foundational.

S v Bhulwana; S v Gwadiso
▸
[1995] ZACC 11; 1996 (1) SA 388 (CC); 1995 (12) BCLR 1579 (CC); 1995 (2) SACR 748 (CC) Constitutional Court, 1995/1996
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178 Legal Skills
Limitation clause, reverse onus, presumption of innocence, proportionality, fair trial
I. Facts

Reverse onus provisions under drug legislation presumed that an accused who possessed more than a certain quantity of cannabis was dealing, unless the accused proved otherwise — shifting the burden of proof from state to accused.

II. Legal Issue

Was the reverse onus provision consistent with the constitutional right to a fair trial, especially the presumption of innocence?

III. Decision

The reverse onus provision was declared unconstitutional.

IV. Reasoning

The provision infringed the presumption of innocence. A reverse onus can result in conviction even where a court has a reasonable doubt about guilt. On the limitation clause: combating drug dealing was important, but the means were disproportionate — the state could use evidentiary presumptions without placing a legal burden on the accused.

Ratio Decidendi

A reverse onus provision that creates a real risk of convicting an accused despite reasonable doubt violates the presumption of innocence and will not easily be justified under the limitation clause. Proportionality is key.

Why It Matters

Developed South Africa's limitation analysis. Rights are not absolute, but limitations must be reasonable and justifiable. Criminal procedure must be transformed by constitutional values.

Exam Use

Use for: presumption of innocence; fair trial rights; reverse onus; proportionality; limitation clause; constitutional invalidity of legislation; court hierarchy; state power in criminal justice.

Model Paragraph

In S v Bhulwana; S v Gwadiso, the Constitutional Court held that a statutory presumption requiring an accused to prove that cannabis possession was not for dealing violated the presumption of innocence. The prosecution must prove every element of a criminal offence beyond reasonable doubt, and legislation inconsistent with constitutional fair-trial rights may be declared invalid.

Memory Sentence

Bhulwana/Gwadiso = reverse onus + risk of wrongful conviction = unconstitutional.

Certification of the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996
▸
[1996] ZACC 26; 1996 (4) SA 744 (CC); 1996 (10) BCLR 1253 (CC) Constitutional Court, 1996
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Constitutional Principles, certification, constitutional supremacy, first draft not certified
I. Facts

South Africa used a two-stage transition: unelected negotiating parties drafted the Interim Constitution; then a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly drafted the Final Constitution. The Interim Constitution required the Final Constitution to comply with the 34 Constitutional Principles and the Court to certify compliance. Political parties and private bodies objected to various provisions.

II. Legal Issue

Did the draft Final Constitution comply with all 34 Constitutional Principles?

III. Decision

The Court refused to certify the draft Final Constitution.

IV. Reasoning

The Court's certification function was judicial, not political — it measured the text against the binding Constitutional Principles, not political preferences. The Court approached the Principles holistically: first whether the basic structure accorded with the CPs; then whether actual provisions complied. The basic structure was accepted but several problems were found, including provisions attempting to shield ordinary statutes from constitutional scrutiny, problems with local government powers, and reduction of provincial powers.

Ratio Decidendi

The Constitutional Court had to certify whether the draft Final Constitution complied with the 34 Constitutional Principles. The first draft was not certified. Certification is a judicial, not political, function.

Why It Matters

Proves that the new constitutional order was based on legal supremacy, not political convenience. Even a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly was bound by legal limits. Constitutionalism was not merely symbolic.

Exam Use

Use for: constitutional supremacy; the negotiated constitutional revolution; role of the Constitutional Court; 34 Constitutional Principles; legitimacy of the Final Constitution; rule of law in the transition; two-stage transition.

Model Paragraph

In the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Court refused to certify the draft Final Constitution because it did not comply fully with the 34 Constitutional Principles in the Interim Constitution. The case demonstrates the legal nature of South Africa's negotiated transition: even a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly was bound by constitutional limits. It is key authority for constitutional supremacy and the rule of law during constitution-making.

Memory Sentence

First Certification = first draft failed.

Final Constitution certified after amendments, completion of constitutional transition
I. Facts

After the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Assembly revised the draft, targeting the defects identified by the Court. The amended text was submitted for certification.

II. Legal Issue

Did the amended constitutional text comply with the 34 Constitutional Principles?

III. Decision

The Court unanimously certified the Amended Text. Mandela signed the Constitution on 10 December 1996 at Sharpeville. It took effect on 4 February 1997.

IV. Reasoning

The Court focused on whether the concerns from the First Certification Judgment had been properly addressed. The amendments targeted the identified defects. The Court was satisfied each shortcoming had been remedied.

Ratio Decidendi

After the Constitutional Assembly corrected the defects from the First Certification Judgment, the Constitutional Court certified the amended text. The Final Constitution is legitimate because it was produced by an elected Constitutional Assembly and certified by an independent court against pre-agreed principles.

Why It Matters

Completed the legal transition from the Interim Constitution to the Final Constitution. Strengthened the legitimacy of the Constitutional Court — political actors accepted the Court's authority and revised the text.

Exam Use

Economic Freedom Fighters v Speaker of the National Assembly and Others
▸
[2016] ZACC 11; 2016 (5) BCLR 618 (CC); 2016 (3) SA 580 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2016
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Public Protector, accountability, rule of law, separation of powers, Nkandla
I. Facts

The Public Protector investigated security upgrades at President Zuma's Nkandla homestead, finding non-security features had been installed at public expense and directing the President to pay a reasonable portion of the costs. The National Assembly resolved to absolve the President. The President did not comply with the Public Protector's remedial action.

II. Legal Issue

Was the Public Protector's remedial action binding? Did the President and National Assembly fail to fulfil constitutional obligations?

III. Decision

The Public Protector's remedial action was binding. The President breached section 83(b). The National Assembly's resolution absolving him was invalid. National Treasury had to determine the amount payable.

IV. Reasoning

Mogoeng CJ grounded the judgment in accountability, rule of law and constitutional supremacy. The Public Protector is a Chapter 9 institution whose remedial action cannot be ignored through self-help. If an affected party believes the Public Protector acted wrongly, the proper remedy is to approach a court — not to ignore the remedial action. The National Assembly cannot replace binding remedial action with a politically convenient process.

Ratio Decidendi

The Public Protector's remedial action is binding unless set aside by a court. The President and National Assembly cannot ignore or circumvent it.

Why It Matters

One of the strongest modern cases on constitutional accountability. Confirms constitutional obligations are legally enforceable even against the President. Gives real force to Chapter 9 institutions.

Exam Use

Use for: rule of law; accountability; Public Protector; Chapter 9 institutions; presidential duties under s83(b); parliamentary oversight; separation of powers; constitutional supremacy.

Model Paragraph

In EFF v Speaker, the Constitutional Court held that the Public Protector's remedial action against President Zuma was binding and could not be ignored. The President's failure to comply breached his duty under section 83(b) to uphold, defend and respect the Constitution, while the National Assembly failed in its oversight duties. The case is key authority for accountability, rule of law, Chapter 9 institutions and constitutional supremacy.

Memory Sentence

EFF = Public Protector's remedial action binds unless set aside by a court.

City of Tshwane Metropolitan Municipality v Afriforum and Another
▸
[2016] ZACC 19; 2016 (9) BCLR 1133 (CC); 2016 (6) SA 279 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2016
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Preamble, belonging, transformation, ubuntu, public symbols, street names, interim interdicts
I. Facts

The City of Tshwane decided to change street names associated with South Africa's colonial and apartheid past. Afriforum sought an interim interdict to stop the City from removing the old names, arguing the changes infringed Afrikaner cultural rights and sense of belonging.

II. Legal Issue

Should the interim interdict remain in place? How should courts understand belonging, transformation and public symbols under the Constitution?

III. Decision

The Constitutional Court upheld the appeal, set aside the High Court orders, and dismissed the interim interdict.

IV. Reasoning

The Court placed the case in historical context. Apartheid had given public recognition mainly to white history and symbols. The Preamble commits South Africa to recognise injustices of the past, heal divisions and affirm that South Africa belongs to all who live in it. Ubuntu means belonging is relational — a claim to belonging that disregards historical exclusion is inconsistent with ubuntu. Afriforum failed to show irreparable harm; the balance of convenience did not favour stopping the City's lawful policy decision.

Ratio Decidendi

Public symbols are not neutral. The Preamble demands transformation, inclusion and recognition of historical injustice. Belonging must be shared and inclusive, not monopolised by one cultural or racial group.

Why It Matters

Links many themes: Preamble; historical injustice; ubuntu; transformation; belonging; public symbols; judicial restraint; separation of powers; interim interdicts. Shows transformation is also symbolic.

Exam Use

Use for: Preamble; constitutional belonging; transformation; ubuntu; public symbols; visual redress; separation of powers; interim interdict requirements.

Model Paragraph

In City of Tshwane v Afriforum, the Constitutional Court held that Afriforum had not met the requirements for an interim interdict preventing the City from changing street names. The Court used the Preamble, ubuntu and South Africa's history of exclusion to explain that public symbols are not neutral. Belonging under the Constitution is inclusive and transformative, not a right to preserve one group's monopoly over public honour and memory.

Carmichele v Minister of Safety and Security and Another
▸
[2001] ZACC 22; 2001 (4) SA 938 (CC); 2001 (10) BCLR 995 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2001
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Duties of state, development of common law under s39(2), positive obligations, gender-based violence
I. Facts

Alix Carmichele was attacked by Francois Coetzee, who had previously been charged with serious sexual violence-related offences. She sued the Ministers of Safety and Security and Justice, arguing police and prosecutors had failed to protect her despite knowing of Coetzee's history of violence.

II. Legal Issue

Could the common law of delict be developed to recognise a legal duty on police and prosecutors to protect Carmichele from harm?

III. Decision

The matter was remitted for reconsideration in light of the constitutional duty to develop the common law.

IV. Reasoning

Section 39(2) requires courts to develop the common law in light of the Constitution. Common-law rules on wrongfulness, negligence and state liability must be shaped by constitutional values. The state has a duty to protect rights including dignity, freedom and security of the person. Police and prosecutors exercise public power and are constitutionally required to protect members of the public.

Ratio Decidendi

Courts have a constitutional duty under section 39(2) to develop the common law in line with the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. The state may have positive obligations to protect people from violence where officials negligently fail to act.

Why It Matters

Foundational case on transformative constitutionalism in private law and delict. Shows the Constitution affects not only legislation and public power, but also common-law duties. Rights can require positive state action.

Exam Use

Use for: section 39(2); development of common law; state duties; positive obligations; gender-based violence; transformative constitutionalism; indirect horizontal application.

Model Paragraph

In Carmichele, the Constitutional Court held that courts have a duty under section 39(2) to develop the common law in line with the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. The case recognised that police and prosecutors may owe legal duties to protect individuals from violent harm where constitutional rights are at stake. It is key authority for positive state obligations and constitutional development of the common law.

Daniels v Scribante and Another
▸
[2017] ZACC 13; 2017 (4) SA 341 (CC); 2017 (8) BCLR 949 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2017
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Transformative constitutionalism, dignity, property/land, horizontal application, positive obligations
I. Facts

Ms Daniels lived on a farm under insecure tenure. Her dwelling was not fit for dignified human habitation. Ms Daniels wanted to make basic improvements: levelling floors, installing water supply, a wash basin, a second window and a ceiling. The landowner refused.

II. Legal Issue

Could Ms Daniels make basic improvements to her dwelling without the landowner's consent? Did the Constitution impose positive obligations on the private landowner?

III. Decision

The Constitutional Court held Ms Daniels was entitled to make the basic improvements to her dwelling.

IV. Reasoning

The Court interpreted ESTA through the Constitution, especially dignity and section 25(6) protecting people with insecure tenure. Tenure security must be meaningful. Property rights must be viewed in historical context — South Africa's property law was deeply connected to dispossession and exclusion. Under section 8(2), the landowner owed Ms Daniels a positive obligation to ensure she lived in conditions compatible with human dignity.

Ratio Decidendi

The Constitution may impose positive obligations even on private landowners. An occupier with insecure tenure may make basic improvements necessary for dignified habitation even without the owner's consent, subject to meaningful engagement.

Why It Matters

Shows constitutional rights are not only defensive rights against the state. They can require positive steps to dismantle inherited inequality, even in private relationships. Links dignity, land reform, property, horizontal application and socio-economic justice.

Exam Use

Use for: transformative constitutionalism; property and land; dignity; horizontal application; positive obligations; section 25(6); post-apartheid redistribution; section 8(2).

Model Paragraph

In Daniels v Scribante, the Constitutional Court held that an ESTA occupier could make basic improvements to her dwelling to render it fit for dignified habitation, even without the landowner's consent. The case shows that tenure security must be interpreted through dignity and transformative constitutionalism. It is key authority for horizontal application and positive obligations in private property relationships.

Daniels = dignity can require positive obligations on private landowners.

MEC for Education: KwaZulu-Natal, and Others v Pillay
▸
2008 (1) SA 474 (CC); [2007] ZACC 21 Constitutional Court, 2007/2008
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed Foundations Legal Skills
Equality, reasonable accommodation, cultural/religious expression, school uniform code
I. Facts

A learner at a KwaZulu-Natal public school wanted to wear a small gold nose stud as part of her South Indian Tamil/Hindu cultural and religious identity. The school code prohibited jewellery. The school refused an exemption.

II. Legal Issue

Did the school's neutral rule against jewellery amount to unfair discrimination by failing to accommodate the learner's cultural and religious practice?

III. Decision

The Constitutional Court held the school's refusal amounted to unfair discrimination. The practice was protected even though voluntary rather than mandatory.

IV. Reasoning

A neutral rule can still discriminate if it marginalises a particular group. Whether a religious or cultural practice is voluntary or compulsory is not decisive for whether it qualifies for protection. Reasonable accommodation requires institutions to sometimes adjust general rules to avoid marginalising people whose religious or cultural practices differ from the majority.

Ratio Decidendi

A facially neutral school rule may amount to unfair discrimination if it fails to reasonably accommodate a learner's religious or cultural practice. A practice need not be mandatory to receive constitutional protection.

Why It Matters

Shows equality is substantive, not merely formal. Treating everyone the same can still be unfair if a neutral rule burdens some groups more than others. Reasonable accommodation is central to substantive equality.

Exam Use

Use for: equality; unfair discrimination; culture and religion; reasonable accommodation; school codes of conduct; formal vs substantive equality. Key in both Foundations and Legal Skills.

Model Paragraph

In MEC for Education, KwaZulu-Natal v Pillay, the Constitutional Court held that a school's refusal to allow a learner to wear a nose stud as an expression of her South Indian, Tamil and Hindu culture amounted to unfair discrimination. The case confirms that religious or cultural practices need not be compulsory to receive protection and that reasonable accommodation is central to substantive equality.

Groenewald NO and Another v Swanepoel
▸
2002 (6) SA 724 (E) Eastern Cape Division, 2002
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed Foundations Legal Skills
Civil claim after criminal conduct, delict, judge's duty, pleadings and evidence
I. Facts

A civil claim for damages arose from alleged criminal conduct (robbery and murder). The defense argued the civil claim was premature because the defendant had not yet been convicted in criminal proceedings.

II. Legal Issue

Does a civil claim for damages depend on the defendant first being convicted in a criminal court?

III. Decision

Civil liability is not dependent on prior criminal conviction. Section 342 of the Criminal Procedure Act confirms a conviction or acquittal does not bar a civil action.

IV. Reasoning

Civil and criminal proceedings serve different purposes and operate according to different rules (balance of probabilities vs beyond reasonable doubt). Judges must decide based on pleadings/evidence properly before them, without deciding unraised points. A judge should not decide a case on a point not raised without giving parties an opportunity to address it.

Ratio Decidendi

A civil claim arising from criminal conduct is not premature because criminal proceedings have not been finalised. Civil and criminal liability are independent. Judges must not decide matters on unraised points without inviting argument.

Why It Matters

Very useful for Legal Skills — teaches procedural fairness and disciplined legal reasoning. Illustrates the civil/criminal distinction clearly.

Exam Use

Use for: difference between civil and criminal proceedings; delictual damages after criminal conduct; procedural fairness; judicial duty; pleadings and evidence; why judges must invite argument on decisive points.

Model Paragraph

In Groenewald NO and Another v Swanepoel, the court held that a civil claim for damages arising from criminal conduct does not depend on the defendant first being convicted. Civil and criminal proceedings serve different purposes and operate according to different rules. The case also confirms that judges should not decide matters on unraised points without inviting argument.

Beadica 231 CC v Trustees for the Time Being of the Oregon Trust

[2020] ZACC 13; 2020 (5) SA 247 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2020
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed Foundations
Legal traditions, mixed legal system, contract, ubuntu, public policy, constitutionalised private law
I. Facts

Tenants/franchisees failed to exercise their lease renewal options in time. The landlord sought eviction. The tenants argued strict enforcement was unfair and contrary to ubuntu/constitutional values.

II. Legal Issue

▸
[1996] ZACC 26; 1996 (4) SA 744 (CC); 1996 (10) BCLR 1253 (CC) Constitutional Court, 1996
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Constitutional Principles, certification, constitutional supremacy, first draft not certified
I. Facts

South Africa used a two-stage transition: unelected negotiating parties drafted the Interim Constitution; then a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly drafted the Final Constitution. The Interim Constitution required the Final Constitution to comply with the 34 Constitutional Principles and the Court to certify compliance. Political parties and private bodies objected to various provisions.

II. Legal Issue

Did the draft Final Constitution comply with all 34 Constitutional Principles?

III. Decision

The Court refused to certify the draft Final Constitution.

IV. Reasoning

The Court's certification function was judicial, not political — it measured the text against the binding Constitutional Principles, not political preferences. The Court approached the Principles holistically: first whether the basic structure accorded with the CPs; then whether actual provisions complied. The basic structure was accepted but several problems were found, including provisions attempting to shield ordinary statutes from constitutional scrutiny, problems with local government powers, and reduction of provincial powers.

Ratio Decidendi

The Constitutional Court had to certify whether the draft Final Constitution complied with the 34 Constitutional Principles. The first draft was not certified. Certification is a judicial, not political, function.

Why It Matters

Proves that the new constitutional order was based on legal supremacy, not political convenience. Even a democratically elected Constitutional Assembly was bound by legal limits. Constitutionalism was not merely symbolic.

The municipality extended a security contract unlawfully without public bidding. It continued requesting and receiving security services, then refused to pay, citing the procurement's invalidity due to its own administrative failures.

II. Legal Issue

Can a public authority rely on procurement invalidity to avoid paying for services it requested and received?

III. Decision

The municipality could not simply refuse payment; a just and equitable remedy was required.

IV. Reasoning

Bilchitz AJ noted that transforming private law requires drawing on ubuntu (harmonious relationships, cooperation, mutual respect, and reciprocity). Requesting services and then using one's own illegality to avoid payment is bad faith and contrary to constitutional values.

Ratio Decidendi

Public authorities must act ethically and consistently with ubuntu, even in contractual settings. Legality and contracts require good faith and reciprocity.

Why It Matters

Shows the line between public and private law is blurred; state contracts are constitutional matters requiring ethical conduct and ubuntu.

Carmichele v Minister of Safety and Security and Another
▸
[2001] ZACC 22; 2001 (4) SA 938 (CC); 2001 (10) BCLR 995 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2001
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Duties of state, development of common law under s39(2), positive obligations, gender-based violence
I. Facts

Alix Carmichele was attacked by Francois Coetzee, who had previously been charged with serious sexual violence-related offences. She sued the Ministers of Safety and Security and Justice, arguing police and prosecutors had failed to protect her despite knowing of Coetzee's history of violence.

II. Legal Issue

Could the common law of delict be developed to recognise a legal duty on police and prosecutors to protect Carmichele from harm?

III. Decision

The matter was remitted for reconsideration in light of the constitutional duty to develop the common law.

IV. Reasoning

Section 39(2) requires courts to develop the common law in light of the Constitution. Common-law rules on wrongfulness, negligence and state liability must be shaped by constitutional values. The state has a duty to protect rights including dignity, freedom and security of the person. Police and prosecutors exercise public power and are constitutionally required to protect members of the public.

Ratio Decidendi

Courts have a constitutional duty under section 39(2) to develop the common law in line with the spirit, purport and objects of the Bill of Rights. The state may have positive obligations to protect people from violence where officials negligently fail to act.

Why It Matters

Foundational case on transformative constitutionalism in private law and delict. Shows the Constitution affects not only legislation and public power, but also common-law duties. Rights can require positive state action.

Daniels v Scribante and Another
▸
[2017] ZACC 13; 2017 (4) SA 341 (CC); 2017 (8) BCLR 949 (CC) Constitutional Court, 2017
Core Sheet High ✓ Confirmed CON178
Transformative constitutionalism, dignity, property/land, horizontal application, positive obligations
I. Facts

Ms Daniels lived on a farm under insecure tenure. Her dwelling was not fit for dignified human habitation. Ms Daniels wanted to make basic improvements: levelling floors, installing water supply, a wash basin, a second window and a ceiling. The landowner refused.

II. Legal Issue

Could Ms Daniels make basic improvements to her dwelling without the landowner's consent? Did the Constitution impose positive obligations on the private landowner?

III. Decision

The Constitutional Court held Ms Daniels was entitled to make the basic improvements to her dwelling.

IV. Reasoning

The Court interpreted ESTA through the Constitution, especially dignity and section 25(6) protecting people with insecure tenure. Tenure security must be meaningful. Property rights must be viewed in historical context — South Africa's property law was deeply connected to dispossession and exclusion. Under section 8(2), the landowner owed Ms Daniels a positive obligation to ensure she lived in conditions compatible with human dignity.

Ratio Decidendi

The Constitution may impose positive obligations even on private landowners. An occupier with insecure tenure may make basic improvements necessary for dignified habitation even without the owner's consent, subject to meaningful engagement.

Why It Matters

Shows constitutional rights are not only defensive rights against the state. They can require positive steps to dismantle inherited inequality, even in private relationships. Links dignity, land reform, property, horizontal application and socio-economic justice.

Exam Use

Use for: transformative constitutionalism; property and land; dignity; horizontal application; positive obligations; section 25(6); post-apartheid redistribution; section 8(2).

Model Paragraph

It is key authority for horizontal application and positive obligations in private property relationships.

I. Facts

Challenged the constitutionality of statutory provisions requiring voters to produce barcoded identity documents to register and vote in the 1999 elections.

II. Legal Issue

Did the barcoded ID requirement infringe the constitutional right of citizens to vote?

III. Decision

The Constitutional Court upheld the statutory requirement.

IV. Reasoning

The right to vote is indispensable, but the state must provide a mechanism to regulate and manage voting. The legislature is entitled to determine the conditions, and courts will only intervene if the conditions are irrational or violate rights. The barcoded ID requirement was rational and did not infringe rights since citizens had a reasonable opportunity to obtain them.

The right to vote requires regulatory legislation. Electoral requirements are constitutional if they are rational and do not create an unjustifiable obstacle to the exercise of the franchise.

Democracy, elections, voter registration, barcoded ID, right to vote
I. Facts

A parallel challenge to the barcoded ID requirement for voter registration, arguing it arbitrarily excluded voters who only possessed older identity documents.

II. Legal Issue

Did the statutory requirement of barcoded IDs for voting unjustifiably exclude citizens, violating section 19?

III. Decision

The challenge was dismissed.

IV. Reasoning

Following New National Party, the Court held that the legislative requirement was rational. The state had taken reasonable steps to issue the necessary documents, and the exclusion of voters who failed to obtain them was not the result of the statute, but of their own inaction.

Ratio Decidendi

The state is permitted to enact rational regulations for voter rolls. Rational regulations that cause incidental exclusion due to voter inaction are not unconstitutional.

Why It Matters

Reinforces New National Party, confirming that the right to vote is regulatory and requires voter responsibility.
